# Chapter 217: The Last Byzantines

By the fifteenth age, {{c|3294|Byzantium}}'s shade
Was all that stayed; the {{c|3047|Fourth Crusade}} had sacked
{{p|Constantinople}}, twelve-oh-four, and laid
A Latin throne, for sixty years, intact;

{{p|Nicaea}}, {{p|Trebizond}}, {{p|Epirus}} kept
The Greek flame in their exile; then the line
Of {{c|3295|Palaiologos}}, {{c|3296|Michael VIII}}, stepped
In, sixty-one; restored, but in decline:

The city, and some islands, and a strand
Of western {{p|Anatolia}}; and there
The {{c|3221|Ottoman Turks}} took it, land by land,
And ringed it round. {{c|3297|Andronikos II}}'s care

Was money, peace; he let the army fall;
The {{c|3298|Catalan Company}} he hired
To fight the {{c|3107|Turks}}; they turned on him, and all
Of {{p|Thrace}} they pillaged; {{p|Athens}} acquired;

The army was all mercenaries now,
And faithless; and the civil wars were rife;
{{c|3299|John VI Kantakouzenos}} would allow
The {{c|3221|Ottoman Turks}} in, to win his strife,

In thirteen-forty-seven; and they came
To {{p|Europe}}, to the {{p|Balkans}}, there to stay;
His wish cost dear. And then {{c|3300|Manuel II}} came
To reign, in ninety-one, a remnant's sway:

The city, {{p|Thessalonica}}, the rest:
{{p|Peloponnese}}'s {{p|Morea}}; isles few;
And {{c|3301|Manuel}} then rode west, to seek at best
Some aid from {{p|France}}, {{p|England}}, {{p|Italy}}, too;

With honor and with sympathy received,
But little help: in {{p|Paris}}, {{p|London}}; he
Of all their emperors alone achieved
{{p|England}}, where {{c|2196|Henry IV}} would host and see

Him, {{x|Christmas}} fourteen hundred, {{p|Eltham}}; and
His Greek scholars taught classics to the men
Of {{p|England}}, humanism's seed; no band
Of crusade came; the {{p|West}} was busy then

With its {{x|Great Schism}}, its Hundred Years' War;
So {{c|3301|Manuel}} came back home with empty hands.
His son, {{c|3302|John VIII}}, then tried one hope more:
{{c|3303|Council of Ferrara-Florence}}'s bands

Joined East and West on paper, thirty-eight;
The Greek delegation, to save the town,
Agreed, in their desperate, last estate,
Accepted, bowing, for the walls and crown,

the Latin terms for ships and men from {{p|Rome}}.
At home the clergy would not have it so:
the question was whose hat would rule the dome,
the cardinal's or turban's. They said no.

"Better the turban than the miter," said
the city's lords: one altar, whose the crown.
The aid came: {{c|3304|Crusade of Varna}}; it bled
in forty-four; {{c|3231|Murad II}} cut it down.

And John's brother, {{c|3305|Constantine XI}}, the last,
In {{p|Peloponnese}}, at {{p|Mistras}}, crowned;
The march home was too perilous; he cast
His pleas to {{c|2825|Pope}}, {{p|Venice}}, {{p|Genoa}}, round,

The {{c|3306|Holy Roman Emperor}}; and came
A few hundred Italians, galleys few;
No great crusade. They mended, all the same,
The walls of {{c|3307|Theodosius II}}, drew

The chain across the {{p|Golden Horn}}; and still
{{c|2683|Hagia Sophia}} rang till the end
Under {{c|3240|Constantine XI}}; he would not, till
The last, give up the city, yield, or bend,

Nor take the Sultan's offer of his life.
When, May the twenty-ninth, the walls broke through,
He threw his robes off, plunged into the strife
To die a common soldier; none then knew

His body; he was turned to marble, sealed,
The {{c|3308|Marble Emperor}}, and waits; he'll wake
When the city's reclaimed. On that last field
Died {{p|Rome}}'s long line, upon the walls' last break.

Eleven hundred twenty-three years long,
From {{c|3309|Constantine I}} onward; and the line,
An emperor of {{p|Rome}} had reigned; the song
Back to {{c|2490|Augustus}}, ended; this, the sign.
