# Chapter 133: The DE Split and the Toba Catastrophe

They came ashore at {{p|Yemen}}: wet and green,
Thick grass, where seasonal rivers still ran,
Was {{p|Arabia}} in that {{x|Pleistocene}};
The land was hospitable to the plan

Of a small band in search of a new home.
They stayed; they multiplied; and then, out east,
Along south {{p|Asia}}'s coast they came to roam,
Along the shore, where choosing mattered least:

The way was easier to trace than choose:
The dry interior upon the left,
The sea upon the right hand, theirs to use,
The prompt of coastal food, the shells, the heft

Of fish, the tide pools, and the bells and bowls
Of mangrove swamps: and the migration's blur,
Its terms, its generations, and its tolls,
Of coast-walking, a slow and eastward stir.

And early in this eastward flow, the trunk
Of {{c|1854|CT}} had split; and from its root there came,
A few thousand years before, and deep sunk,
Two clades, as daughters: {{c|1855|DE}} and {{c|1856|CF}} by name.

And one of these two clades, the one called {{c|1855|DE}},
Would do a thing dramatic, while the way
Of {{c|1856|CF}}, the other, and its tale and plea,
Must wait until the chapter next. Today:

And {{c|1855|DE}} itself divided: {{c|1850|D}} from {{c|1861|E}},
Whose chapter, its return by land, awaits:
In {{p|Africa}} again {{c|1861|E}} came to be.
Today we follow {{c|1850|D}} beyond these straits.

{{c|1867|Haplogroup D}} was once of widest spread
Of all {{p|Y}}-lines on earth, and not the small,
The narrow, dry-cornered estate, instead,
Assigned today; for {{c|1850|D}} roamed free through all

Of {{p|Asia}}: there {{c|1850|D}} was the prow and fit
Of the first wave's south route; its folk would shoot
Down the {{p|Indian}} Ocean's chute, with it,
Peopled pre-{{c|1868|Dravidian}} coasts, the root

Of what is {{p|India}} today; moved on
To {{p|Southeast Asia}}; and, along the isles
Of {{p|Indonesia}}, then to {{p|Java}} drawn,
Pushed north through {{p|Myanmar}} and through {{p|Thailand}}'s miles

Of forest, to the far frontier, the bar
Of {{p|China}}'s southwest: so the drift would be,
The early coastal drift, vow, avatar,
That carried {{c|1850|D}} across, from sea to sea.

{{c|1850|D}} was the free and first-arriving one,
The pioneer, whose patient progress there
Had won the lands of southern {{p|Asia}}, none
Before it there, of human hand still bare.

And then. Some seventy-four thousand years
Ago (the earth's own chronicle, again,
Recorded it, as in its rock appears),
{{p|Sumatra}}, by a land-bridge joined, and when

{{p|Toba}}, a mountain there, erupted. Not
A small one, nor the ordinary flow
Of cinder-coned and ordinary hot
Outburst: the largest blast earth had to show

In twenty-five million years. And its throw,
Three thousand cubic kilometers cast
Of rock and ash, fell over all below,
On {{p|Asia}}, and the continent so vast,

Shadowing it. It dimmed the sky; the rim
Of the bright sun was blocked for years, and all
The temperature dropped fast, and, cold and grim,
That had been warming felt the winter's thrall.

Into a sudden cold, the hammer-blow,
Like a fist on the living systems, fell,
The stun of the eruption, closing slow,
On south and east {{p|Asia}}. The forests' knell

Was rung: they died. Food sources failed. The whole
Of green, in mist and fog of ash, would stop;
Photosynthesis ceased; and so the toll,
Through seasons of growth, with no harvest crop,

Fell on the settled life of those who bore
The lineage {{c|1850|D}}: all collapsed. The spread,
The first-wave migrants' body and their core,
Through tens of stations, settlements that led

{{p|Iran}} to {{p|Indonesia}}, starved and died,
And lay in the cold; and beneath the ash,
Pneumonia-stricken, and the light denied,
The coastal settlements lay; and the cache

Of inland camps lay buried deeper yet.
Most carriers of {{c|1850|D}}, within the close
Of years behind {{p|Toba}}, had paid the debt
Of death; and then the barriers that rose

To life were absolute. And groups that came
Within the second wave found stations there,
And settlements, still standing, still the same,
But emptied, bare of people. Lands that wear

Once {{c|1850|D}} as their majority, were seized
By later comers, coming from the west,
{{c|1849|C}}- and {{c|1862|F}}-descendants, whose way was eased,
From zones less hurt, who now expanded, pressed

Into the vacancy left. {{c|1850|D}} was all
But lost; almost; and, scattered on the map,
In three refugia, all of them small,
By sheer good hap, some fenced off, in the lap

Of distance, buffered; some by luck, where folds
Of ground broke up the ash, survived. And so
The first of these, the one the story holds:
{{p|Japan}}, cut off by sea. And here the blow

Of {{p|Toba}}, come from off the {{p|Asian}} shore,
Arrived, but thin; the winds' long course went wide;
The ash came with no fullness in its roar,
And crude geography was on their side.

Mere luck, of long-faced islands set offshore,
Sufficed to keep that slight stand living, too:
{{c|1850|D}}-bearing folk survived there, and they bore,
In later ages, grew into the new

And founding strata of {{p|Japan}}'s own name.
The marker {{c|1850|D}}, near half a nation whole:
Today near half of {{p|Japanese}} men claim
The father-line's inheritance, the scroll

Of that first wave, preserved in islands, passed
Through tens of thousands of years; from that date
In {{p|Japan}}, {{c|1850|D}} runs deeper, holding fast,
Than culture's overlays, however late:

The {{p|Ainu}} have {{c|1850|D}}; the {{p|Yamato}} too;
Gently but firmly, all the island chain,
The whole long archipelago, runs through,
The seed-vault of the first great southern train

Of early migrants. Second, the plateau
Of high {{p|Tibet}}: cold, elevated, far
From where the plume moved; little ash below
Had fallen; for the plume's own moving car

Went south and east, not north. That plateau, grim
Already, showed its strange resilience: those,
Inhospitable, and in mercy slim,
Who had arrived before {{p|Toba}} arose

Remained. The altitude they had endured
Now saved them, and rude difficulty; on
Lived {{c|1850|D}}; and hardship into help matured
In {{p|Tibet}}; and {{p|Tibetan}} men have drawn

That lineage at rates that echo still
The spread before the ash; hard land had kept,
Took up, preserved the line, the plateau's will,
What easy lands served up to ruin; slept

With it across millennia. The third
Is in the {{p|Bay of Bengal}}: islands, small,
The {{p|Andaman Islands}}, so little heard,
And scattered, tiny, and with nothing tall

Or wide infinity of land to spare,
Between {{p|India}} and {{p|Myanmar}}, they lay,
And, crucially, too far away from there,
From {{p|Toba}}'s plume to smother. To this day

The {{p|Andamanese}} peoples, who are three,
In what may be the purest form that stands
({{c|1869|Jarawa}}, {{c|1870|Onge}}, {{c|1871|Sentinelese}}), hold {{c|1850|D}}
Surviving: the relay of the first bands

Undiluted, held in a folk alone;
Until these recent centuries, they stood
In total isolation, on their own,
Apart from all the human neighborhood.

No cultural link joins them: none. The point
Is one the {{x|Gaiad}} presses: some would weave
A story out of them, and would anoint
Three carriers of {{c|1850|D}}. Do we believe

Among the islands, to an ancient flow,
Inherited from there, some ancient trace?
The {{x|Gaiad}} says to stop. The answer: no.

The story is catastrophe. It's not
A hidden cultural oneness; the loss,
Near-total, of one line, is what we've got,
And not some old inheritance or gloss

Of some civilizational descent
Inherited and old. Long is the span
Of seventy thousand years: see what's meant:
No tongue lives that long stretch, no language can;

A thousand deaths they die. And on the ground
Is culture built, rebuilt, rebuilt, before,
And over, upward from the floor, around;
A father-marker, found in three, no more,

In three disconnected peoples, is fact
About the {{p|Y}}-chromosome, and no more,
Beyond it, and no further tie or pact;
And nothing of the culture which they bore

Within their mouths, is told us: it is lost,
Dead, which no soul, no living soul, recalls,
Dead with the droughts and the cold years that crossed
Behind {{p|Toba}}. Cultural islands, walls

Are their own local growth, long and alone:
{{p|Japan}} is {{p|Japan}}; and no heirloom lies
Beneath them; and {{p|Tibet}} is {{p|Tibet}}'s own;
The {{p|Andamans}} are theirs; there are no ties.

The chromosome's no soul; and it is what
The flag upon the gene-map comes to show:
Biology can tell, and nothing but:

Not of religion, nor of thought's long tree,
Nor of philosophy, nor what they are,
In what they tell themselves, these living three.
