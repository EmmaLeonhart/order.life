# Chapter 168: Confucius and Laozi

In {{p|China}}, while the {{p|Zhou}} king's rule grew thin,
And vassals fought as kingdoms of their own,
In {{p|Spring and Autumn}}, from the chaos in
The land, a hundred schools of thought were sown:

There were {{p|Confucian}}, {{p|Daoist}}, {{p|Legalist}},
The {{p|Mohist}}, and the {{p|School of Names}}, and more,
The {{p|Yin-Yang}}; and of all of them, the list
Was led by two, who stood at the root's core.

{{c|1958|Confucius}}, {{c|2296|Kongzi}}, {{c|2297|Master Kong}}, was born
In {{p|Lu}}, of noble blood, but fallen low;
His father died when he was three; and worn
By poverty, his mother raised him so.

He learned the {{p|Zhou}} rites; and he served in {{p|Lu}},
And rose, and was the minister of law;
But those he crossed were strong, and out he flew,
And fourteen years he wandered, and he saw

No ruler who would hear him; home he came,
And taught, and set the classics right; and died
Believing he had failed; and yet his name
Lives on, the teacher of the world's wide side.

His students wrote his sayings down; he said
That five ties make the world: the king and men,
The father, son; the husband, wife; the head
And younger brother; friends with friends; and then

Each one has his own duty: rule with {{p|ren}},
Benevolence; and serve with loyal heart;
The father nurtures; sons keep {{p|xiao}}; and when
Each does his own, the world is not apart.

But when the kings are cruel, and sons forsake,
The world falls into chaos; so the cure
Begins with the king's virtue, which will make
The rest grow good, as wind bends grass, and sure.

No god's command, no judgment after death,
Nor reason's rule alone, but ties of kin,
And learning, rite, and thought, with every breath,
Make the {{p|junzi}}, the gentleman within.

He is not born, but made: he learns the songs,
The rites, {{p|Book of Changes}}, and the {{p|Annals}};
And beauty's tied to goodness; and he longs
To shape his feeling through the old channels.

{{c|2144|Laozi}}, the {{c|2301|Old Master}}, kept the scrolls
Of the {{p|Zhou}} court, and older far than {{c|1958|Kong}};
{{c|1958|Confucius}} came to him; of all his goals
He told him: let them go; your wish so strong

To set the world right: drop it. Then he left
The court, which rotted, on a water-ox,
And rode out west; and at the mountain's cleft
The gatekeeper there, {{c|2302|Yinxi}}, by the rocks,

Knew him, and begged him: write it down. And so
Five thousand words he wrote, the {{p|Dao De Jing}},
And rode on west, and none would ever know
Where he had gone, or see him, or his thing.

The {{p|Dao}} that can be spoken of is not
The lasting {{p|Dao}}; the Way's beyond our speech;
And it moves by {{p|wu wei}}, without the knot
Of forcing; who would force, will fail to reach.

The soft things of the world will overcome
The hard; the water yields, and wears the stone;
And so the sage does little, and the sum
Is much; and so the king who rules alone

By force will fail, and rouse revolt; but he
Who rules but lightly, lets the people be,
Rules well: rule a great state as you would see
A small fish cooked, and do not stir it free.

{{c|1958|Confucius}} said: be good, and lead by deed;
{{c|2144|Laozi}} said: do nothing, let it flow;
And both grew from the {{p|Zhou}}'s same broken need,
And {{p|China}} kept them both, and kept them so:

In public, the {{p|Confucian}} rite and law;
In private life, the {{p|Daoist}} hill and stream;
The scholar served the court, and also saw
The mountain, and the tea, the empty dream.

The poem honours this. It keeps no score
Between the many gods and one; the way
Of {{p|China}}, keeping both, is that one door,
One house, which keeps two teachings, day by day,

Its own rites, both of them, in its own view:
Not things that clash, but parts, as they were meant;
Not rival faiths, but partners, are the two;
The ones who made them knew the way they went.

So honor them: {{c|1958|Confucius}}, who would teach
The ties, the rite, the {{p|ren}}, the {{p|junzi}}'s art;
{{c|2144|Laozi}}, of the Way beyond our speech;
Two poles of one house, never held apart.
