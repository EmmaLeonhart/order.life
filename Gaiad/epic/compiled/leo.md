# Leo (9宮) - Days 225-252


# Chapter 225: First Migration

The Book of {{c|3402|Lehi}} opens on the sea:
Not the warm lake, the river's shallow ford
That apes could wade; but the {{p|Pacific}}, free
And vast, the largest face of water, poured

Across the hemisphere, where no land lay
To pave with solid ground; and yet it called,
They answered: sixteen thousand years, the day
Before our era, when the north was walled

By ice; the corridors were locked; the coast
Was open. And the sea was lower then,
By sixty fathoms; and the shelves, the most
Of them, lay wide and tidal, for the men,

Where the kelp thickened, and the otter, seal,
The shore-bird, and the intertidal strand
Gave food to those who knew the current's wheel;
And {{c|3402|Lehi}} knew the current, and the land:

Not one man, but a people, and a line,
A maritime and patient clan, of yore,
Who fished the coasts of {{p|Asia}}; by the sign,
Hopped from {{p|Japan}} to {{p|Kamchatka}}, and more,

To the {{p|Aleutian}} chain, the stepping-stones
That arced across the north {{p|Pacific}}, teeth
Of mountain rock; and southward, by the zones
Of glaciers, blue and towering, beneath,

Which calved their icebergs into fjords; through,
Past {{p|Haida Gwaii}}, where current met the tide,
And salmon ran in streams, the season's due,
The first and oldest entry, by the side

Of ocean; thirst for headland, and for bay,
The river-mouth where fish ran thick, and where
The shellfish lay in intertidal clay,
And driftwood washed ashore, for warmth and care,

And for the frame of shelters on the beach.
They sailed out to {{p|America}}; they came
By boat, the humble craft whose greatest reach
Of all the {{x|Pleistocene}} was this, the same:

Skin-boat, dugout, and the sewn-plank canoe
That hugged the evergreen and kelp-draped shore;
They drank the coast and ate the sea: the true
Mussel and clam and urchin, rockfish, more,

The halibut; the tidal fisher's art,
The eye that read the current and the swell.
The sea was all their road; for, from the start,
The oldest {{c|3404|Americans}} knew it well,

The ocean's rhythm; and their step, the stroke
Of paddle, and their road the coast, through all;
Their compass was the gray whale's road, which spoke
From {{p|Asia}} to {{p|America}}, the call

The whale had known for ages; and the men
Followed the great leviathan's bright way,
The breath along the kelp-highway; and then
The sign: at {{p|Monte Verde}}, south, it lay,

In {{p|Chile}}, fourteen thousand years ago:
Folk lived at the {{p|Americas}}' far end
Before the inland corridor would show
An opening; and so by sea they'd wend.

Off {{p|California}}, {{p|Channel Islands}} hold
The {{c|3405|Arlington Springs Woman}}'s old bones, there,
Thirteen thousand years, alone, silent, cold,
On isles that only boats could reach; and where,

In {{p|Oregon}}, at {{p|Paisley Caves}}, the trace
Of the first {{c|3404|Americans}} still remains;
By trowel and by screen, the coast's own place
Is kept: they came by water, and the plains

Of both the continents they filled, while yet
The inland lay locked. And they went on south,
Past {{p|California}}'s golden kelp, and set
Down by {{p|Mexico}}'s warm {{p|Pacific}} mouth,

Through {{p|Central America}}'s narrow crest,
To {{p|Colombia}}, {{p|Ecuador}}, the long
Peru's coast, where the {{p|Humboldt Current}}'s best
Fishery on earth, the anchovy's song,

The cold upwelling of the fertile deep.
And some turned inland, up the rivers, through
The passes, the interior's wide sweep:
The grassland, forest, valley, desert; new

Homes; but the coast was first, the purest road,
The oldest one, the mother-road of all,
The {{p|Americas}}' first way. Honor, owed,
To {{c|3402|Lehi}}, the first migrant, at the call

Of the sea-road; whose drama was the oar,
The patient reading of the coast's long strand;
Who did not wait for melting ice, nor for
The land-bridge, but who took the ocean's hand,

And felt the kelp beneath the hull, the pull
Of current, and the whale's road out ahead;
And followed it, so faithful and so full,
To a world where no human foot had tread,

And made it home. The Book of {{c|3402|Lehi}} starts
Upon the water, in a boat; the note
Not of the hunters, but the fishers' arts,
The paddle's stroke, the sea-bird's crying throat

Above the morning swell; the coast, that went
South, and still south, the longest road a tongue
Would tell; and on the mouth, the salt it lent,
The sea, to the first {{c|3404|American}}, young.


# Chapter 226: Division of Tongues

The fluted point was lovely, and could kill:
Shaped like a willow leaf, a channel run
Up either face, the {{c|3406|Clovis}} spear's own skill,
Chipped stone thinned near to light, as by the sun,

And almost surgical; the knapper's eye
Turned at the camp, by night, the patient art,
Percussion: strike, examine, strike, and try,
Until the biface took its final part:

The bane of mammoth, mastodon, the vast
Ground sloth, the horse, the camel, all things great
That walked the {{x|Pleistocene}}, until the last.
Thirteen millennia before our date,

The {{c|3406|Clovis}} people turned, and moved inland
Into the land, and through the ice-free way
'Twixt {{p|Laurentide}} and {{p|Cordilleran}}'s hand
Of ice, a tundra road, a grassland's sway,

Through {{p|Alberta}}, {{p|Montana}} now; and brought
The point: the {{c|3406|Clovis}} point, named for the place,
{{p|Clovis, New Mexico}}, where first they sought
And found it; and it spread, at such a pace,

Across both continents in centuries,
{{p|Patagonia}}, {{p|Nova Scotia}}'s coast,
The fluted shape, the signature of these
Who learned the megafaunal hunt the most:

The bond between the hand and mammoth's fall.
They hunted in coordinated bands:
The drive, the surround, the communal call
Of killing: and a dozen working hands

Drove the herd toward the arroyo's edge,
The cliff, the bog, where the great mammoth's weight
Became its doom; mired in the marshy sedge,
It stood; the hunters closed, and, sure and straight,

Their atlatl darts went into blood and flank,
The shoulder, belly of the beast; and none
Had learned to fear the human feast, the rank
Of hunters; and the whole land shook, undone.

Their kind had fifty million years evolved
Without a human hunter; and they knew
No fear of the two-legged; nothing solved
Their trust, unlinked and fatal, was their due:

The mammoth, mastodon, the short-faced bear,
The {{c|3407|Smilodon}}, ground sloth elephant-tall,
Defenseless, giant; thirty-five, from there,
Kinds vanished in two thousand years, and all.

The warming played its part, the grass that shrank,
The melting ice; but in the mammoth's heart
The spear-point was the proof, and where it sank
The hunter asked no leave; the prey, no part.

The {{c|3406|Clovis}} hunters spread, as water fills
Down the great {{p|Mississippi}}, branch by branch,
Across the {{p|Great Plains}}, through the forest hills,
The {{p|Eastern Seaboard}}'s woods, an avalanche

Of camps on terraces; and kill-sites laid
On grasslands; mammoth-bone in charcoal found
{{p|Alaska}} to {{p|Tierra del Fuego}}; made
A thousand years filled both; the speed, profound.

A world had waited ages for this one,
A hunter skilled, adaptable, and new;
And the debt was paid in extinction, done.
But from the killing came the living, too:

The bands spread into niche on niche; the lands,
Diverse, demanded tools and ways anew
For desert, forest, and the complex strands
Of coast; from one, a thousand peoples grew;

And from one point, a thousand point-styles came:
{{c|3408|Folsom}} and {{c|3409|Dalton}}, {{c|3410|Plano}}, {{c|1196|Eden}}, each
Tuned to its hunt: the bison, {{c|1196|Eden}}'s aim
With long and narrow blade; and {{c|3408|Folsom}}'s reach,

Delicate channel, for the smaller game;
The local hearth replaced the common one
Of the first wandering; and, not the same,
Each turned within: its valley, river, sun,

Its prey. Honor the {{c|3406|Clovis}} point, the first
To span {{p|America}} from coast to coast,
The lovely tool of stone, in which were versed
The hands that opened up a world, a host;

And honor too the mammoth's bone, the tusk
Of mastodon in sand, the ground sloth's claw
In the cave; the magnificent, in dusk
Vanished; the enormous dead, by that law,

Gave up their continent, astonished, to
The small, the clever, and the new; and so
From one tongue many tongues; the many grew,
Divided, as the people learned to go.


# Chapter 227: Division of Tongues

And then the silence fell between them: not
The silence of the empty land, but kin
Who could not greet or chide, for they forgot
The other's tongue; and words that once had been

The common coin, the word for water, clean,
Had shifted, vowel, consonant, in turn,
Until the old man's speech, with years between,
Was strange; the grandchild's ear could not discern.

Ten thousand years before our era, great
Diversity of tongues began to grow
Across the hemispheres; and distance' weight
Pressed on the tongue as glacier on stone, slow,

Relentless; stressed and unstressed, ground and worn
Into new speech that neither side could know;
The same divergent storms that once had torn
{{p|Proto-Indo-European}}; and so

Into {{c|3411|Sanskrit}}, {{c|3349|Greek}}, {{c|3388|Latin}}, {{c|3412|Germanic}},
Worked on the first {{c|3404|Americans}}' one speech;
Here the walls were greater: the volcanic
{{p|Rocky Mountains}}, the {{p|Amazon}}'s green reach,

Its labyrinth; the {{p|Atacama}}'s sand,
Rainless for centuries; the dramas built
Walls no human could cross; and, in each land
Behind its wall, a tongue grew to the hilt.

{{c|3413|Na-Dené}}, the great northern family's way,
Rang to the {{p|American Southwest}}'s floor
From far {{p|Alaska}}, from the {{c|3414|Tlingit}} bay;
So distinct from its neighbors that it bore

The mark of its own crossing, the second,
From {{p|Asia}}, after the first; and they came
Their grammar and their sound, by none reckoned;
Their own, not of the southern tongues' own frame.

{{c|3415|Algonquian}}, vast and whispering kin,
Family of the eastern woods and shore
Of the {{p|Great Lakes}}; and from the {{c|3416|Cree}}, within
The northern birch, to {{c|3417|Blackfoot}} plains of yore,

Each one a thousand years of parting's yield
From the first ancestor, whose words had cracked
On the forest path; the caribou's field
Drew one band north; the deer drew south; the fact

Of parting. {{c|3418|Siouan}} poured across the plains,
{{p|Missouri}}'s mouth to {{p|Carolinas}}' east;
The {{c|3419|Lakota}}, {{c|3420|Dakota}}, {{c|3421|Crow}}; their strains
Shaped by the open sky, the bison's feast,

Wind-carried syllables across the grass.
{{c|3422|Uto-Aztecan}}: the long thread that ran
From {{p|Idaho}}'s volcanic shelf, to pass
Down to {{p|Mexico City}}'s urban span:

{{c|3423|Shoshone}} and {{c|3424|Comanche}} to the north,
The {{c|3425|Hopi}} in their mesa towns; and far,
The {{c|3426|Nahuatl}}-speaking folk, pouring forth,
The {{c|3427|Aztec}}'s pyramids, where temples are;

One family, three thousand miles in length,
The proof of connection running beneath
The multitude of surface; and in strength,
{{c|3428|Mayan}}, the glyph, the written word's own wreath,

The only script the {{p|Americas}} made:
A syllabary, angular, in stone,
And painted on the codex page; it bade
The first and only reading, all its own.

{{c|3429|Quechuan}}: tongue that nursed the {{c|3403|Inca}}'s reach
From {{p|Quito}} to {{p|Santiago}}, the road's
Own language; and the knot, the quipu's speech,
Accounting; and the lingua franca's loads

Down all {{p|South America}}'s spine; and how
Rule without writing? By the knot, the string
Of colored cord, encoded; and, somehow,
The fingers read the threads, the dangling thing.

Hundreds more: {{c|3430|Penutian}}, {{c|3431|Hokan}}'s line
On the {{p|Pacific}} coast; and {{c|3432|Tupi}}, down
The {{p|Amazon}}; and {{c|3433|Chibchan}} on the spine
Of {{p|Central America}}'s tangled crown:

Six hundred tongues at contact, and before,
A thousand; and their unity had broke
Like a stone by the knapper's hand; and more,
Each piece, a cutting edge, a perfect stroke,

A tool of thought. Honor the tongues: each one
A world; each grammar a philosophy;
Each lost tongue is a flag furled, and done,
A sealed prophecy none can read; for see:

A tongue dies; a library burns; the creed,
The dream, the daily naming of the world
Goes silent; and the silence, as decreed,
The saddest flag that ever was unfurled

Over the ruin of the alliance
Between the human mind and human tongue;
Six hundred voices, a thousand's science,
Divided, as the hemisphere was young.


# Chapter 228: Norte Chico

Eight thousand years before our era, south
The {{c|3413|Na-Dené}} moved, and all the world with them,
While glaciers slowly drew back from the mouth
Of the north, and the northern forest's stem

Crept north across the warming land; and went
A second stream flowed from {{p|Alaska}}'s moss
And mountain-shadowed coast, the southward bent
Of the old road rekindled; and, across,

Their tongue bore no kinship to tongues around:
The late arrivals, whose crossing had come
From {{p|Asia}} long after the first had found
And filled the land; their grammar was the drum

That marked them as the second wave. And where
The salmon hung in curtains, silver, bright,
The {{c|3414|Tlingit}} settled; totem poles, like prayer
Of cedar, rose into the mist's grey light,

With crests of eagles, bears, and ravens, grained
In the red wood: the heraldry of clan,
Kin's covenant with beasts; and wealth, once gained,
The potlatch gave away, as was their plan.

The {{c|3434|Haida}}, on {{p|Haida Gwaii}}, would build
The ocean-going war-canoe, a hull
Of single cedar, sixty feet, which filled
The open strait with war and trade, past gull

And terror, prows as killer whale's own mouth,
Or the thunderbird: the {{c|3413|Na-Dené}}, their way,
Mastered the coast that flowed toward the south,
Their tongue shaped by the tide and salmon's day.

And others kept on moving, south and south,
Through mountain passes, past the desert's sun,
Through the {{p|Great Basin}}'s sage, the silence' mouth,
Into the {{p|American Southwest}}, run

In the canyon's clefts; and there, much later,
The {{c|3435|Athabaskan}} peoples spread and fanned
Out through the desert, migration greater:
{{c|3436|Apache}}, {{c|3437|Navajo}}, across the land;

The {{c|3438|Diné}}, those called the People, who arrived
Where the {{c|3439|Puebloan}} and {{c|3440|Hohokam}} had worked
The canyon, mesa, sand; and so they thrived,
And learned the patient corn, nor ever shirked.

The corn was coming. Long ago, the land,
Seven thousand years before our time, where
{{p|Sierra Madre}} cut the sky, the hand
Of the {{c|3441|Mesoamerican}} who knelt there

Before a grass, the {{c|3442|teosinte}}, dry
And poor, whose seed was in a tiny cob
No longer than a thumb; the farmer's eye
Saw what selection's patience made its job:

The kernel's chance, the raw stuff of the art,
The greatest engineering ever known:
Evolution steered, a weed set apart
To be the staff of life; and each seed sown

Was chosen: largest seed, and ear grown long,
The softer kernel, and the greater yield,
More generous; and over ages, strong,
The cultivar took over all the field:

{{c|3443|Maize}}: the grass remade by the human will,
Engineered miracle, a crop which could
Not seed itself without the planter's skill
To strip the husk and plant it, as it should:

The truce between the species, partnership
That bound the farmer and the field, the field
And farmer, in a covenant, the grip
Of neither could without the other yield;

The tamer and the tamed, both bound, both fed,
In the great bargain; and from it would come
All civilizations after it, fed
By corn: no granary, no temple's drum,

No surplus, priest, or king, no counted day,
No writing, no great realm without the corn;
The corn was all, the hearth; and, in its way,
The world of the {{p|Americas}} was born.

And on {{p|Peru}}'s dry coast, where through the deep
{{p|Humboldt Current}} spilt its cold upwelling
Beside the driest land, the richest sweep
Of sea, the first {{c|3404|Americans}}, dwelling,

Built what would be the {{c|3444|Norte Chico}}'s first core:
Not yet the plazas, not the sunken court,
But fishing villages along the shore,
The cotton net, the anchor stone, the sort

Of racks, the anchovy's bright silver rows,
The settled life, the first commitment made
To a single place: we won't wander; those
Who stay: the shore, the field, the river's aid;

The nomad's freedom given up for chains
Of plenty. So honor the {{c|3413|Na-Dené}}'s way,
The second wave, whose separate tongue, the gains
Of totem, potlatch, cedar boat, would stay;

And honor corn, the grass whose feeder was
The patient hand, the covenant of seed
And sower; and the fishers, who, because
Of their dry coast, made of the sea their need,

The first {{c|3404|Americans}} who chose to stay,
Who built the lasting village, and the creed
Of settled life; and so they paved the way
For pyramid and plaza, priest, decreed.


# Chapter 229: Olmec Foundations

In desert by the sea they built; no lord
Commanded it, no slave was driven; so
And the {{c|3444|Norte Chico}} cities, in accord,
Gave the {{p|Americas}} the first to show

The monumental and the planned, before
Our era by three thousand years, there, through
Coastal {{p|Peru}}, a narrow strip of shore
'Twixt {{p|Andes}}' wall and the {{p|Pacific}}'s blue,

Where thirty rivers cut the desert's wedge
To fertile valleys; there the {{c|3445|Caral}} raised
Their pyramids; not of the {{p|Nile}}'s smooth edge,
Perfect, whose limestone files still gleam, and praised,

But mounds of quarried stone and reed-bag fill,
The platform-pyramids, which rose in rounds
Above the valley floor upon the hill:
Six at {{p|Caral}} alone; and from its grounds

The largest rose a hundred fifty feet;
With no ceramics, writing, metal tools,
It reached the city; and it was complete
Enough to marshal thousands by its rules

To raise the rough and sacred platforms; and
The priests' authority came not from sword
But song: for those who dug in {{p|Caral}}'s land
Found not one weapon, and no warring horde;

Instead they found the flute: thirty-two flutes
Of condor bone and deer bone, music's thread
Woven into the founding, through the roots;
The quipu too, already here, the spread

Of knotted string, the breakthrough, from the start,
Tactile tongue of the one who'd keep the store,
Who counted harvest, cotton bale, and part
Of fish; the knots, economy's own lore.

In colored string. {{p|Caral}} was not alone:
The {{c|3444|Norte Chico}} spread out across the ring
Of coastal valleys; {{p|Aspero}}, the stone
Of the sea-founding, where the nets would bring

The anchovy by tons; and cotton fields
Supplied the nets: the coast and valley's run,
The fisher and the farmer, and their yields,
The protein and the fiber, twined as one:

The fish fed farmers; farmers' cotton made
The nets that caught the fish: the circle grew
The surplus, and the offering was laid
Before the spirits of the earth, their due.

As old as {{p|Sumer}}'s ziggurats, and as
The {{x|Old Kingdom}}'s great stone along the {{p|Nile}},
The {{c|3444|Norte Chico}} stood, and the proof it has
Is this: that culture is no single isle

Of the {{p|Fertile Crescent}}; convergent, free
Invention: give the human fish enough,
And cotton, and a river; and you'll see
The pyramid: the urge is in our stuff.

And northward, in the humid lowlands where
{{p|Gulf of Mexico}}'s rivers spread their silt
On {{p|Tabasco}}, {{p|Veracruz}}, richest there
Of {{p|Mesoamerica}}, the {{c|3446|Olmec}} built.

Fifteen hundred years before us, there burst
{{p|America}}'s mother-culture: the heads,
Basalt, from the {{p|Tuxtla Mountains}}, first
Dragged sixty miles, without the wheel; the threads

Of labor: tons, by raft and roller, rope,
And ramp, the pull of hundreds; and, by craft,
The {{c|3446|Olmec}} portrait of the ruler's scope,
The head, nine feet, as carved from one great shaft:

The full lips, the broad nose, the helmet's band,
Precise, the face of this king, not of all:
The {{c|3446|Olmec}} carved the human, by the hand,
In basalt, the first portrait, nine feet tall.

The {{c|3446|Olmec}} gave the calendar, the vast
{{x|Long Count}}, vigesimal, the counting art,
System the {{c|2308|Maya}} kept; the baktun, cast,
Katun, tun, uinal; moon, sun, {{c|95|Venus}}' part,

Which in the {{c|2308|Maya}} courts would reach its height,
But here was born; the sacred ballgame too,
The rubber ball, the I-shaped court; the rite
Where the losing captain fell, the gods' due,

The human sacrifice within the game,
The beating heart, the life raised to the sky.
They gave the jaguar-god, the rain-god's claim,
The child, were-jaguar, and the infant's cry,

The myth all after them would inherit;
The mother-culture, first to carve and count,
To sacrifice, to build mounds, to merit
The forest, ruling labor, to surmount

The jungle with the sacred center's ground.
Honor {{p|Caral}}, the fishers on that coast
Whose nets and flutes were where the first was found
Of the {{p|Americas}}' cities, first post;

And honor too the {{c|3446|Olmec}}, and the heads
That stare out from the jungle floor, the calm
The gaze of kings who wore the jaguar's threads
Of might, the calendar's knowledge, the psalm:

The twin foundations of the world to come,
The temple, plaza, ball-court that would raise
And echo and refine; the blueprint, from
The {{c|3446|Olmec}}; and the shrine, the glyph, the days

Their children's children would create: the stair
Of pyramids, the written glyph, the art;
The {{c|3446|Olmec}} gave the blueprint; and the prayer
Of history replied, built on that start.


# Chapter 230: Maya Dynasties

The jungle parted, and the city rose:
White limestone causeways cut the forest's sea
Like scars of light through green; and at their close
The pyramid, the temple, climbing free

Above the ceiba trees into the sky,
The fountain of the sacred, stairway's frieze
From human to divine. The names roll by
Like temple bells: {{p|Tikal}}, {{p|Copán}}; and these,

{{p|Palenque}}, {{p|Calakmul}}: the {{c|2308|Maya}}'s towns,
The jeweled, painted capitals, the age
{{x|Classic}}, of war and peace, of art, the crowns
Of starry reckoning; the highest stage

The {{p|Americas}}' mind, the jungle's child.
A thousand years before our era, slow,
The {{c|3446|Olmec}} mother-culture, waning, mild,
Gave birth to its inheritor: and so

The {{c|2308|Maya}} took the calendar, the art,
The glyph, the pyramid, jaguar; refined
Them to a brilliance none matched, set apart
In the {{p|Americas}}; and vast, their mind.

Their writing logosyllabic, and full,
Eight hundred glyphs, the angular and carved
And painted word, the only one, the pull
Of the pre-Columbian world, which starved

For letters; and the scribe, the sacred hand,
Moved brush on codex bark and stela's face,
Recording dynasty, the conquest, and
The capture of the rival, birth, and grace

Of their accession, bloodletting; the fates
Of kings in stone, that still speak to the one
Who learned to read them. And the numbers' weights:
In twenties counted; and the zero won:

The placeholder, the nothing holding place
Of absent sums; as in {{c|3447|Indian}} lore,
Convergent, independent; in the space
Of mind the strangest find: its worth, its core,

Its value in its emptiness. Then, time:
The {{x|Long Count}}'s architecture, days amassed
To hundreds of millions of years; the climb
Of the baktun, four hundred years each, cast

In such a system that the priest could tell
{{c|95|Venus}}' synodic period, its way,
Its morning star and evening; and as well
Five eighty-four days and a part, they'd say,

Its error in five centuries so small
No naked eye has matched; eclipses, too,
The {{x|Dresden Codex}}' tables reckoned all,
The moon's shadow foretold; the priest, in view

On the temple's top, announced the sun's death,
The jaguar-sun, down to {{p|Xibalba}}'s night,
The underworld; and its return, the breath
Of the new day foretold, fulfilled, in light.

At {{p|Palenque}}: the great king's tomb, below,
{{c|3448|K'inich Janaab Pakal}}'s, down the stair
In the {{p|Temple of the Inscriptions}}; so
The carved lid shows the king who's falling there

Into the earth-beast's jaws, to be reborn
In {{p|Xibalba}}; jade mask upon his face,
The jade and cinnabar, the offerings worn,
A tomb like {{c|2032|Tutankhamun}}'s, the place,

The pyramid's own heart. And then, the war:
{{p|Tikal}} and {{p|Calakmul}}, the two great states
Of the {{x|Classic}}, beautiful, savage, sore
Of rivalry: alliance, and the hates

Of the betrayed; the star-war glyph that told
Destruction total; the king captured, brought
Back for the sacrifice; the line of old
Replaced; the vassals turned, re-formed, and fought

Around the victor: politics, the game
Of jungle realms, the bargain of the crown
Divine. And then, the ninth age: and there came
Collapse: the system stretched, and broken down;

The people had outgrown the fragile soil;
The slash-and-burn could not keep pace; the drought
Came, and the rains failed, reservoirs; the toil
Of feeding millions failed; the poor, without,

Starved first; the kings made war for what was left;
And city after city closed its door:
The stucco crumbled, and the jungle, deft,
Took back the plaza; ceiba split the floor

Of temple stairs; the howler monkey sat
Where the king offered prayer and blood in vain
To gods who did not answer. Yet, for that,
The {{c|2308|Maya}} did not vanish; they remain:

The dancer dances; and the language lives,
Seven million speakers still, of the tongue
That carved the glyph; the living culture gives
Its thread, unbroken since the stairs were young;

No vanished past, but a thread that's still strong,
The {{p|Americas}}' oldest lettered mind;
Honor the {{c|2308|Maya}}: scribes who, all along,
Read stars, wrote time; the zero they would find,

Who built the pyramid in jungle's green
Cathedral; made the king a hero, and
A sacrifice; who wove the seen, unseen,
Into the angular glyph, by scribe's hand;

Honor the astronomers on the height
Of temple-tops; the bloodied king, whose gift
Fed the calendar's count; the dead, the bright
Living, seven million, whose voices lift

The oldest writing of the western world;
The {{c|2308|Maya}} tongue is spoken to this day,
Where jungle's flag is furled, and, unfurled,
The ancient covenant would not give way.


# Chapter 231: Phoenicians of Zarahemla

The epic turns its eye now to the sea:
Not {{c|3402|Lehi}}'s coastal creep, the first one's way,
But the open crossing, fearful and free,
The ship that came out of the east one day.

Five hundred years before our era, when
Sank all the {{p|Mediterranean}}'s rim;
{{c|3449|Phoenician}} sailors, who had been the men
Who rounded {{p|Africa}} at {{c|3450|Necho}}'s whim,

And come back telling of the southern stars,
The noon sun in the north; the ocean's son
Turned west, not by empire; but the scars
Of exile, when {{p|Jerusalem}} was done:

For {{c|2024|Nebuchadnezzar}}'s armies broke the wall,
And burned the {{x|Temple}}; and the {{c|3451|Judean}} throng
Was marched to {{p|Babylon}}; but yet not all;
Some fled to {{p|Egypt}}, south; and some, along

The coast, reached the {{c|3449|Phoenician}} ports, and shore
Of {{p|Sidon}} and of {{p|Tyre}}, whose sailors plied
From {{p|Carthage}} out to {{p|Cornwall}}'s tin, and more,
{{p|Canary Islands}} to the {{p|Black Sea}}'s side:

{{c|3449|Phoenician}} net against the world's last bound;
And some, as the {{c|3452|Mulekite}} record still tells,
As {{p|Zarahemla}}'s keeps it, crossed. Built sound,
{{c|3449|Phoenician}} ship, with oar and sail, through swells,

With pitch-caulked hull that poured through trough and crest,
A thousand years of shipcraft in its frame;
They sailed by stars: {{c|3453|Polaris}}, and the rest,
The {{x|Bears}}, the rising constellations' flame;

{{p|Sidon}}'s sailors knew the {{p|Atlantic}}'s ways:
The trade winds blowing west, the current true,
The {{p|North Equatorial}}, which conveys
Ships across, by mechanics, through and through.

From the {{p|Canary Islands}} to the shore
{{p|Caribbean}}, twenty-eight hundred miles;
A bireme at five knots, with wind, no more
Than twenty-three days, past the western isles;

As ships had sailed before, when they went round
{{p|Africa}}, which {{c|2183|Herodotus}} wrote down;
A lesser feat. And there are signs, still found:
{{p|Paraíba}}'s stone, found in {{p|Brazil}}'s green crown,

{{c|3449|Phoenician}} script; the {{c|3340|Roman}} jars, dredged up
From {{p|Guanabara Bay}}; and, in the slain,
Embalmed {{c|3454|Egyptian}} dead, the coca's cup
And nicotine, uncrossed, none could explain.

{{c|3456|Mulek}}, son of {{c|2271|Zedekiah}}, the last
Of {{p|Judah}}, fallen to {{p|Babylon}}; lit
The scattering; {{c|3456|Mulek}} escaped; and passed
With the {{c|3449|Phoenician}} sailors west; and it

The exile made a guest; and he would land
On {{p|Mesoamerica}}'s coast; polyglot,
Founded {{p|Zarahemla}}, the mixed, the grand,
Where {{c|3457|Israelite}} and sailor wed; their lot

Joined the native; and the mingling carried
The {{p|Mediterranean}}'s blood and tale
Into {{p|Americas}}' veins, and married,
Not as conquerors, but a quiet trail,

Too slow for history: the ship that beached,
The sailors stayed; the children spoke the tongue
Of mother, lost the father's; and, unreached,
The trade-routes closed; and the memory hung

By lore's thin thread, till that was frayed and lost,
And fragments stayed: we came out of the east,
Across the great water; storm-tossed, we crossed
In the destruction's time: memory's feast

A hundred nations kept it in their breast:
The ship, the storm-tossed passage from the sea;
Grandmother told the child, and, so possessed,
The child the grandchild: fragile, wild, and free,

Our human memory. Honor the bold
{{c|3449|Phoenician}} sailor and {{c|3451|Judean}} exile,
Whose joy or desperation found, of old,
The way between the known world and the isle

Unknown, the {{p|Mediterranean}}'s shore
And {{p|Caribbean}}'s forest-mantled way;
Honor {{p|Zarahemla}}, mixed, and its core:
The meeting-place of east and west, where they

Met, in the human story, plain and true:
The sea does not divide; the sea connects;
The oldest roads are salt and vast and blue;
Our memory, with all its old defects,

Does not erase the voyages that went,
Only the trace, the fragile record, crossed
The ocean keeps, in wave dissolved and spent;
The human spirit sails, it sails, though lost,

Farther than any chart or map can grant;
The sea remembers what the land forgets:
That each coast was a stranger's shore; the plant
Of foreign embers is the hearth that sets

The native child at home; each people's birth
Owes something to the stranger's gift, the hand,
The exile's seed, sown in alien earth,
Of sailor; and new creed and tongue would stand.


# Chapter 232: Oaxaca's Lords

Upon a mountain flattened by the hand
Of {{c|3458|Zapotec}} ambition, rose the plan
Of {{p|Monte Albán}}, white, a city planned
Above the valley's green, where time began

To count itself in stone and sculpted glyph,
Danzante figures carved along the wall—
The captives naked, broken, bodies stiff
In poses of defeat, that all who call

Upon this hilltop city's gate might know
The {{c|3458|Zapotec}} had conquered, and the proof
Was written in their enemies' tableau,
Their suffering immortalized on roof

And terrace wall, and temple, and the door.
Five hundred years before our era, there,
The mountain's summit leveled to a floor
Of plazas, tombs, and temples, the first stair

Of a civilization that would stand
A thousand years above {{p|Oaxaca}}'s vale,
Where three great valleys met and formed the land
Of cloud and corn, the green and fertile trail

That fed the lords who built upon the height.
No {{c|2308|Maya}} hand had shaped this city's art—
No {{c|3459|Teotihuacan}} design or rite
Had lent its grid—this was a world apart,

A sovereignty of the {{c|3458|Zapotec}} mind
That owed no debt to northern {{p|Mexico}}
Or southern {{p|Petén}}'s jungle lords, the kind
Of independence that the rivers' flow

Between the two sierras guaranteed:
{{p|Sierra Madre}} east, {{p|Sierra}} west,
And in between the valley's sheltered seed
Of culture, uncontrolled and self-possessed.

They wrote—and this is what must not be lost—
The {{c|3458|Zapotec}} carved their tongue into stone
Before the {{c|2308|Maya}} did, and paid the cost
Of being first: the system stood alone,

A writing born out of {{p|Oaxaca}}'s need
To mark the calendar, to name the dead,
To track the debt and memorize the deed
Of every ruler, every war that bled

Across the hillside terraces and tombs.
The glyphs were logographic—each a sign
A day-name, a place-name, a word that looms
In the genealogical divine

Record of who begat whom, and of who
Held power in which generation's chain—
The {{c|3458|Zapotec}} lord's authority grew
From ancestry: the living must maintain

The covenant the ancestors had sealed,
And every tomb beneath the plaza floor
Was an ancestor whose might, unconcealed,
Still flowed on upward through the temple's door

Into the living ruler's sacred right.
And in the valley's eastern arm there rose
A second people, masters of the night
Of genealogy—the {{c|3460|Mixtec}}, those

Whose painted books would prove the most complete
Surviving record, since the world began,
Of the {{p|Americas}}: the deerskin's sheet
Unscrolled to show a thousand years of clan

And kingdom, marriage, war, and sacrifice,
Each figure painted in the codex style:
The profile face, calendrical device
Of birth-names, the elaborate compile

Of every lord and lady, every throne.
{{c|3461|Codex Nuttall}} tells the tale of {{c|3462|Eight Deer}}—
{{c|3463|Jaguar Claw}}, he the conqueror whose own
Ambition nearly unified the sphere

Of {{c|3460|Mixtec}} kingdoms into one domain:
He won eleven cities, took the name
Of {{c|3464|Toltec}} royalty, and his campaign
Of marriages and murders built a frame

Of might such as {{p|Oaxaca}} had not seen—
Till his foes conspired, plotted, and seized
The conqueror himself, and the machine
Of his ambition stopped, and they appeased

The ancestors with {{c|3462|Eight Deer}}'s sacrifice.
The record lived; the codex kept the chain
Of each alliance, each cast of the dice
That marriage was in {{c|3460|Mixtec}} rule: the gain

Of one lord's daughter for another's son
Was not mere love but sovereignty transferred,
And every painted wedding scene had won
Or lost a kingdom—and the painted word

Of codex was the law, the proof, the seal
Of royal birth, authority, the ground
The genealogy that would reveal
On which a rightful government was found,

Required in the {{c|3460|Mixtec}} world of law.
{{p|Monte Albán}} declined; in the ninth age
Its plazas emptied, and the final thaw
Of {{c|3458|Zapotec}} unity left a stage

Of rival towns across the valley's downs,
And into those fragments the {{c|3460|Mixtec}} came—
Not as conquerors bearing spears and crowns
But as sons-in-law who married their claim

Into the {{c|3458|Zapotec}} high nobles' line,
The interweaving of two ancient bloods
That blurred the boundary and the design
Of who was {{c|3458|Zapotec}} and who the floods

Of {{c|3460|Mixtec}} genealogy had brought—
Two peoples woven into one estate,
Their cultures braided in the thread they wrought
Of gold and turquoise, jade and the ornate

And dazzling metalwork that {{c|3460|Mixtec}} hands
Produced—the finest goldsmiths of the age,
Whose lost-wax castings rivaled any land's
Achievement, every pendant a small stage

Of miniature perfection, every ring
A sermon in the craft of patient fire.
Honor the lords of {{p|Oaxaca}}—the string
Of {{c|3458|Zapotec}} and {{c|3460|Mixtec}} that aspire

To heights above the valley's green embrace,
Who wrote their language first upon the stone,
Who painted on the deerskin every face
Of every ancestor, and kept the bone

And record of their line through all the age—
Who owed no northern lord, no southern king
Their sovereignty, who wrote upon the page
Of their own mountain the remembering

Of everything they were: the self-made crown
{{p|Oaxaca}}'s lords, who built upon the hill
A city that could look upon the brown
And fertile valley with the ancient will

Of those who know exactly who they are,
And where they came from, and whose blood they bear—
The genealogists who read each star
Of ancestry and found their purpose there.


# Chapter 233: Andean Precursors

Before the {{c|3403|Inca}} raised their sun-crowned throne,
Before the royal roads stretched east and west,
Four kingdoms laid the deep foundation stone
On which the {{p|Andes}}' final lord would rest.

For nothing rises from the empty air—
The greatest reign is but the heir of all
The smaller realms that had been planted there
Before it, and the {{c|3403|Inca}}'s golden hall

Was built upon the floor that {{c|3465|Moche}} had poured,
That {{c|3466|Nazca}} drew, that {{c|3467|Wari}} organized,
That {{c|3468|Tiwanaku}}'s temple-builders stored
With sanctity—and each had memorized

A portion of the {{x|Andean}} plan. South—
Along the coastal desert's barren shelf
Where rain has never fallen and the mouth
Of every river is a thread of self-

Contained fertility between the sand—
The {{c|3465|Moche}} rose: and {{p|Sipán}}'s warrior-priest clan,
Whose tombs would yield the richest contraband
Of gold and silver that the {{p|Andes}}' span

Of buried centuries would ever show.
The {{c|3469|Lord of Sipán}} went down to his grave
With gold and turquoise, copper's burnished glow,
A retinue of those who could not save

Themselves from honor: guards, attendants, wives,
Interred beside him, the sacrificial
Cost of their rule, all measured out in lives,
Ultimate credential and official

Proof that {{c|3465|Moche}} lordship did not end at death.
And then the pottery: the stirrup-spout
Ceramic portrait heads that catch the breath
Of every modern eye: each face devout

Or laughing, warlike, sickened, old or young,
Each vessel was a portrait from the life
Of someone real; and there's no other tongue
Of clay has spoken with such lifelike fife

And naturalism: the {{c|3465|Moche}} potter's wheel
Turned out the faces of a people's race
In terra cotta truth, the common weal
Of a whole people, captured in the grace

Of clay: the blind man, runner, and the priest,
The warrior with his captive on a rope,
The couple in their love, wholly released
From modern prudishness; and so, the scope

Of {{c|3465|Moche}} art was all the scope of {{c|3465|Moche}} life;
No human thing escaped the potter's skill.
And south, along the desert, where the knife
Of drought has stripped the land of green, until

No green thing lives upon the pampa's floor,
The {{c|3466|Nazca}} drew their lines. Upon the stone
And gravel of the {{p|Nazca}} plateau's shore
Of nothingness, they traced the overblown

And vast geometry of the divine:
The hummingbird, the spider, monkey's curl,
The condor, its wings spread in endless line
Against the brown—each figure's sprawl and swirl

Invisible from ground level, the scale
So vast that only from the sky above—
Or from the mountains' edge—the full detail
Reveals itself: a spider drawn with love

Across three hundred meters of the plain.
They drew what human eye could never see
From standing height; the desert's dry domain
Has kept it whole: and so the gallery

Was for the gods who gazed; and for the dead
Who walked the lines in spirit; and the act
Of drawing was itself the prayer said—
The making was the rite, and the abstract

And walking meditation through the dark
Of moonlit desert nights, the faithful feet
That traced the monkey's tail and left their mark
Upon the pampa's hot and stony sheet.

The {{c|3466|Nazca}} kept their rite within the dust.
And east, above the desert, on the height
Of cold plateau, the highland's upward thrust,
The altiplano's feast of sky and light

Meets {{p|Titicaca}}'s sacred, lake-deep blue;
There the {{c|3468|Tiwanaku}} built their holy seat.
At thirteen thousand feet, where thin air drew
The breath of pilgrims laboring to meet

Their god; the {{p|Gate of the Sun}} was carved there,
A single block of andesite, the face
Of the great {{c|3470|Staff God}}, weeping, starved, who'd stare
In ecstasy, rays streaming into space

From the divine head like the condor's crown—
The pilgrims came from each {{x|Andean}} vale,
From coast and jungle, and each highland town,
To bring their offerings along the trail

That led to {{p|Tiwanaku}}'s sacred core.
This was no capital of war, no sword;
The {{c|3468|Tiwanaku}} ruled by the temple's lore,
By pilgrimage, by holiness outpoured

That radiated outward, all about,
Out from the sacred center like a hymn:
The peoples of the {{p|Andes}} felt devout
Before the {{c|3470|Staff God}}'s gaze; and the thin, dim,

High altiplano air, oxygen-lean,
Became the proof of spiritual height—
The closer to the sky, the more was seen
Of the divine, the closer to the light.

And {{c|3467|Wari}}, organizer of the way,
Took {{c|3468|Tiwanaku}}'s highland faith, and showed
The state could spread it wider, and could lay
Beyond the pilgrim's reach, the building road:

The {{c|3467|Wari}} built the roads, the terraced hill,
The centers that the {{c|3403|Inca}}, without guilt,
Would take as theirs; the relay-runner's skill,
The storehouses along the way, all built;

The clay of {{c|3403|Inca}} rule was mixed, the frame
Already shaped and set out to be dried;
The {{c|3467|Wari}} built; the {{c|3403|Inca}}, when they came,
But glazed and filled it, and it was supplied.

Honor four mothers of the mountain throne:
{{c|3465|Moche}} the artist, and {{c|3466|Nazca}} the mystic,
And {{c|3468|Tiwanaku}} the holy, and the stone
Of the altar, {{c|3467|Wari}} the logistic

And the pragmatic builder of the road—
Four pillars underneath the {{c|3403|Inca}}'s crown,
Four kingdoms that had each already showed
A portion of the answer: set it down

That nothing rises from the empty air,
That each great golden age stands on the ground,
The labor of the ones who planted there
The seed, and what the later age had found.


# Chapter 234: Mound Cities

The earth itself was architecture: no
Quarry gave stone on {{p|Mississippi}}'s plain,
No limestone cliff, no granite ridge; and so
The land gave soil: the basket-load, the grain

Of clay, the human back, the human hand,
The patient piling, day on day, a slope,
A million baskets, rising from the land,
No pyramid of stone, but earth; the scope

Of labor, muscle, and the weight of loam.
And first, in the {{p|Ohio}} country's hills,
There the {{c|3471|Adena}} built their mounds: the home
Of the dead; but more than graves or landfills:

The mound was what the land itself would yield
When people sculpt the earth to sacred form:
The conic mound, the earthen ring, the field
Enclosed and ritual, kept from the storm,

A meeting-place where living met the dead.
The {{c|3472|Hopewell}} came, two thousand years ago;
Ambition quickened, and the trade-net spread,
Till {{c|3472|Hopewell}} held the {{p|Great Lakes}}' copper glow,

Obsidian from {{p|Yellowstone}}'s far lands,
And mica from the {{p|Appalachian}} floor,
Shells from the {{p|Gulf of Mexico}}'s warm sands,
And silver from {{p|Ontario}}'s cold store:

A net across the continent, that brought
Its luxuries to the {{p|Ohio}}'s graves:
The dead with half a continent's wealth, wrought
Copper cut into falcons; and the waves

Of obsidian knapped to paper-thin,
The mica mirrors, polished to reflect
The ancestor's own face, the link, the kin
Between the living and the circumspect

And watchful dead who lay beneath the mound.
The mounds grew rich; and, on {{p|Ohio}}'s height,
And the {{p|Great Serpent Mound}} upon its ground,
Thirteen hundred feet of earth, all coiled tight,

A serpent swallowing an egg, the sun,
The world: the serpent holds it in its scales;
The serpent swallows it; and it was done
By hand, the earth-work on the wooded vales.

And then, the city: {{p|Cahokia}} rose
Beside the {{p|Mississippi}}, where the stream
Met the {{p|Missouri}}, {{p|Illinois}}: and those
Three rivers' junction, fertile as a dream,

The richest ground in all the northern land,
The soil the corn required; this was the realm
Of maize, from the {{x|Mesoamerican}} hand
Of wisdom, that came north; and at the helm

A surplus such as north had never known;
From that surplus, {{p|Cahokia}}, its crown:
By year one thousand, twenty thousand, grown
Where {{p|East St. Louis}} is, a teeming town,

As great as {{p|London}} in that age, no less;
The largest north of {{p|Mexico}}; and there,
When corn and river and ambition's press
Joined; and a city rose from mud and air:

{{p|Monks Mound}}, the center, a hundred feet high,
Of earth in terraced stages, base as wide
As {{c|1965|Khufu}}'s {{p|Great Pyramid}}; to the sky
The chief's great hall: the hierarchy's pride

Of rank on the {{p|Mississippi}}; the chief
On top, set on the summit, as decree
Of strata, made of earth and height, belief
In rank. And then {{p|Woodhenge}}: the posts, to see

The equinox's light, the solstice dawn,
Red cedar in a ring, the debt of sun
Each season marked; the calendar was drawn
In ceremonies tied to where it spun

Upon the eastern ridge; for those who need
To know the year are cities. And then, when
By twelve hundred, it was over: the seed
Thinned out; the mounds fell silent; and again

The {{c|3473|Mississippian}} way, like a wind,
Scattered to south and east, in chiefdoms, where
At {{p|Moundville}}, {{p|Etowah}}, the mounds were pinned
In smaller towns; the idea was there:

The earth could be the monument they meant:
The soil beneath the feet, when cured and wrought
By human labor and by long intent,
Could rise as high as any stone that's brought.

Honor the mound-builders: {{c|3471|Adena}}'s clay,
And {{c|3472|Hopewell}}, {{c|3473|Mississippian}}; the hands
That carried up a million loads, the way
With no wheel, beast, or iron; yet their lands

Were crowned with monuments that still endure,
Still rise above the {{p|Mississippi}}'s plain,
Still testify, immovable and sure,
That earth itself, when shaped by human pain

And purpose, is the oldest building made:
Not stone imposed upon the ground, but ground
Itself, lifted by will, collective, laid
And made into the sacred, mound by mound,

To hold the dead, to hold the chief above
The people's gaze; the soil beneath the tread
Of those who built with nothing but their love
Of land they stood on, raised it overhead.


# Chapter 235: Desert & Cliffs

The desert teaches everything or none:
Those who came to the {{p|Southwest}}'s burning floor
Of canyon, mesa, silence, and the sun,
'Twixt {{p|Rockies}} and {{p|Sierra Madre}}'s door,

Found little given freely; and the rain
Fell rarely; and the teacher of all things
That live spoke stern: Adapt or perish; plain,
No third way. Three peoples heard what that brings,

And each wrote its own essay of the sand
On how the human mind, when unafraid
Of hardship, turns the wasteland green: the land
First, of the {{c|3440|Hohokam}}, canal and spade,

Where {{p|Phoenix}} spreads its asphalt grid today;
But fifteen hundred years before, they drank
The river: {{c|3440|Hohokam}} had found the way
Of irrigation, bank by channeled bank:

Hundreds of miles of hand-dug channels led
The {{p|Salt}} and {{p|Gila}} Rivers' snowmelt, poured
To corn and squash and cotton in the bed
Of the {{p|Sonoran}} furnace; clay-lined, stored

Against the seepage; and the desert, made
By human labor into garden ground,
Was the {{c|3440|Hohokam}}'s quiet gift; no shade
Of pyramid, of temple; but they found

The desert's harshness pardoned; and they drew
Water from riverbed to field; the yield
Far more than the bare desert ever grew
Fed many, that the dry land could not shield.

They built their ball courts too: the rubber game
That {{p|Mesoamerica}} invented, played
In the {{p|Sonoran}} heat, the drama, same
Of cosmos; thought came northward, in the trade

Along the trails from {{p|Mexico}} to these
Dry lands; for cultures are not islands; hands
Pass on the thought. And second, in the trees,
The {{c|3474|Mogollon}} of the high mountain lands,

Between the desert and {{p|Mogollon Rim}},
The potters, with their {{c|3475|Mimbres}} ware, the rare
Black-on-white, with a line so sure and trim,
So spare, so absolute, so full of care:

Of fish and crane and rabbit, figures, bold
And playful, dancing on the bowl; each one
A scroll of nature, to the essence told:
One line, the wing; one curve, the river's run;

One dot, the eye: the potter knew that all
Could be reduced to essence, perfect, dry,
The living form abstracted. At the fall
Of the potter, the finest bowl, to die,

Was killed: a hole punched through the painted clay
To free the vessel's soul, to go below
The floor beside the dead; and so, the way
Of art for life and death: and both would show

The beauty that outlived the potter's day.
And third, the greatest, in ambition, scale:
The {{c|3476|Ancestral Puebloans}}, and then their way
Of {{p|Chaco Canyon}}'s monumental tale:

In sandy wash below the canyon's stage
Of rock and sage, the {{c|3477|Puebloans}} would then show
What gathered human will could build, the gauge
Of the unlikely: houses, row on row,

{{p|Pueblo Bonito}}, with six hundred rooms,
Four stories, fitted stone, the largest host
In {{p|North America}}; and as it looms,
{{p|Chaco}}, no town, but something else: a post

Of ceremony, gathering, a crown
Of roads that met upon the canyon's floor:
The roads ran straight, thirty feet wide, cut down
Through mesa-tops, for ritual, and more:

Not roads for trade, but for the sacred way,
Like {{c|3466|Nazca}} lines laid out upon the land,
Aligned to solstice, equinox's day,
The pilgrims' paths, as drawn by sacred hand.

{{p|Mesa Verde}}: cliff-dwellings carved below
The sandstone overhangs, the rooms of stone
Where rock withheld the rain's destructive flow,
Sheltered beneath the canyon wall alone:

{{p|Cliff Palace}}, with a hundred fifty rooms,
Hung in the rock-face like a nest, and reached
By handholds, safe from summer's sun that dooms,
Defensible, from western winds unbreached,

Engineered comfort in the cliff's embrace.
The rains grew less; the thirteenth age brought drought
That settled like a judgment on the place:
The water failed; the corn's demand ran out

Beyond the sky's gift; the arithmetic
Of land that cannot feed its people; so
South, east they went; the cities emptied quick;
The heirs went to the {{p|Rio Grande}}, below,

Bearing what cliff and canyon had bestowed:
The kiva's sacred roundness underground,
The corn-dance and the rain-prayer, which was owed
To ancestors; the people, wise and sound,

Who built upon the hardest earth, and when
Its patience ended, did not die, but sought
Rebirth in gentler land. Honor the men
And women of the desert, all they wrought:

The {{c|3440|Hohokam}}, {{c|3474|Mogollon}}, {{c|3439|Puebloan}}: they
Made of the desert no mere empty space,
But home; and what they made, still to this day,
Is written on the canyon's weathered face:

The hardest land, and from it all their art;
The scarcest water, kept with deepest care;
The people of the margins, and the heart
Of all their making in the dry, clear air.

# Chapter 237: Eskimo-Aleut Expansion

A third migration, and the last: the gate
Of {{p|Beringia}} was drowned, the broad plain
Submerged beneath the melted glaciers' weight
And risen sea; but on the {{p|Arctic}}'s main,

The passage lay open to those who had
The means and will: the frozen ocean's road
Was not a wall; and from {{p|Alaska}}, clad
In furs, a thousand years ago, there strode

The {{c|147|Thule}}, across the world's own top; its night
Of {{p|Arctic}} winter held no fear for those
More than the warm savanna's noonday light
For warmer folk; they mastered, as one knows,

The kayak: skin and bone, the single seat
Of walrus-hide on driftwood frame; the boat
Most intimate, the hunter wore complete,
Sealed by a drawstring skirt about the throat,

The man and vessel one; and when the wave
Rolled the kayak over, he did not drown,
But, as the water wished, would so behave,
Roll back up: the {{c|3486|Eskimo}} roll, the crown

Of trust between the body and the craft.
The umiak: the open boat, the freight
Of women and the camp, broad, fore and aft,
Of tents and tools and children, and the weight

Of cached provisions; where the kayak was
The sword, the umiak the shield, the load,
And stable platform. And the sled, because
Of the {{c|147|Thule}} and their dogs, the winter road

Across the sea-ice, in the freezing fog,
The driver on the runners, and the team
Of huskies bred to pull: the human, dog,
Ten thousand years of taming, its extreme

And final test; without the dog, no home
Was possible: the living engine. And
The igloo: from the snow itself, a dome,
The very thing that kills, turned shelter's hand;

The frozen enemy, turned warm with breath,
Insulation in the substance of cold:
Some forty blocks of wind-packed snow, from death
A shelter, spiraled inward, and, behold,

A house built swiftly, that in spring would melt
And leaves no scar upon the land; the hand
Of ingenuity laid flat, that felt
The ice, and said: Here too; here, I will stand,

Even in coldest place, the killing dark,
I'll make a home. And then the whale, the vast
Bowhead, leviathan, the {{c|147|Thule}}'s ark
Of plenty: one could feed a village, cast

Through a whole season; blubber, rendered oil
For lamps that burned the polar night; the bone
That framed the house when driftwood failed the toil;
The baleen's springy plate, as tool, alone

Served for the metal that they lacked: the whale
Was the estate on which the pact was made
With frozen sea. From the umiak's rail,
Six hunters, sinew line, the toggling blade

Of the harpoon that turns beneath the mass
Of blubber, holds; the sealskin float paid out
By patient hunter on the water's glass,
The whale dives, and tires, and dives, without

Its sleep and blood, till the great beast succumbs,
And one whale's body feeds the waiting shore.
East from {{p|Alaska}}, as the season comes,
The {{c|147|Thule}} opened the {{p|Arctic}}, door on door:

{{p|Beaufort Sea}}, the {{p|Archipelago}}'s maze
Of islands; {{p|Hudson Bay}}'s enormous bound;
The {{p|Labrador}} coast's fog, and the fjords' haze
Of {{p|Greenland}}'s west; in four centuries, crowned

The continent, three thousand miles of ice;
And where they settled, the {{c|3487|Dorset}} went down:
The {{c|3487|Dorset}}, there before, whose quiet price
Of lonelier tradition held the crown

Of {{p|Arctic}} for a thousand years: no dog,
No bow, no open boat; their mastery
Was small, lone hunter's skill; and in the fog
Of {{c|147|Thule}}'s dogs and bows and crews at sea,

The {{c|3487|Dorset}} froze to margins and to lore
Of the {{c|3488|Inuit}}, who still call them {{c|3489|Tunit}}:
Gentle giants, strong, the old ones, before,
Who could not keep the new world's pace, or fit,

And vanished from the ice. Honor the last
Great wave, the {{c|147|Thule}}, who paid the {{p|Arctic}}'s price,
Accepted its impossible, and cast
The kayak's shell, trained dogs, spiraled the ice

To dome, chased whale through swell, and made a home
Of frozen dark: the proof that there's no place
So hostile on this planet that the loam
Of human mind and will cannot find space

To live there: not to merely just survive,
But prosper, hunt, and sing, and pray, and raise
The children, keep the ancestors alive
In stories: those who looked out on the glaze

Of ice, and did not turn away, but said:
This too can be traversed; this too, our land;
And we will learn. And so the {{c|147|Thule}} would spread
Across the world's own crown, with sled and hand.


# Chapter 238: The Redeemer

In each land comes a figure, one who bears
The weight of heaven's message on his back,
Who walks among the broken and repairs
The covenant the people let go slack,

Who teaches, and who suffers, and who goes
Away, who promises to come again;
And then the people, left behind, compose
The scripture of departure; and the pain

Of absence is the engine of the faith.
Two figures now, two continents apart,
Two centuries apart; and yet the wraith
Of one same archetype beats at the heart

Of both: the {{c|3490|Redeemer}}, he who descends,
Who teaches peace, who builds the law, who goes
Before the mission ends; whose leaving lends
The deepest awe the people ever knows.

In {{p|Tula}}, which the {{c|3464|Toltec}} would erect,
In central {{p|Mexico}}; and there, below,
Where warrior-columns, grim, stand to protect
The legend, over history, would go:

A man: {{c|3491|Ce Acatl Topiltzin}}, born
On {{x|One Reed}}'s day, the priest-king, in the cause
Of the {{c|3492|Feathered Serpent}}: wind, breath, the morn
Star, {{c|3493|Quetzalcoatl}}, whose rising and whose pause

As evening star, meant death and life in one
Arc of the sky; who wore the quetzal's green
Feathers in the temple's dark; as the sun,
He bore the name of culture's gift, the sheen:

The corn, the calendar, the arts of peace,
The writing and the wisdom of the scribe;
The {{c|3464|Toltec}} culture's gift, and its release
Of human culture from war's bloody tribe.

{{c|3494|Topiltzin}} taught that human sacrifice
Was an abomination; that the floor
Be clean of the obsidian's device;
That butterflies and flowers, and no more,

Were offerings the {{c|3492|Feathered Serpent}} sought;
The human and divine were bound, their prayer
Of beauty, not of blood. For what he taught,
{{c|3495|Tezcatlipoca}}'s priesthood, standing there,

Reaching for the one altar, with the glass
That smoked, its god's own face, would not allow
The flowers; and they would not let them pass,
But plotted on the priest; and they, somehow,

Brought him a mirror: his own face, diseased
And old; the terror of his mortal fate
Undid him; and with pulque made drunk, they seized
His shame, and barred him {{p|Tula}}'s throne and state.

He walked east, the sea his last desire,
And at the {{p|Gulf}}'s edge, weeping and bereft,
Set himself free: burned upon a pyre
His heart became the morning star; and left,

He sailed away, the raft's light growing dim
Upon the eastern ocean's rim; and both
Are so: he promised to return; of him
The age would come again; and, by that oath,

The world would learn of flowers and not blood.
Then north, a thousand miles, two ages on,
A figure came across the water's flood:
The {{c|3498|Peacemaker}}, remembered; and, as dawn,

The {{c|3497|Haudenosaunee}} know him: the root
Of their own constitution; in the light
Of longhouse embers, sacred, resolute,
He came across {{p|Lake Ontario}}'s night,

From the north shore, a stranger, who forsook
His {{c|3499|Huron}} people; and he found the five
At war: the {{c|3500|Seneca}}, {{c|3501|Cayuga}} shook,
{{c|3502|Onondaga}}, {{c|3503|Oneida}}, {{c|3504|Mohawk}}; strive

Not against strangers, but each other: score
On score of blood-feud; for a brother, son
Killed to avenge; revenge's endless war
Ate nations from within; and he, the one,

The {{c|3498|Peacemaker}}, he offered grief's release:
The {{x|Great Law}}: the {{c|3505|Great Law of Peace}}, the law,
{{x|Gayanashagowa}}, the great league of peace:
Five nations by one oath, beneath the awe

Of one roof: each its hearth and its domain;
But all the five-fold matters, as they fell,
Were settled at {{p|Onondaga}}, the main
And central flame, where fifty sachems dwell,

The royaneh, deliberating the law:
The path of the unanimous; the right
Of the clan mothers, who could so withdraw
A chief who ruled unwisely; and the might

Of women over men's ambition; both
Held voice; and war required consent of all.
He planted the {{c|3506|Great White Pine}}, there, the oath,
The {{x|Tree of Peace}}, and, under it so tall,

The five-fire longhouse, laid out east to west:
The {{c|3504|Mohawk}}, keeper of the eastern door;
And {{c|3500|Seneca}}, the west; the center, best,
{{c|3502|Onondaga}}, the keeper of the lore

Of law; beneath the roots, the hatchet, sunk,
The war-club, buried, that no hand should take;
The arrow snapped; beneath the pine's great trunk,
The nations, freed, the blood-feud's chain would break.

And then the {{c|3498|Peacemaker}} departed. Like
{{c|3494|Topiltzin}}, the refrain the same: the one
Who, broken-hearted or fulfilled, will strike
West, or into the sky, his teaching done,

And leaves the law and the behest to keep
It burning, as a light upon the coast;
The people choose the teaching, or the deep
Violence they were asked to lose, its ghost.

Two {{c|3507|Redeemers}}: one of feathers, one pine;
One of the pyramid, one of the floor
Of longhouse; one, the morning star's design;
One planted the tree, and closed the war's door;

The pattern of the world: the teacher came
And healed; his banner, raised, and then withdrawn;
And what he left behind is still the same:
The covenant he made, and peace lives on

Where it is kept, from day to day; the law
He gave is kept by all the will of those
Who say, We will keep it. Honor, with awe,
The {{c|3490|Redeemer}}, who carries heaven's throes,

Its aching weight, and sets it down before
The dreamer and the warrior; says: create
A world of flowers, not blood; law, not war;
The planted tree, whose branches, in their state

Of peace, shelter the nations, who lay down
Their weapons, choose the harder path: the pact
Of the healed and shared, the never-grasped crown
Of might; and this is the {{c|3490|Redeemer}}'s act,

His testament: I came, I taught, I leave;
And what you build without me is the proof
Of whether you believed. Now you must weave
The peace yourselves, beneath the planted roof

Of the tree, by the law I gave; the star
Of morning rises, and the pine will grow;
The longhouse warmth is kept for those who are
True to the teaching; choose the flower, so,

Not the blade; choose the council, not the war;
Choose the covenant the {{c|3490|Redeemer}} made,
And he will come again; but not before
You keep the peace without him, unafraid.


# Chapter 239: The Fall of Tenochtitlan

The city rose out of the lake, a prayer
Answered in stone and floating garden-ground:
{{p|Tenochtitlan}}, jewel beyond compare,
Where {{c|3508|Huitzilopochtli}}'s children, crowned,

Did not resist the beauty, but had built
It into causeways on the water, arched,
And aqueducts whose arches split the gilt
Of morning; each quarter hummed and marched

With trade, a hundred thousand lives: the plume,
The feather, obsidian blade, the bean
Of chocolate, and the cotton of the loom;
Two hundred thousand souls lived there, between

Its walls, more than in any {{p|Europe}}'s town
At that same age; from {{p|Chapultepec}}'s spring,
Clean water fed the fountains coming down
To plazas where the priests and merchants' ring

Went on, where architects would plan the feasts
Of carving: serpent, eagle, sun; and there
The skull-rack's grim accounting; for the priests'
City was not undone by squeamish care:

Tribute of {{c|3509|Triple Alliance}}'s wars
Fed the realm's hunger and the altar's need;
And {{c|3510|Moctezuma}} ruled from polished floors,
The absolute and guaranteed, decreed

Lord of the {{c|3511|Mexica}}; {{p|Texcoco}} and
{{p|Tlacopan}} beside him, three cities' chain
In one alliance; and the markets, spanned
At {{p|Tlatelolco}}, wider than the main

Of {{p|Salamanca}}; sixty thousand fed
Its dance of trade: the jaguar pelt, the plume
Of quetzal, gold and jade and turquoise spread,
Vanilla, rubber, copal, and the loom,

The lapidary's and the goldsmith's art;
The {{c|3512|Spaniards}}, later, swore they'd never seen
In any {{p|Europe}} market such a mart;
And the chinampas, floating gardens, green

On woven reed upon the lake, where grew
The maize and squash and amaranth, the soil
So engineered, the most productive, through
The hemisphere; and so their keepers' toil

Fed a realm. Then, the sails: fifteen-nineteen;
{{c|3513|Hernán Cortés}} then stepped ashore, with his horse;
And the {{c|3514|Totonacs}} told, as they had seen,
The {{c|3511|Mexica}}'s ways, gave the lever's force:

The tributary peoples, ground beneath
The {{c|3509|Triple Alliance}}'s heavy rack
Of flower-war and tax, would so bequeath
Their hatred to the stranger; and the crack

Was this: {{c|3496|Cortés}} did not conquer it alone;
He broke the {{c|3511|Mexica}} with their own foes,
Alliance turned against itself, the bone
Of realms. Then {{c|3510|Moctezuma}}, as signs rose,

Would hesitate: was this {{c|3493|Quetzalcoatl}}, come
Back from the eastern sea, as was foretold,
The {{c|3492|Feathered Serpent}}? Or an envoy, from
Some distant lord? And while he weighed, and told

The omens, signs, {{c|3496|Cortés}} advanced, and he laid
The {{c|3401|European}} purpose through the lines
Of {{c|3511|Mexica}} diplomacy, a blade
Through silk. They met on causeway, by the shrines:

{{c|3510|Moctezuma}} in his jade and in gold,
The feathered headdress, all the grand display;
And {{c|3496|Cortés}}, in rusted iron, grim and cold,
Unwashed, his handful, weak in number; they

Were armored in the certainty that {{c|472|God}}
And gold were one pursuit. Then, the slaughter;
And on the {{c|3515|Noche Triste}}, through the causeways trod
The {{c|3516|Spanish}}, driven bloody through water;

But they returned; they always would return,
With the {{c|3517|Tlaxcalan}} allies, who'd destroy
Their ancient foes, the {{c|3511|Mexica}}; he'd learn
The lesson realms all fear, the subject's ploy:

The subjects' hate is the invader's gift.
And an ally too small for eye to see,
{{c|3518|Cocoliztli}}, plague, terrible and swift,
Ran on before {{c|3516|Spanish}} boots: smallpox, free,

Silent, needing no sword; it moved along
The causeways, markets, palace, and the hut
Of the gourd-worker, through the weak and strong;
And forty in a hundred fell, were cut,

The {{c|3511|Mexica}}, before a single pike
Had breached the citadel. The siege then came:
Eighty days of starvation, thirst, the strike
Of cannonade; the causeways cut, the same

Waters held by brigantines he made
From lakeshore wood; {{c|3519|Cuauhtémoc}}, the last
Tlatoani, fought on his knees, and stayed
In rubble, till the city, boneyard, passed

Into surrender's silence: no more drums
From the great temple's height, no market's sound,
No laughter in the plazas; and there comes
Colonial night. In the ruin, found,

A scribe, {{c|3520|Mormon}}, the keeper of the trust
Of record, and gathered memory's hope:
The painted books, the codices from dust
Of kings, the genealogies, the rope

Of knotted calendar, the count of days,
The feasts, the names of gods, from ages past;
And fled north to the mount, the desert's maze,
Hid from the conqueror, buried to last.

The record slept beneath the earth, a seed,
A stratagem of rebirth, long delayed.
Honor {{p|Tenochtitlan}}, whose roads lead
Across the world the sword would break and fade,

But could not make the memory unmanned:
The chinampas, and all the chocolate trade,
The serpent and knife, the calendar stand
Of stone, the jade-green mask; all of it laid

In soil, and waiting for the day the hoard
Of the old record-keeper's found once more,
And the long toil of forgetting's restored
To light; the city rises, as before;

And so the memory no conquest's might
Could ever make undone, returns to dwell
In its old home, the city of the light
Upon the lake, alive in those who tell.

And north to {{p|Chinchaysuyu}}'s coastal heat,
South, {{p|Collasuyu}}'s {{p|Altiplano}} clan,

# Chapter 250: The Civilized Trap

They did all that was asked of them; and still
They lost their land. Five nations of the south:
The {{c|3636|Cherokee}}, the {{c|3637|Choctaw}}, with the will
Of {{c|3638|Chickasaw}}, and from the river's mouth

The {{c|3639|Creek}}, whom some named {{c|3640|Muscogee}}; the last,
The {{c|3641|Seminole}}: whose law and custom stood
So like the settlers' own, the word was cast:
Their neighbors named them "civilized," and good.

"Civilized" meant: you look so much like us
We nearly see you. And they shaped, in trust,
Their lives to fit the conqueror, and thus
Found out the reckoning a lie, and dust.

The {{c|3636|Cherokee}} led first. In mountain lands
Where the blue ridges meet the southern sky,
Where laurel slopes feed rivers, in their hands
The country lay since forests first grew high;

And now they took the {{c|3401|European}} way,
And from the conqueror the poorest gift,
His alphabet, and made it, in their day,
A thing he never dreamed, a world's whole shift.

Of {{c|3244|English}} marks, but grasped the thing they frame:

In {{c|3636|Cherokee}} could be set down as well
As {{c|3244|English}} could. He took no borrowed word;
At night he heard the syllables' own spell:

A sign. From {{c|3244|English}} some he took the form,
Forgotten there, and gave it sounds anew:
So D was "a," and R was "e"; the norm

Alone; it asked no years of school; in weeks
It taught what {{c|3244|English}} took in years; and hearts
Learned letters, and the reading nation speaks

In eighteen twenty-eight, a paper bold
Set by {{c|3644|Elias Boudinot}}, whose prose
Gave voice, in their own script, to what they hold:

A tongue that wrote itself in its own hand,
By one man's genius in the silence made,
A thing no other nation of the land
Had done. And they built government, and laid

In eighteen twenty-seven the whole frame
Of a constitution: three branches set
Like the republic's own: a chief, the same,
A senate, court, and written code; and yet

Their capital at {{p|New Echota}} stood,
Where council sat and argued in the script
{{c|3642|Sequoyah}} gave them, as republics would,
Among republics, lawful and equipped.

The {{c|3637|Choctaw}} built their schools; the {{c|3638|Chickasaw}}
Raised farms to match the settlers' own; the {{c|3639|Creek}}
Rebuilt after the {{x|Red Stick War}}, the law
Of {{c|3645|Andrew Jackson}}'s first campaign: the weak

And strong alike, the allied {{c|3639|Creek}} who'd sought
For peace, and those who fought him, he made pay:
At {{p|Fort Jackson}}, by the {{x|Treaty}}, he caught
Some twenty-three million acres away.

The {{c|3641|Seminole}} in {{p|Florida}}'s green maze
Of swamp and hammock held its own. And now
The bitter center lies: in all their ways
They did all that was asked. They took the plow;

They built in {{c|3401|European}} style; they let
The church bell sound across their towns; they wrote
Their constitutions, printed their gazette,
Schooled children, and they wore the settler's coat;

And it was not enough; it never would
Be so. The word "civilized" was a lock
That had no key. The verdict, understood,
Had passed from court to ledger, and the stock

Was land, worth more than people on it. Gold
Was found in {{p|Georgia}}, on {{c|3636|Cherokee}} ground;
The settlers' hunger would not be controlled
By constitution or by paper bound.

The more the {{c|3636|Cherokee}} grew like their foe,
The more the settlers felt the threat they made:
A people who disproved, by what they'd show,
The tale of race; if they could build, and trade,

And write a constitution, and could stand
Themselves by written law, then what excuse
Remained for their removal? What of land
Wasted on savages, the old abuse

Of the wandering brute who knew no plow?
The {{c|3636|Cherokee}} disproved the lie, and bore
A greater hate than warriors knew; and how?
They held the mirror up, the face it wore;

The nation saw itself and could not bear
The image; what it owed its law, the price,
Was a truth far too thick to swallow there;
It would not pay; the {{c|3636|Cherokee}} paid twice.

So honor {{c|3642|Sequoyah}}, who gave letters
To his own people, with no borrowed thread,
A syllabary that breaks the fetters
Of silence on the voice; and the page spread

Not as the settlers' only. Honor too
The {{c|3636|Cherokee}} {{x|Phoenix}}, whose defiance
In print was the most civilized and true
Reproof one nation gave; its reliance

On ink and paper proved that "savage" lied,
And "civilized" was just the settler's jest.
They built what they were told to build, and tried
To be what they were told; and, for the rest,

For all the promises that they fulfilled,
Their wage was the removal's beating drum:
The towns and farms and schools that they would build
Were counted nothing, and their voice struck dumb.


# Chapter 251: The Last Confederacies

One more time came the vision, the old flame
Of unity that {{c|3583|Pontiac}} once lit:
Together, or not at all: and the claim
Came now the greatest dreamer, born to it:

{{c|3646|Tecumseh}}, called the {{c|3647|Shooting Star}}, whose speech
Was simple, true, and came too late: "The land
Belongs to all; it lies beyond the reach
Of any chief to sell it by his hand.

"Sell, if you will, the air, the rain, the stream;
But not the ground on which we pray; it held
The bones of those who came before, the dream
Of those to come; no single name has spelled

"Away the claim of all who walk the earth."
He walked from the {{p|Great Lakes}} to the south shore,
From {{c|3620|Shawnee}} towns, {{p|Ohio}}'s place of birth,
To {{c|3639|Creek}} and {{c|3637|Choctaw}}, {{p|Florida}}'s green door,

He bore the word: unite or die; one league
Of all the nations, east and west, unbound
To any {{c|3401|European}} king's intrigue;
{{c|3583|Pontiac}}'s flame reborn on greater ground:

Not war alone, on {{c|3581|British}} or the rest,
{{c|3404|Americans}}, but a refusal, whole,
To set a price on earth: the land possessed
In common trust, a people's common soul.

And then his brother, {{c|3648|Tenskwatawa}}, rose,
The {{c|2776|Prophet}}, from the dust of drink's despair,
A broken man, remade by vision's throes:
He died, he said, and came back from the air

Where the {{c|1470|Master}} of Life had shown him how
The whole world could be healed: give up the drink,
The {{c|3401|European}} goods, the metal's vow,
Return to the {{c|3591|Creator}}'s ways, the link

Of corn and deer, of prayer in the tongue
{{c|3591|Creator}} knew. At {{p|Tippecanoe}} there grew,
At {{p|Prophetstown}}, of wood and bark, among
The gathered nations, a capital new,

Where tribal quarrel melted into prayer,
And warriors of a dozen nations came
To hear the {{c|2776|Prophet}} speak and to prepare
The world made new. {{c|3646|Tecumseh}}, in his aim,

Went south; and while he traveled, on the town
Marched {{c|3649|William Henry Harrison}}, afraid
Of nations joined more than of any crown;
At dawn, November seventh, came the raid

In eighteen-eleven; the {{c|2776|Prophet}} fought
Against his brother's strict command; he swore
The bullets would not harm them; magic, sought
In vain: the capital was lost; the war

Of lead ran its arithmetic: they burned
{{p|Prophetstown}} and its stores; the {{c|2776|Prophet}}'s cry
Of shields from bullets, overturned. Returned,
{{c|3646|Tecumseh}} found but ashes, by and by;

He did not break. The {{x|War of Eighteen-Twelve}}
Became his thread: the {{c|3581|British}} needed men
In the {{p|Great Lakes}} and west; and he, to delve
Their need, gave what no deal could buy; and then

At {{p|Detroit}} he bluffed {{c|3650|Hull}}: the very same
His warriors, marching past the window thrice
In different dress; and {{c|3650|Hull}}, in fear and shame,
Gave up {{p|Detroit}} to half his strength, the price

Of theater in war. And yet the due
Of such a friend the {{c|3581|British}} did not pay:
At the {{p|Thames}}, {{p|Ontario}}, there withdrew
{{c|3651|Procter}} and all his horse, and rode away,

And left {{c|3646|Tecumseh}} and his warriors lone
Against the charge of {{c|3652|Harrison}}; the swamp
Was his last stand, where horsemen's waves were thrown,
And musket-roar the drumbeat and the stomp

Of a lost hope; and there {{c|3646|Tecumseh}} fell,
October, eighteen-thirteen; and around
His body closed the wood; and none could tell
The hollow where his warriors made his mound;

They hid him from {{c|3404|Americans}} who'd strip
His skin for keepsakes. And the dream was torn,
But not yet cut; then rose {{c|3653|Black Hawk}}, whose grip
Held to the homeland where the {{c|3655|Sauk}} were born:

{{c|3654|Ma-ka-tai-me-she-kia-kiak}}, the war
Chief of the {{c|3655|Sauk}}, in eighteen thirty-two,
Crossed back the {{p|Mississippi}} east once more,
Home to {{p|Saukenuk}}, which a treaty, through

A drunken chief's false mark in eighteen-four,
Had signed away, with no council's consent;
He crossed with women, children, by the score,
The hungry, wanting only, as they went,

To plant their corn on their ancestral ground.
The {{c|3404|Americans}} called it war; the band,
Starved, lonely, through {{p|Wisconsin}} hunted, hound
By hound, until at {{p|Bad Axe}}, where the land

Met water, steamboat {{x|Warrior}} and the line
Of guns cut down the women as they tried
To swim across; the white flag, as a sign
Of their surrender, spurned; the children died

And floated in the current; and the flow
Of {{p|Mississippi}} reddened. Farther down,
The {{c|3641|Seminole}} refused. Three wars, and slow,
From eighteen-seventeen, in swamp, not town,

To eighteen-fifty-eight: the longest stand,
{{c|3561|Indigenous}} resistance, in the maze
Of {{p|Florida}}'s swamps, in the saw-grass land,
{{c|3656|Osceola}}, no chief, but in his ways

The very soul of the resistance, fought
The {{c|3484|American}} army till it bent;
They seized him under truce; by treason caught,
Which {{c|3657|Jesup}} called the diplomat's intent.

{{c|3656|Osceola}} died in a prison. Yet
The {{c|3641|Seminole}} fought on, until the claim:
The nation spent some forty million, set
With fifteen hundred lives, to move the name

Of fewer than four thousand souls; and some
Would never yield; within the {{p|Everglades}},
In hammock and in cypress, still they come
And go, unconquered, where the saw-grass fades,

Whose home removal never changed. So praise
{{c|3646|Tecumseh}}, called the {{c|3647|Shooting Star}}, whose light
Crossed the whole continent, and in our days
Each no to selling land still burns it bright.

The earth has spoken: land is not for sale;
The covenant unbroken. Honor, too,
{{c|3653|Black Hawk}}, who crossed the river home, the trail
Paid in blood for the right to plant, and through

The sky his fathers knew, to rest. And weep
For {{p|Bad Axe}}, where the children died; and praise
{{c|3656|Osceola}}, seized under truce, whose deep,
Unconquered {{c|3641|Seminole}} still walk the ways

Of {{p|Everglades}}, no noose about their throats.
The last confederacies burned and fell
And burned again; but still the embers' notes
Glow on; and what {{c|3646|Tecumseh}} learned, they tell:

No single nation stands alone; the land
Belongs to all; the bones beneath the stone
Are covenant no treaty's signing hand
Can break; the {{c|3647|Shooting Star}} burns on, its own.


# Chapter 252: The Unsealed Book

Now seal the book. No: it was never sealed;
The book was always open, set in grain,
In corn and council flame, and so revealed
In tongues that the {{c|3591|Creator}} gave the plain:

Three hundred families of speech, a throng
Of languages that named the world, the air
Of a whole hemisphere; and in their song
The names of {{c|472|God}}, that none need render there.

The rivers spoke them, and the mountains held
Them in their granite; prayer of ages sprang
From sacred springs, ten thousand years; unspelled,
This was the book; this always was. It rang

Through all the ages; then, beside that book,
In one year, then, the record would draw near:
a man in {{p|Palmyra}}, {{p|New York}}, would look
into a stone and see the plates appear:

the golden plates that {{c|3661|Moroni}} had sealed
within a hill, the record of a son
of {{p|Israel}} who crossed the sea, revealed
in "{{x|Reformed Egyptian}}," and so begun

with {{c|3402|Lehi}}, from {{p|Jerusalem}}, who came,
and {{c|3659|Nephi}}, who would sail across the sea:
the first book that would give this land a name,
its prophets, kings, its {{c|1497|Christ}}, its living tree.

{{c|1497|Christ}} came to Bountiful, and taught, and healed,
and blessed their children: the Redeemer's day
that this book also keeps, its promise sealed
to come again, and not to pass away.

It named the peoples {{c|3658|Lamanites}}: the rites
of Laman's line, and darkened for the sin
their fathers did; and here the quarrel bites:
whose fathers were they? Jacob's, or the kin

who walked from {{p|Beringia}}? The gold plates
said {{c|3402|Lehi}}; and the land said ice and coast;
and the one house of the dead, at its gates,
takes both: the tree has room for every ghost.

In March of eighteen-thirty {{c|3660|Joseph Smith}}
set out the book {{c|3661|Moroni}} had concealed,
and his eleven witnesses, forthwith,
signed that they saw the plates the hill revealed.

In that same year, eighteen-thirty, the act
Of Congress came that mattered most: the plan,
The {{x|Indian Removal Act}}, signed, backed
By {{c|3645|Andrew Jackson}}, {{c|3662|Sharp Knife}}: so the man

The {{c|3636|Cherokee}} named him; he'd devoured
The {{c|3639|Creek}} homeland, the grim general, who
Took {{p|Florida}}, over treaties towered;
Now president, with the {{c|3626|Republic}}'s view

Of westward hunger, made one policy:
Remove them all. Each nation to the east
Of the {{p|Mississippi}}, each {{c|3636|Cherokee}}
Farm with its constitution, press, and feast

Of learning; {{c|3637|Choctaw}} rule; each {{c|3638|Chickasaw}}
Town with elected council; {{c|3639|Creek}} who'd made
His home again since {{c|3663|Jackson}}'s war: the law,
Move west. The east, the settlers said, was laid

By {{c|472|God}} for them, a {{p|Promised Land}} aside
For the {{c|3484|American}} republic's hand;
The darker peoples, exiled, were to ride
Beyond the {{p|Mississippi}}, to a land

Where the republic promised them once more
A home "as long as grass shall grow," the same
Words spoken at each treaty there before,
The vow, as soon as made, broken in shame.

{{c|3664|John Ross}}, chief of the {{c|3636|Cherokee}}, by blood
One-eighth, but theirs by measure of the day,
Fought as they fought since {{c|3642|Sequoyah}}: the flood
Of law and language, written word; the way

To the {{x|Supreme Court}}: they were a nation;
No state could lay its laws upon the land
By treaty; and {{p|Georgia}}'s legislation
To seize their soil and rule them, void and banned.

And {{c|3665|John Marshall}} ruled; the {{c|3666|Chief Justice}} found
In {{c|3667|Worcester}} versus {{p|Georgia}} that the state
Had no authority upon the ground
Of a nation sovereign by treaty's weight.

The highest court was clear. And {{c|3663|Jackson}} laughed:
"{{c|3665|John Marshall}} has made his own decision;
Now let him enforce it." Thus the statecraft
Of the republic's law and its vision

Shrank to a bully's sneer: the law cast down
By the elected king; for what was awe
Of {{x|Constitution}} to the gold, the crown
Of {{c|3636|Cherokee}} hills, and the settler's maw?

The {{x|Court}} spoke; and the {{c|3668|President}} refused;
In that refusal died the founders' vow
That law, not might, would rule; the words were used
Only for some: the {{c|3636|Cherokee}} learned how,

As had the {{c|3497|Haudenosaunee}}, the wise
{{c|3628|Little Turtle}}, what the whole land had learned:
The settler's law protects the settler's prize;
And first the {{c|3637|Choctaw}}, in the winter, turned

West, eighteen-thirty-one, through ice and snow,
Caravans of the dispossessed; the sun
Hid weeks in cloud; the dead were left below
Left unburied on the frozen road; and one

{{c|3637|Choctaw}} chief, looking back, gave it its name:
"A trail of tears and death." The {{c|3639|Creek}} went too;
The {{c|3638|Chickasaw}} were sold into the same;
Last, {{c|3636|Cherokee}}, in thirty-eight; and through

A cold and calculated roundup came
The soldiers to each farm and cabin door,
And herded families at bayonet's aim
Into the stockades, onto filthy floor

Of camps that penned them, and they could bring naught;
The homes they built, the orchards, and the press,
The {{c|3636|Cherokee}} {{x|Phoenix}}, and all they wrought
In schools and courts, the settlers would possess,

Who rushed like ghouls in packs, before the last
{{c|3636|Cherokee}} was gone. Sixteen thousand went;
Four thousand died; the children first, then fast
The old; and mothers carried, as they bent,

Their dead, until the tide of grief became
The only text that they could say aloud.
And now, the same year, side by side, the same:
Two peoples walked, one road, one weeping crowd:

In eighteen-thirty, as the act was signed,
the book came out; and in the year of tears,
in eighteen-thirty-eight, the same state's mind
drove out the Saints, from Missouri's frontiers.

Its governor signed Order Forty-Four:
the Saints must leave the state, or else be slain.
The same year, same republic, the same door:
the {{c|3636|Cherokee}}, the Saints, went west in pain.

The book did not move them. It was the land,
the farms the state had wanted, and the gold;
the book had sent four preachers out to stand
with Delaware and Shawnee, and they told

the chiefs: your fathers were the prophets' line,
your dead are kings and seers in what we read;
the {{c|3402|Lehi}} of your house is also mine.
That was the quarrel: who would hold the dead.

The book had called their skin's darkness a curse,
and they would turn "white and delightsome" when
redeemed; the Church would later change the verse
to "pure," in nineteen eighty-one, again.

It was one house's quarrel over skin,
and over fathers, and who keeps the name:
the {{c|3673|Mormons}} and the {{c|3658|Lamanites}} are kin,
one people, and one altar, and one flame.

And so this book here in your hands lays bare
the plates again, and opens them up wide:
the long walk from {{p|Beringia}}, laid there,
the corn, the mounds, the cities; and beside

them, {{c|1497|Christ}} in Bountiful and {{c|3402|Lehi}}'s sail,
the record of ten thousand years of prayer,
the quipu knots, the codex, and the tale
of every people who had once lived there.

This is the book that always stays unsealed,
the record that was never lost, the names
of every nation, every saint, revealed
to {{c|3660|Joseph Smith}} or to the council flames.

Here ends the {{x|Book}} of {{c|3402|Lehi}}, his and theirs:
the {{c|3402|Lehi}} of the plates, and of the land,
whose history was carved in mounds and prayers,
whose scripture is the earth on which they stand.

A hemisphere that never lay there blank
for any pen; a full and holy stage
where ten thousand years of prayer, rank on rank,
had made it holy, age on holy age.

The {{x|Trail of Tears}} was Jackson's; and the hate,
the {{x|Indian Removal Act}}, his hand.
But the peoples endure, and keep their gate;
the record is intact. The peoples stand.

This is that record. Keep in mind each name:
{{c|3646|Tecumseh}}, {{c|3606|Tupac Amaru}}; recall
{{c|3642|Sequoyah}}, and the council's ancient flame
From {{p|Cusco}} up to {{p|Manitou}}; and all

The {{c|3587|Guarani}} violins, the Great {{x|Law}}
Of the {{c|3497|Haudenosaunee}}; and the drill
Of {{c|3628|Little Turtle}}; {{c|3656|Osceola}}; raw
{{c|3641|Seminole}} in the swamp, unconquered still;

The children in the {{p|Mississippi}}'s flow;
The {{c|3637|Choctaw}} chief who named the tearful road;
The {{c|3636|Cherokee}} {{x|Phoenix}}; {{c|3664|John Ross}}, and so
The {{x|Court}} that ruled for justice; and the load

Of cruelty the {{c|3668|President}} laid down.
The book is open; hold it now, revealed
By {{c|3661|Moroni}}, and by memory's crown,
That what was done is kept, the dead unsealed

To speak through each line; and the sun put out
Of each lost nation rises in the word
That will not be lost, and, beyond all doubt,
Honor them all; honor the cost; be heard.

The {{x|Book}} of {{c|3402|Lehi}} ends. The record stands;
The continent remembers; hold it now
Here in your hands; the earth and all its lands
Remember; and the dead keep, still, their vow.
