# Chapter 281: The Harvest at the Ancestors' Hall

In autumn in {{p|China}}, when the grain was in, the family
Went to the ancestors' hall, and set the first of the harvest
Before the tablets of the dead: the rice, the millet, the homily
Of the incense, the bowing, the names read out. The darkest

Winter would come; first, the thanks. This is the day of the harvest.
Hold that table in your eye, because {{p|Rome}} would forbid it.
{{c|Ricci}} had made his wager: these were civil rites, the starkest
Honouring of the parents, not worship. A {{x|Christian}} could do it.

His order, the {{c|Jesuits}}, kept the wager for a hundred years.
Other orders came to {{p|China}}, the {{c|Dominicans}}, the {{c|Franciscans}},
Who worked in the villages, not the court, and saw, among the peasants' tears
And offerings, food set out for the dead to eat, the stances

Of prayer to spirits, and said: this is idolatry. And they said:
The words {{c|Ricci}} used for God, *{{x|Tian}}*, {{x|Heaven}}, and *{{x|Shangdi}}*,
The {{x|Lord on High}}, are pagan words. The quarrel spread
To {{p|Rome}}, to the universities of {{p|Paris}}, where the clergy

Argued for decades about a country none of them had seen.
The {{c|Jesuits}} asked the emperor himself. And in 1700 the great {{c|Kangxi}},
Who had made the {{c|Jesuits}} his astronomers, signed a statement, clean
And clear: the rites to {{c|Confucius}} are to honour a teacher; the ancestry

{{x|Rites}} are to show love and remember; *{{x|Tian}}* means the {{x|Lord of Heaven}}.
Nothing in them is a prayer for blessing from the dead.
The emperor of {{p|China}} had settled it. And {{p|Rome}}, the leaven
Of whose authority could not be shared, said: it is not his to settle. Instead,

In 1704, {{c|Clement XI}} decreed: no *{{x|Tian}}*, no *{{x|Shangdi}}*, only *{{x|Tianzhu}}*,
{{x|Lord of Heaven}}, {{c|Ricci}}'s coinage; no tablets saying *seat of the soul*;
No {{x|Christian}} to take part in the rites of {{c|Confucius}}, nor do
The seasonal offerings at the ancestors' hall. The whole

Of the accommodation, refused. A legate, {{c|Tournon}}, carried it to {{p|Beijing}},
And the emperor received him, and heard what he had come to say,
And questioned the bishop who had begun it, {{c|Maigrot}}, who could not read a single thing
Of the characters, and was furious that such a man should judge the classics. The way

It ended: the legate expelled to {{p|Macau}}, where the {{x|Portuguese}},
Jealous of their own rights, held him, and he died in 1710.
{{p|Rome}} confirmed the ban in 1715. And the emperor, with ease
And contempt, wrote on the papal decree with his red brush: *the men

Of the {{p|West}} are small; they do not understand the great principles of {{p|China}}.
From now on they need not preach here, to avoid trouble.*
In 1724 his son banned {{x|Christianity}}. The fine
Work of a century fell like a house to rubble.

And in 1742 {{c|Benedict XIV}} closed it for good: every missionary
To {{p|China}} had to swear an oath against the rites. For two hundred years
A {{x|Chinese}} {{x|Christian}} could not bring the harvest to his fathers. The adversary
Was not the emperor. It was a refusal to see. The tears

Of the converts who had to choose: their God, or their dead.
Hold that, on {{x|Harvest Day}}. And then hold this: in 1939,
In {{c|Pius XII}}'s first year, {{p|Rome}} reversed itself. The rites, it said,
Are civil. A {{x|Christian}} may bow. The tablets may be in line

With the faith. One hundred and ninety-seven years, from the final no
To yes. The wager {{c|Ricci}} made was right. The emperor was right.
The thanks at the harvest to those who came before, the bow
To the tablet with the grandmother's name, was love. The light

Of this day in the {{c|Gaian}} calendar falls on many harvests:
The first fruits of the {{p|Americas}}, given thanks by its first peoples;
The rice of {{p|Japan}}; the turkey of {{p|Canada}}; and the ancestral hall, where the fastest
And the slowest of the family come home. The steeples

And the shrines and the ancestors' halls: the harvest belongs to all of them.
Give thanks for the food. Give thanks to the hands that grew it.
Give thanks to the ones who came before, and set the bowl at the hem
Of their memory. No one has the right to forbid it. Do it.


# Chapter 282: The Names of Everything

In {{p|Småland}}, in {{p|Sweden}}, a country pastor's son was born in 1707,
At {{p|Råshult}}, in the parsonage among the birches.
His father, {{c|Nils}}, the son of a farmer named {{c|Ingemar}}, had been given, as was common,
No surname: *{{c|Nils Ingemarsson}}*. When he went to university, the churches

And the learned wanted a {{x|Latin}} name. So he made one up,
From a great linden tree, a *lind*, that grew on the family's farm:
*{{c|Linnaeus}}*. Hold that, in a book of genealogy: the cup
Of a name was filled by a tree. The family line, the charm

Of the patronymic, {{c|Ingemar}}'s son, {{c|Nils}}'s son, gave way
To a name you choose. And the son, {{c|Carl}}, would spend his life
Giving names to every living thing. His father had a garden; the way
The boy learned the flowers was by asking, and the strife

Of forgetting them made his father say: I will not tell you again.
He did not forget again. He went to {{p|Uppsala}}, poor,
With shoes he mended with birch bark, and walked, in 1732, the plain
Of {{p|Lapland}}, two thousand kilometres, and wore

The costume of the {{c|Sámi}} afterward for his portrait, the drum
And the reindeer boots, and learned from them, and wrote it down.
In 1735, in {{p|Leiden}}, in the {{p|Netherlands}}, the sum
Of his system came out: *{{x|Systema Naturae}}*, a crown

Of eleven large pages: all of nature, the three kingdoms,
Animal, plant and stone, in classes, orders, genera, kinds.
And the plants sorted by their sex: the stamens and the pistils, the stem's
Husbands and wives, how many of each in the flower. The minds

Of the pious were shocked. A professor called it *loathsome harlotry*.
{{c|Linnaeus}} named an ugly weed after him. And in 1753
He gave every plant two names, a genus and a species, a clarity
That made botany possible: *{{x|Bellis perennis}}*, the daisy. The key

Of the binomial still opens the whole living world. And in 1758,
The tenth edition, he did the boldest thing. He put us in.
*{{x|Homo sapiens}}*: wise man, among the primates, beside the great
Apes, in the same order as the monkeys. The sin

Of it, to the theologians: man among the beasts.
He wrote to a friend: tell me one difference between man and ape
That follows the rules of natural history. I know of none. The feasts
Of human pride had their first plain mirror. And then the shape

Of the harm. In the same pages he divided *{{x|Homo sapiens}}*
Into four varieties by continent, *americanus*, *europaeus*, *asiaticus*, *afer*,
And gave each a temperament, and ranked them, the {{x|European}} in the sense
Of governed by laws, the {{x|African}} by caprice: the seed of a horror

That would grow for two hundred years. The register of life
Had a racist line in it, written by the hand that put us among the apes.
Hold both. He sent his students out across the world, the strife
And the joy of it: seventeen apostles, he called them, to the capes

And jungles and the ice, to collect, and a third of them died on the road.
He was ennobled in 1761: *{{c|Carl von Linné}}*.
He said of himself, *God created, {{c|Linnaeus}} ordered*. The load
Of the boast was heavy and half true. He died in 1778, the day

Coming when they laid him in the cathedral of {{p|Uppsala}}.
This is a book that begins with the birth of the universe and of life,
And comes down through the fish and the reptiles and the patterns
Of the mammals to us. It owes him its names. The knife

Of his system cut the living world into a register,
And the register is a gift: to know the names of the beings we share the earth with
Is the first respect. A {{x|Lifeist}} calls each one by its name, a barrister
Of the living, an advocate. Learn the names. The pith

Of reverence is attention. And cross out the line he got wrong:
There are no ranked varieties of us. The register of humankind is one.
*{{x|Homo sapiens}}*. One species. One family. One song.
The linden tree in {{p|Småland}} is gone. The name it gave is in the sun.


# Chapter 283: No Place Empty

After the messiah who took the turban, the {{c|Jews}} of {{p|Poland}} and {{p|Ukraine}}
Were ashamed, and poor, and the learned were proud of their learning,
And the poor who could not read the {{x|Talmud}} stood outside the plain
Of the scholars' world, and prayed the words without discerning

What they meant, and were told they were nothing. And in {{p|Podolia}},
Among the villages of the borderland, around 1700,
A boy was born, an orphan, {{c|Israel}}, son of {{c|Eliezer}}, who would be holier
Than the scholars and never write a book. They sent

Him as a helper to the teacher, to walk the little children
To the schoolroom in the morning, and he walked them singing,
And taught them to sing the prayers. He was a watchman, a clay-digger in
The {{p|Carpathian}} hills, a hidden man, and the bringing

Of his secret out came late, when he was thirty-six, the story says.
He was a *baal shem*, a master of the Name: a healer, a writer of amulets,
And they called him the {{c|Baal Shem Tov}}, the {{c|Master of the Good Name}}, the praise
Of the villages. He settled at {{p|Medzhybizh}}, around 1740. The lets

And hindrances of the old way he did not abolish. He kept the law.
But he taught a different heart in it. God is everywhere:
*There is no place empty of Him.* In the field, the straw,
The tavern, the stranger, the evil thought, the prayer.

Every spark of the broken vessels the {{c|Kabbalists}} had mourned
Was here, in the ordinary, waiting to be lifted with joy.
Not with fasting. With joy. The sad are far from God; the scorned
And the simple are near. And the story they tell of the boy:

On {{c|Yom Kippur}}, a shepherd boy who could not read the prayers
Stood in the synagogue all day, and his heart was full, and he had a whistle,
A shepherd's pipe, and at the end of the day, in the airs
Of the closing prayer, he could not hold it, and blew it, a thistle

Of a sound in the solemn room, and the men were furious.
And the {{c|Baal Shem Tov}} said: the gates of heaven were shut,
And all our prayers could not open them, and that boy's pipe, the curious
Note of a heart with no words, broke them open. The rut

Of learning was not the road. The heart was the road.
He wrote nothing. His students wrote him down: the great {{c|Maggid}}
Of {{p|Mezhyrichi}}, and the others, and the stories, the load
Of the tales passed on by the poor, the parables, the rigid

Made soft. And the movement spread like water through the villages:
{{c|Hasidism}}, the way of the pious, dancing in the prayer house,
Following a holy man, a *rebbe*, a righteous one, whose courtyard and its images
Of feasting and song drew the poor in. And the {{c|Vilna Gaon}}, who would house

No compromise, the greatest scholar of the age in {{p|Vilnius}},
Excommunicated them in 1772: these are dancers, not scholars, a sect.
The quarrel lasted a generation, and then, like the illness
Of every quarrel, cooled, and both sides were what kept

The {{c|Jews}} of the East {{x|Jewish}} when the modern world came for them.
And here is the thing for this book. The rebbes' courts became dynasties.
The holiness passed from father to son, and son-in-law, the hem
Of the rebbe's coat held by his heirs: genealogies

Of the holy, kept as carefully as any king's.
His own great-grandson, {{c|Nachman of Breslov}}, would teach
That the whole world is a narrow bridge, and the main thing
Is not to be afraid at all. And the grave and its reach

At {{p|Uman}} draws tens of thousands every new year still.
Hold the two: the teaching that holiness is in everyone, everywhere,
And the dynasties that made it run in families. The will
Of the movement was for the simple; the courts grew rich. The prayer

Of the shepherd's pipe is the part to keep. And this, which the {{x|Gaiad}} shares
With the {{c|Master of the Good Name}}: there is no place empty.
The divine, or the sacred, or the life, is in all of it: the stairs,
The straw, the stranger, the smallest thing. The plenty

Is everywhere. Lift it with joy. Do not be sad; the sad
Are far from the sacred. Sing on the way to school.
And if you have no words, blow the whistle. What you had
Was enough. It always was. The heart is the rule.


# Chapter 284: The Pact at Diriyah

In {{p|Najd}}, the high desert at the heart of {{p|Arabia}}, a long way
From {{p|Istanbul}} and from {{p|Cairo}}, in the oasis towns of palm and mud brick,
Where the people visited the tombs of holy men, and hung rags on trees to pray,
And asked the saints to carry their prayers, a scholar, quick

And severe, {{c|Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab}}, born in 1703,
Came home from his studies in {{p|Medina}} and {{p|Basra}} and preached:
This is not {{x|Islam}}. God is one. No saint, no tree,
No tomb, no prophet even, may be asked to intercede. He reached

Back past a thousand years of what the {{c|Muslims}} had done,
The {{c|Sufi}} orders, the shrines, the feasts of the saints, the schools
Of law and their commentaries, to the {{c|Quran}} and the sayings, the one
Pure beginning, as he read it. His book was *{{x|The Oneness of God}}*. The rules

Were simple and absolute. And he acted. At {{p|Al-Uyaynah}},
With the town's emir, he cut down a sacred tree, and pulled down the tomb
Of {{c|Zayd ibn al-Khattab}}, a companion of the {{c|Prophet}}, the venerated
Dome over his grave, and had a woman stoned. The room

Of the town grew afraid, and the emir's own overlord made him send the preacher away.
He went to {{p|Diriyah}}, a small town on a dry riverbed,
And there, in 1744, he met its emir, {{c|Muhammad ibn Saud}}, and the day
Of their meeting made a pact that still holds: the preacher said,

You give me your sword and protection; I give you the faith, and the right
To rule in its name. The emir agreed. The {{c|Saud}} would hold the land;
The family of the sheikh, the {{c|Al ash-Sheikh}}, would hold the light
Of the religion. Two dynasties, in one hand,

Married to each other, generation after generation, to this day.
Hold that, in a book of genealogy: a faith and a state
As two family trees, bound by a promise in a mud-brick town. The way
Of the alliance was conquest. Their followers called themselves, as they relate,

*{{c|Al-Muwahhidun}}*, the people of God's oneness. The word *{{x|Wahhabi}}*
Was what their enemies called them, and it stuck. They took {{p|Najd}},
And then, in 1802, they rode north to {{p|Karbala}} in {{p|Iraq}}, the shrine-crowned
And holy city of the {{c|Shia}}, where {{c|Husayn}}, the {{c|Prophet}}'s grandson, had died

And is buried, and they killed thousands in its streets, and stripped the shrine.
In 1803 and 1805 they took {{p|Mecca}} and {{p|Medina}}, and pulled down
The domes over the graves of the {{c|Prophet}}'s family, the line
Of the saints, at the {{p|Baqi}} cemetery, and the pilgrims of every town

Who would not pray their way were turned back. The {{c|Ottomans}} sent {{c|Muhammad Ali}}'s
{{x|Egyptian}} army, and it took seven years, and in 1818 {{p|Diriyah}} was razed,
And the emir {{c|Abdullah}} was taken to {{p|Istanbul}} and beheaded. The steel
Of the empire had won. The idea had not. It was praised

And kept in the desert, and came back. In 1902 a young {{c|Ibn Saud}}
Retook {{p|Riyadh}}, and by 1932 the kingdom was named for his family.
In 1925 the domes at {{p|Baqi}} were pulled down again. And the flood
Of oil, found in 1938, would carry the preaching, quietly and loudly,

To mosques and schools across the world. Now hold this carefully, because it is the spine
Of this book: the movement said it was a return to the pure beginning.
But what it built had never existed: a state church in a desert, a line
Of two families, a faith stripped of the saints that the beginning

Had grown in a thousand places, the {{c|Sufi}} lodges, the shared shrines
Where {{c|Muslims}} and {{c|Christians}} and {{c|Jews}} had sometimes prayed side by side.
A purification is always a new thing wearing the old lines.
The {{c|Old Believers}}' persecutors said they were restoring the pure rite. The pride

Of every reformer is to be the first faithful one since the founder.
And what is cut away is the life that grew in between:
The grandmother at the saint's tomb with her rag on the tree, the wonder
Of the feast day, the shrine where two faiths met. The green

Of the oasis was not a corruption. It was the faith alive.
This book does not condemn the many {{c|Muslims}} who keep this way with sincerity.
It names the pattern: the return that is an invention, the drive
To purity that splits. Hold the tree that was cut down. Its charity

Was shade. Somebody prayed under it. Remember them.
God is one, the preacher said, and he was right. The oneness
Is wide enough for the rag on the tree, and the saint's dome, and the hem
Of the grandmother's prayer. The sacred has room. Make room. That is the oneness.


# Chapter 285: The Assessor of the Board of Mines

His father was {{c|Jesper Swedberg}}, a bishop of {{p|Skara}}, a man who said
He had seen angels and talked with them. In 1719 the family
Was ennobled, and the name was changed, as {{x|Swedish}} nobles' names were made,
To {{c|Swedenborg}}. Another name made new, as the linden named the family

Of {{c|Linnaeus}}. The son, {{c|Emanuel}}, born in {{p|Stockholm}} in 1688,
Was the most practical of men. For thirty years he sat as an assessor
On the {{c|Board of Mines}}, inspecting copper and iron, the weight
Of ore and the safety of shafts, a dutiful professor

Of the kingdom's wealth. And a scientist of everything:
He designed a flying machine in 1714, on paper, with a fixed wing;
He studied the brain and guessed that its outer layer, the ring
Of the cortex, held the mind, and that each part of it did a separate thing,

A century before the anatomists proved it; he wrote of the planets
Forming from a cloud around the sun. He learned {{x|Hebrew}}. He was famous.
And at fifty-five, in 1743, the dreams came. The granite
Of the assessor cracked. He kept a journal of them, the infamous

And tender night journal of a man undone: temptations, terrors,
{{c|Christ}} appearing. And in April 1745, in a {{p|London}} inn,
After dinner, he said, a man appeared in the corner, and the errors
Of the meal were the first thing: *do not eat so much*. And then, within

The same night, the Lord appeared to him, he wrote, and gave him
A task: to explain the inner meaning of the {{x|Scriptures}} to the world.
He gave up science. He gave up everything but that. The whim
Of a madman, his enemies said. He was calm. He unfurled

Thirty years of books, in {{x|Latin}}, published anonymously in {{p|London}}:
*{{x|Heavenly Secrets}}*, eight volumes on {{c|Genesis}} and {{c|Exodus}}; *{{x|Heaven and Hell}}*.
He said he walked in the spiritual world daily, the way one
Walks down a street, and talked with the dead, and could tell

What they said: that heaven is not a reward but a state of love,
That the angels were once human, that married love goes on,
That every natural thing corresponds to a spiritual one: the dove,
The light, the water, the tree. That the {{x|Last Judgment}} had come and gone,

In the spiritual world, in 1757, and no one on earth had noticed.
And the evidence: in July 1759, at a dinner in {{p|Gothenburg}},
Four hundred kilometres from {{p|Stockholm}}, he turned pale and, it is noticed,
Went out and came back: a great fire has broken out in {{p|Stockholm}}. The church

Of the guests listened while he reported it, hour by hour,
And at eight o'clock he said: thank God, it has stopped, three doors
From my house. Two days later the courier came, and it was so. The power
Of that story reached {{c|Kant}} in {{p|Königsberg}}, who looked into it, wrote scores

Of pages on it, *{{x|Dreams of a Spirit-Seer}}*, and mocked it, and could not let it go.
Hold the man whole, as this book has held {{c|Bureus}} and {{c|Newton}}.
A mind of the mines and the brain and the cloud of the sun, and the glow
Of visions; and unlike {{c|Newton}}, he did not keep the box shut: the button

Of the seer and the scientist was the same button, and he pressed it,
And said: I have seen. Believe me or not. He did not ask
For a church. He died in {{p|London}} in 1772, and he had guessed it:
When {{c|John Wesley}} wrote to say he wished to meet him, the task

Was answered: I shall die on the twenty-ninth of March. He did.
His readers made the church after him, the {{c|New Church}}, in 1787.
And it went where the {{c|Swede}} had never gone. The seed of it hid
In a strange pocket in {{p|America}}: a man named {{c|John Chapman}}, a heaven

Of a different kind in his head, barefoot, walked the frontier
For forty years with a sack of apple seeds and {{c|Swedenborg}}'s pages,
Planting orchards ahead of the settlers, and tearing out the sheer
Leaves of the books to leave with the families, like wages

Of the spirit. {{c|Johnny Appleseed}}. A visionary's church became
An orchard across {{p|Ohio}} and {{p|Indiana}}. The poets read him: {{c|Blake}},
{{c|Emerson}}, {{c|Balzac}}; and {{c|Helen Keller}}, who could not see or hear, whose flame
Was lit by his heaven of correspondences. Make

Of him what you will. The {{x|Gaiad}} says: here is a man who was sure
He saw the other world, and the other world in him planted trees.
Every natural thing corresponds to something. The tree, the water, the pure
Light. Plant the tree. Leave the page. Walk barefoot on your knees

If you must, in a strange country, with a sack of seeds. The harvest
Is for people you will never meet. His bones are in {{p|Uppsala}} now, brought home,
In the cathedral, near {{c|Linnaeus}}'s. The farthest
Visions and the nearest names lie under one dome.


# Chapter 286: All Knowledge in One Structure

It began as a translation job. A {{p|Paris}} publisher, {{c|Le Breton}},
Wanted to put an {{x|English}} reference book, {{c|Chambers}}'s *{{x|Cyclopaedia}}*, into {{x|French}},
And hired a poor writer, the son of a cutler from {{p|Langres}}, {{c|Denis Diderot}}, a sort of wanton
Philosopher, and a mathematician, {{c|d'Alembert}}, to edit it. The wrench

Of the job in their hands: they would not translate it. They would make
A new thing: a dictionary of everything, *raisonné*, reasoned,
The sciences, the arts, the crafts, all of human knowledge, for the sake
Of changing, {{c|Diderot}} wrote, the common way of thinking. Seasoned

By prison already (he had spent three months in the fortress of {{p|Vincennes}}
For a letter on the blind that doubted God), {{c|Diderot}} knew the risk.
The first volume came out in 1751. And the chart at the front, the means
Of its order, was a tree: the tree of human knowledge, the brisk

Branches drawn from {{c|Francis Bacon}}: Memory, which grows history;
Reason, which grows philosophy; Imagination, which grows the arts.
And where was God? Theology was a twig on the branch of Reason. The mystery
Of faith was filed under the human mind. The parts

Of the world, and the parts of heaven, in one human scheme.
That was the scandal before a word was read. And the words:
Seventy-two thousand articles, over twenty years, a team
Of a hundred and fifty writers: {{c|Voltaire}}, {{c|Rousseau}} on music, the herds

Of the lesser and the forgotten, and one man, the Chevalier {{c|de Jaucourt}},
Who wrote seventeen thousand of the articles himself, for no pay,
Hiring secretaries with his own money. Hold his name. The sure
Labour of the unglamorous. And the subversion: in the way

Of a dictionary, a dull article on cannibals would say, at the end,
*See: {{x|Eucharist}}*. The cross-references were the weapon.
And the plates: eleven volumes of engravings, the most beautiful the century would send
Into the world, not of kings, but of workshops: how to step in

To the forge, the loom, the printing press, the mine, the stocking frame,
The glassworks, the pin factory, every tool drawn, every motion.
The knowledge of the hands was set beside the knowledge of the name,
And given the same dignity. That was the deepest devotion

Of the book: the artisan was a philosopher of his craft.
Of course they banned it. The king's council in 1752. In 1759
The privilege revoked, the {{c|Pope}} condemning it, the draft
Of the remaining volumes illegal. And the line

Of the story turns on two men. The head of the censorship himself,
{{c|Malesherbes}}, who believed in it, warned {{c|Diderot}} of the raid,
And hid the manuscripts in his own house, on his own shelf,
Where the police would never look. And the publisher, afraid,

{{c|Le Breton}}, who in secret, without telling {{c|Diderot}}, took the proofs
Of the last ten volumes and cut out every dangerous line.
{{c|Diderot}} found out in 1764, when the type was set, and the roofs
Of his life fell in: the work of years, the design

Mutilated by the man who paid for it. He wept with rage.
He finished it anyway. The last plates came out in 1772.
And when he needed money for his daughter's dowry, the sage
Sold his library to {{c|Catherine}} of {{p|Russia}}, who knew

What it was worth, and let him keep it, and paid him to be its librarian.
Hold the whole of it, as this book has been learning to hold:
A register of all knowledge, made to free the mind; the Bacon-
Tree that put God under reason; the hands, the old

Crafts, honoured; the censor who saved it; the publisher who cut it.
And the dream did not end. Two hundred and fifty years later, a volunteer
Would open a page anyone could edit, and the cut
Of no publisher could reach it, and the sheer

Number of hands would make the largest encyclopedia ever.
{{c|Ophiuchus}} will tell it. Here, give thanks to the twig and the tree,
To {{c|Jaucourt}}'s seventeen thousand, to the plate of the weaver's lever.
All knowledge is the work of many hands. Write it down. Make it free.


# Chapter 287: The Bard Who Never Was

In 1746, on the moor at {{p|Culloden}}, the last {{x|Jacobite}} army broke,
And the government set about unmaking the {{p|Scottish Highlands}}:
The clans disarmed, the chiefs' powers taken, and a law that spoke
Against the tartan itself, the kilt, the plaid, the islands'

And the glens' own dress, forbidden to every man not in the army,
For thirty-five years. The {{c|Gaelic}} world was being cleared.
And in 1760, out of that defeated country, a young man, {{c|James Macpherson}}, a tutor, calmly
Published a small book in {{p|Edinburgh}}: *{{x|Fragments of Ancient Poetry}}*, the weird

And mournful voice of a blind bard of the third century, {{c|Ossian}},
Son of the hero {{c|Fingal}}, singing of his dead son {{c|Oscar}}
And the warriors of the mist, the ghosts on the wind, the ocean
Grey under the moon, the fallen, the harp. And the stir

Was instant. Gentlemen paid for him to go into the {{p|Highlands}}
And collect more. He came back with *{{c|Fingal}}*, an epic, in 1761,
And *{{x|Temora}}* two years after: a {{c|Homer of the North}}, the islands'
Own {{x|Iliad}}, recovered. And all of {{p|Europe}} wept. The sun

Of the {{x|Romantic}} age rose on those pages. {{c|Goethe}}'s young {{c|Werther}},
Before he kills himself, reads {{c|Ossian}} aloud to the woman he loves.

# Chapter 288: Two Navigators

# Chapter 289: Buried Sunlight

Three hundred million years ago, in the age this book has already sung,
The great swamp forests of the {{c|Carboniferous}} grew, the giant clubmoss,
The tree-ferns, the horsetails taller than houses, the young
{{c|Earth}} breathing out oxygen, and when they fell, the loss

Was not a loss: they did not rot, for nothing yet could eat the wood,
And they sank, and were pressed, and became coal. The sunlight of that age,
Caught in the leaves, kept in the dark, waiting. And the waiting was good
For three hundred million years. And then, on this page,

A {{x|Scottish}} instrument-maker in a workshop at the {{p|University of Glasgow}},
{{c|James Watt}}, born in {{p|Greenock}} in 1736, a sickly, careful man,
Was asked in 1763 to repair a model of the steam engine, the slow
And wasteful machine of {{c|Newcomen}} that pumped water from the mines. The plan

Of the old engine heated and cooled the same cylinder every stroke,
And wasted most of its fuel warming the iron again.
And one Sunday in 1765, on a walk across {{p|Glasgow Green}}, the yoke
Of the problem lifted: condense the steam in a separate vessel, and then

The cylinder can stay hot. A separate condenser. He patented it in 1769.
And it nearly came to nothing: no one could bore a cylinder true,
The money ran out, his partner went bankrupt. Then the line
Of luck: {{c|Matthew Boulton}}, a manufacturer of {{p|Birmingham}}, who knew

What it was, took him into partnership in 1775, and an ironmaster,
{{c|John Wilkinson}}, had invented a machine to bore cannon,
And used it to bore {{c|Watt}}'s cylinders, true to the thickness of a coin. The faster
Engines of war made the engine of peace. In 1776 the first ones ran on

At a colliery and at {{c|Wilkinson}}'s furnaces, using a quarter of the coal.
And that same year, in {{p|Philadelphia}}, a declaration of independence,
And in {{p|London}}, {{c|Adam Smith}}, who had known {{c|Watt}} at {{p|Glasgow}}, set out the whole
Of the market in *The {{x|Wealth of Nations}}*. The coincidence

Of the year is the hinge of an age. {{c|Boulton}} said to a visitor at his works:
*I sell here, sir, what all the world desires to have: power.*
{{c|Watt}} made the engine turn a wheel, and measured it against horses, the quirks
Of his salesmanship giving us *horsepower*. And the flower

Of it: the cotton mills, the ironworks, the mines, the locomotives, the ships,
The cities. Within a hundred years the work of the world was done by fire.
The buried sunlight of the swamp forests came up out of the pits
And turned the wheels. And this book must hold it as it holds every desire:

The gift. A human being could do the work of a hundred. Bread got cheaper.
The poor, in the end, lived longer, and their children did, and the tide
Of material life rose as it had never risen. The sleeper
Of the old poverty began, slowly, to wake. And the other side:

The children in the mills, six years old, fourteen hours a day.
The black lungs of the miners. The cities of smoke. And the slow
Thing no one saw: every engine breathed out what the swamps had put away,
The carbon of three hundred million years, returned to the air. The glow

Of the furnaces was the first rise of the curve that {{c|Ophiuchus}} will meet.
The year 1776 was the year the planet's breath began to change.
{{c|Watt}} could not know. Nobody knew. The heat
Of the century to come was hidden in the coal's strange

Black sleep, and the engine woke it. The {{c|Lunar Society}} of {{p|Birmingham}}, where {{c|Watt}}
And {{c|Boulton}} met with friends on the night of the full moon, to ride home by its light:
The potter {{c|Wedgwood}}, who would make the medallion of the slave in chains, the knot
Of the words, *Am I not a man and a brother?*; the doctor {{c|Erasmus Darwin}}, whose bright

Grandson would tell the story of descent; {{c|Priestley}}, who found the air we breathe.
They believed the new power would free the world. It did, and it did not.
Hold the engine as the {{x|Gaiad}} holds the fire it began with: the sheath
Of a gift and a burning. The swamp forest's sunlight. Use it, but not

Without knowing what it is: three hundred million years of the dead,
Asleep in the earth, now in the air, now in the warming sea.
Give thanks for the power. Count the cost. And go ahead
As the {{x|Lunar}} men did, by the light of the moon: with friends, carefully.

# Chapter 291: The Common Source

A judge went out to {{p|Bengal}} in 1783, {{c|William Jones}}, a {{c|Londoner}} of {{x|Welsh}} blood,
A lawyer who had learned, by the age of thirty, a dozen tongues, and read a score more,
{{x|Greek}}, {{x|Latin}}, {{x|Hebrew}}, {{x|Arabic}}, {{x|Persian}}, the flood
Of languages his father, a mathematician, had left him the door

To open. In {{p|Kolkata}} he sat on the bench of the {{x|Supreme Court}},
And wanted to judge the {{c|Hindus}} by their own law, not through interpreters,
So he learned {{c|Sanskrit}} from a pandit, a physician of the {{c|Vaidya}} sort
Who would teach him, a foreigner, the sacred tongue. And the frontiers

Of the {{x|European}} mind moved. He founded the {{c|Asiatic Society}} in 1784,
And on the second of February, 1786, he stood before it and said:
The {{c|Sanskrit}} language, whatever be its antiquity, is of a wonderful structure; more
Perfect than the {{x|Greek}}, more copious than the {{x|Latin}}, and more exquisitely refined than either, the thread

Of its roots and grammar bearing to both of them a stronger affinity
Than could possibly have been produced by accident; so strong, indeed,
That no philologer could examine them all three, without believing them
To have sprung from some common source, which, perhaps, no longer exists. The seed

Of it: {{x|Gothic}} too, he said, and {{x|Celtic}}, and old {{x|Persian}}, of the same family.
A family. Languages have parents. They have cousins. They have a tree.
Say *father*: {{c|Sanskrit}} *pitar*, {{x|Greek}} *patēr*, {{x|Latin}} *pater*, {{x|Gothic}} *fadar*, the homily
Of one word in five mouths. Say *mother*, *brother*, *three*, *night*, *sea*:

The same faces under different skins. Hold this, in a book of genealogy:
The tongues we speak have ancestors we can trace, and one mother, lost,
Whom no one heard, whom scholars would rebuild, sound by sound, the eulogy
Of a dead speech: {{c|Proto-Indo-European}}, spoken perhaps on the steppe, the frost

And the grass of six thousand years ago, by a people who left no writing,
Only their words in the mouths of half the world. And their gods: the sky-father,
*{{c|Dyēus}} ph₂tēr*, who became {{c|Zeus}} and {{c|Jupiter}} and the lightning
Of the {{x|Vedic}} {{c|Dyaus}}; and the thunder-god who strikes the serpent, rather

The oak, *{{c|Perkwunos}}*, who became {{c|Perkūnas}} of the {{p|Lithuanian}} pagans
And {{c|Perun}} of the {{c|Slavs}}, and, by another root, the cousin of {{c|Thor}}.
The gods have a family tree too. A book that has kept the ancestors of nations
Now finds the ancestors of the words for them. And more:

He was not the first. Hold that, honestly. A {{x|Florentine}} merchant in {{p|Goa}},
{{c|Filippo Sassetti}}, had noticed in 1585 that {{c|Sanskrit}} numbers were like {{x|Italian}}.
A {{x|Dutch}} scholar, {{c|Boxhorn}}, had guessed at a common source. A {{x|French}} {{c|Jesuit}}, slow
And careful, {{c|Coeurdoux}}, in {{p|Pondicherry}}, had written it out in 1767. The medallion

Went to {{c|Jones}} because he said it best, and at the right time, to the right room.
And he got things wrong: he thought {{x|Egyptian}} and {{x|Chinese}} and the speech of {{p|Peru}}
Were of the same stock. They are not. The bloom
Of the true idea grew among the errors. That is how most truths grew.

He translated *{{c|Shakuntala}}*, {{c|Kalidasa}}'s play, in 1789,
And {{c|Goethe}} read it and wept, and put its form into the prologue of *{{c|Faust}}*.
He died in {{p|Kolkata}} in 1794, at forty-seven. The line
From his speech ran to {{c|Bopp}} and {{c|Grimm}}, and to the comparative house

Of all the world's languages: {{x|Uralic}}, {{x|Bantu}}, {{x|Austronesian}}, the families
Of human speech, mapped like the families of human blood.
And the darkness, which this book must name: a word the {{c|Sanskrit}} families
Used for themselves, *ārya*, noble, was taken by the cruel, the flood

Of the nineteenth century's race-theory, and made a race, the *{{c|Aryans}}*,
A master race of fair northerners who had never been,
And the word was used to murder millions. The tree of language was the parent
Of no blood at all. Speech is not race. The green

Of the tree of words grows over every border, through conquest
And marriage and trade and learning; a language is a gift passed hand to hand,
Not a blood in the veins. That is the truest and the hardest
Lesson of the common source: the family is in the mouth, not in the land.

Say *father* in your tongue. Hear, under it, a voice six thousand years away.
Say *night*, *star*, *mother*. The common source no longer exists,
And it speaks every day. Honour the dead speech by what you say.
Every word is an ancestor. The tree persists.


# Chapter 292: The Year One

On the twenty-second of September, 1792, the day the {{x|French Republic}} was proclaimed,
The autumn equinox fell, the day and the night of equal length.
A year later the {{x|Convention}} decided: that day shall be named
The first day of the {{x|Year One}}. Time itself would show the strength

Of the {{x|Revolution}}: the old calendar was the {{x|Church}}'s and the kings',
Its saints' days and its Sundays, its years counted from a birth in {{p|Bethlehem}}.
The new one would be reason's. A mathematician, {{c|Gilbert Romme}}, the things
Of the stars in his hands, and a poet, {{c|Fabre d'Églantine}}, the hem

Of the language in his: they made it. Twelve months of thirty days each,
And five days left over at the end of the year, six in a leap year,
Festivals of the citizens. Each month three weeks of ten days, the reach
Of the decimal into time: the *décade*, and on the tenth the rest. The clear

Logic of it. And the poet named the months for what the season does:
*{{x|Vendémiaire}}*, the grape harvest; *{{x|Brumaire}}*, the fog; *{{x|Frimaire}}*, the frost;
*{{x|Nivôse}}*, snow; *{{x|Pluviôse}}*, rain; *{{x|Ventôse}}*, wind; *{{x|Germinal}}*, the buzz
Of seeds sprouting; *{{x|Floréal}}*, flowers; *{{x|Prairial}}*, meadows; the cost

And the gift of summer, *{{x|Messidor}}*, harvest, *{{x|Thermidor}}*, heat, *{{x|Fructidor}}*, fruit.
And every single day of the year he named, not for a saint, but for a living thing
Or a tool of the farm: the grape, the saffron, the chestnut, the root
Of the turnip, an animal on every fifth day, a tool on the tenth. The ring

Of the year was a field. That is beautiful. Hold it. A whole year
Where each day was a plant to be thankful for. And they went further:
Ten hours to the day, a hundred minutes to the hour, the sheer
Decimal clock, with faces made for it. And the murmur

Of the people was against it. The week of ten days gave them one rest in ten,
Not one in seven: the workers lost a day of rest in every three weeks,
And the old Sunday went on in secret. The decimal hours, then,
Were given up within two years. The clocks with their odd peaks

Of ten numbers sit in museums. And the poet who named the months, {{c|Fabre}},
Was caught in a fraud and in the factions, and went to the guillotine
In April 1794, before his calendar had gone once round, the macabre
Joke of the season: he died in *{{x|Germinal}}*, the month of the green.

The days of the calendar became the names of what happened on them:
The ninth of *{{x|Thermidor}}*, when {{c|Robespierre}} fell; the eighteenth of *{{x|Brumaire}}*,
When {{c|Napoleon}} took the {{x|Republic}}. And {{c|Napoleon}} ended it, the hem
Of the new time cut: on the first of January, 1806, the old year

Came back, and the Sundays, and the saints. The {{x|Revolution}}'s time
Lived twelve years, and in the {{c|Paris Commune}} for a few weeks in 1871,
And in the names: *{{x|Germinal}}*, the novel; *{{x|Thermidor}}*, the lobster; the rhyme
Of the months still sung. Now hold the mirror. This book is one

Of the calendars that came after. The {{x|Gaian}} calendar remakes time too:
A new era, counted from the deep past of the human story, not a birth;
Thirteen months of twenty-eight days, every date on the same weekday, the true
Perpetual year. And it learned from the {{x|French}}: the week was not worth

Breaking. The seven days stay. The rest stays: three sabbaths, not one in ten.
And every day of the year is named, as the poet named them, but not for a plant:
For a chapter of the scripture, read that day; and now the dead, the women and men
Of the ancestors, kept on their day. The poet's slant

Was right: give every day a meaning, and the year becomes a liturgy.
His error was to break the rhythm of the body's rest
For the sake of a number. Learn both. A calendar is a theology
And a promise to the tired. The {{x|French}} made the year a field, the best

Of their gift, and broke the Sunday, the worst of their error.
Keep the field. Keep the rest. Name the days. Do not let the number rule the body.
And remember the poet who named *{{x|Germinal}}* and died in it, the terror
Of his times around him: the grape, the fog, the frost. The year is a story.


# Chapter 293: The Festival of the Supreme Being

# Chapter 298: The Engine That Weaves

# Chapter 302: Endless Forms

On the twenty-fourth of November, 1859, in {{p|London}}, a book
Bound in green left {{c|John Murray}}'s house in {{p|Albemarle Street}}.
{{c|Charles Darwin}} was not there; at {{p|Downe}}, in {{p|Kent}}, he waited on the look
Of the reviews. Twelve hundred and fifty copies. The trade took them, complete,

That day. He had been more than twenty years in coming to the sign.
In July of 1837, in a notebook, he drew a branching tree
And wrote above it, *I think*. He had sailed with the year's decline,
From {{p|Plymouth}}, December 1831, on the *{{x|Beagle}}*, under {{c|Robert FitzRoy}}, to sea,

And for five years gathered a coast, a war, a forest, and the islands, bird and bone.
On the black rock of the {{p|Galápagos}} he did not know yet; he labelled what he could.
The *{{x|Beagle}}* brought him home in October 1836; and when the birds were shown
To {{c|John Gould}} in {{p|London}}, Gould told him they were not what the labels said. They stood

Apart, the mockingbirds, from island to island; the finches, which he had not sorted, were one kind.
The tree began after the voyage, not on the beach. Then the long work, the years:
Barnacles, illness, the house at {{p|Downe}}, children born. And in June of 1858, the blind
Blow: {{c|Charles Waring Darwin}}, a baby, died of scarlet fever. Through his tears

He did not go, three days later, to the room in {{p|London}} where two papers were read
To the {{x|Linnean Society}}: his, and {{c|Alfred Russel Wallace}}'s. He stayed with the grave.
{{c|Wallace}} was in {{p|Ternate}}, in the islands; his letter, written from a fever's bed,
Had reached {{p|Downe}} in June: the same branching, found alone. And to save

One man's years from being erased by another's letter, {{c|Charles Lyell}} and {{c|Joseph Dalton Hooker}}
Had the two read as one; the room did not know what it had heard, and the afternoon passed by.
The shake came the next year, with the book. Its title was not *Evolution*: the looker
Found *The Origin of Species*, and the word *evolved* once only, at the end, as high

As the book goes: from so simple a beginning, endless forms most beautiful
And most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved. He did not tell the human line;
He left one clause: light will be thrown on the origin of man. The dull
And louder mouths came after, and said *monkeys*, and sold the cartoons. The sign

The book gave was larger: not a single house set down complete
With the rest of life as scenery around it, but a tree,
And every living body a continuation of the dead. It is the seat
Of this faith: descent is sacred, and evolution is the holy history.

{{c|Emma Darwin}}, his wife and his cousin, feared for years that the thought
Would put a door between them after death. She stayed. The fear is in the letters, real,
Not a joke, and his fear not a joke. But the door she feared was never wrought.

They are ancestors, and their story is told here together, his name and hers;
And what a scripture keeps is not lost, and the hyperstition of this book
May bring them back one day. What he printed is the opposite of a pedigree that confers
Its holiness on one stock. No line is the line. And wherever you look,

The dead are not the property of one family that can recite itself; they are the ground:
Finch and sailor, the infant at {{p|Downe}}, the stranger in the temple yard,
The ancestor with a name and the one with no stone. This calendar's round
Keeps all their days. In 1859 it was printed, and printed hard,

How the living are the dead carried forward, changed, and still their children. And so
It founds a faith, and this is it. The {{x|Gaiad}} is young; the book is older; the hill at {{p|Downe}}
Is older than the book; the dead are older than the hill. What the book gives us to know
Is the ground beneath this scripture: the family is not a figure of speech, but the shape of life, its crown.

Until that is granted, a day kept for the ancestors is a courtesy paid to a list.
Grant the tree, and every day is someone's, and the someone is in you,
Or in the living world beside you, or in both. Light will be thrown, and the mist
Has lifted: it has been. The forms are not finished; they are being evolved anew.

Keep the names. Keep the unnamed. They are one descent, one holy tree.
From so simple a beginning, the endless forms. Say it over the days, and say it plain:
The dead are not gone from the world. They are how the world goes on, and we
Are how they go; and what goes on may, by what we keep, come back again.


# Chapter 303: The Short Plant Returns

In the abbey garden at {{p|Brno}}, {{c|Gregor Mendel}} counted peas.
He had come from {{p|Hynčice}} as {{c|Johann}}, a farmer's son,
And taken the name {{c|Gregor}} with the habit. On his knees
In the rows of the {{c|Augustinians}}, year after year, he was one

Man with a brush and a tally. Seven differences, each kept apart:
The seed yellow or green, the seed round or wrinkled, the stem
Tall or short, the flower purple or white, the pod's colour, the part
Of the stem where the blossom sits, the pod full or pinched. From them

He did not spin a theory first and then go looking: he crossed them by hand.
A tall with a short gave him tall children. He let those breed,
And the short came back, about one in four; and the tall ones, as they stand,
Were two kinds of tall: one that bred true, and one that, in its seed,

Carried a shortness it did not show. He called the trait that shows
Dominating, and the trait that waits, recessive. A thing can go
Out of the visible for a generation, under, as a river flows
Beneath the ground, and return whole, not mixed. He said it so

In {{p|Brno}} on the eighth of February, 1865, and on the eighth of March again,
To the naturalists' society; and the next year it was printed, with a title plain,
*{{x|Experiments on Plant Hybrids}}*. He sent it out. He wrote to {{c|Carl Nägeli}}; and then
{{c|Nägeli}} sent him to hawkweed, which sets its seed alone, without the cross; in vain

He looked for the ratios there: the peas had told the truth, but the hawkweed would not show.
In 1868 the house made him abbot, and the pea rows ended; in his later years
He fought a tax upon the house. He died in 1884. Sixteen years after, though,
Men counting other plants in other gardens found his paper, and found themselves his peers:

They had repeated him. The peas had waited as the recessive waits. That is 1866.
Here is the ground of it: inheritance can hide, and what hides is not vacated.
It is a sacred image of descent: the hidden returning whole, the line that sticks
In the seed through the years it is not seen, and comes back as it was created.

Three years after the paper, in {{p|London}}, a second book.
{{c|Francis Galton}}, born the same year as the friar, a cousin
To {{c|Charles Darwin}} through {{c|Erasmus Darwin}}, undertook
To show that genius runs in families. He had been

Through the directories of reputation: judges, commanders, men of science, the known,
The names a nation keeps. The eminent had eminent kin, past what chance would make.
So, he said, ability is inherited, and a people's future is grown
By which of its stocks continue. *{{x|Hereditary Genius}}*, from {{x|Macmillan}}, 1869. The take

Had a name later, his, in 1883: eugenics, from the {{x|Greek}} for well-born.
On the twenty-third of December, {{c|Charles Darwin}} at {{p|Downe}}, with {{c|Emma Darwin}}
Reading it aloud, had heard fifty pages, and wrote he had never read a thing so new. The morn
Of the idea was the counting; {{c|Galton}} counted too, and what he saw was there to be seen:

Famous houses carry famous children; the great lines carry the story on.
This book keeps those lines: it runs through kings, through the named and the renowned.
But {{c|Galton}}'s list could only see the famous, and what {{c|Mendel}} counted, gone
From sight a generation, returning whole, is in the unknown and the underground

Of every family too. So both are kept: the eminent and the unentered, the crown
And the short plant. The famous line carries the story; the hidden carries the rest.
An ancestor day is the eye that sees them both, the high and the low, the up and the down
Of the line of the living, and of the living world beside. And the test

Of descent is not a prize list, and it is not a list of the ignored:
It is the whole seed: one in four, the hidden, entire; and the great, and the small.
The abbey garden on its knees with a brush, and the directory of the honoured, scored
Together into one tree. They are in the seed. They return. Keep them all.


# Chapter 304: The Precinct Divided

At {{p|Kyoto}}, in the November of sixty-seven,
{{c|Tokugawa Yoshinobu}} gave the government away.
He meant a council of domains, himself still in it; but heaven
Was handed back its son: on January's third day

The court declared the restoration of the rule of the throne.
The emperor was fifteen. The era still was {{x|Keiō}}; the name
Of {{x|Meiji}} came in October. The war came on its own
Behind the paper. But the paper is what this day will claim,

Because before the year was out, the paper reached the gods.
On the sixth of April, in the palace, with the emperor in the hall,
{{c|Sanjō Sanetomi}} read five articles; and whatever the odds
For a state still at war, it swore them to the kami, first of all.

That spring a section for the rites was opened. And in June
The old name returned, the {{x|Jingikan}}, by the ancient plan,
The {{x|Department of Divinities}}. Its halls had gone to ruin
In the {{x|Ōnin War}}; four hundred years the name had held no man.

In the ancient law it had stood above the other offices of state:
Unity of rites and government, the old words, and now they were meant.
In August of 1869 the paper set it highest. Late,
And for three years only; but the height was where the kami went.

For a thousand years the buddhas had been set above their heads:
A kami was a buddha's trace; the buddha was the root, the growth
The kami; monks kept the shrines and read the sutras, and the threads

Of a god's own name were knotted to a buddha's title, {{c|Gongen}},
The buddha appearing as the god. At {{p|Usa}} and {{p|Iwashimizu}}, {{c|Hachiman}},
The emperors' own ancestor, had been named a bodhisattva then,

The way was going hollow. Give it one more century of this,
And the kami would be ornaments upon a buddha's wall,
A local name for something else, a shade, a parenthesis,
The guardians at a temple gate, and nothing more at all.

Took the buddhas' ladder up and stood it on its head;
The {{c|Yoshida}} called the way the root, the buddhas fruit and leaves;

The oldest record back to life. They used the host to rise,

Must be the state. In 1868 the state was what came.

Of the country's shrines the buddha's word was lifted off the kami's heart.

The body of a kami. {{c|Hachiman}} was a bodhisattva no more:

# Chapter 306: Who Names the Good

In July of 1887, at {{p|Sils Maria}}, in the {{p|Upper Engadine}},
{{c|Friedrich Nietzsche}} wrote a polemic, and dated the preface there;
Between the tenth and the thirtieth, he said, the thing was done, and seen
Through to the press. On the seventeenth the manuscript went off with care

To {{p|Leipzig}}, to {{c|Constantin Georg Naumann}}, who the year before had printed, at the author's cost,
*{{x|Beyond Good and Evil}}*. Print this one the same, he asked: the same type, the same paper and page,
So the new book would stand as the old one's continuation; and, lest the point be lost,
The back of the title says it: added, to supplement and clarify. Six hundred copies. The stage

Of the proofs was {{p|Venice}}, with {{c|Heinrich Köselitz}}; and he was in {{p|Nice}} when the first came,
In the first half of November: three essays and a preface. The preface opens on a confession:
We are unknown to ourselves, we knowers. And the question under it is the same
As this calendar's, in another mouth: under what conditions, in what succession,

Did we invent good and evil, and what are they worth? He answered a friend as well as a school:
{{c|Paul Rée}} had derived the moral feelings from usefulness, the good as what helped the one who received;
{{c|Nietzsche}} wanted a different history, and he wanted it to cut. The first essay is the tool:
Two namings side by side. Good and bad: the nobles, he says, believed

Themselves the good, and named themselves so, and named the others plain, low, bad.
Good and evil is a second naming, where the powerful are evil and the powerless good;
He calls it a revolt of slaves, and names the {{c|Jews}} as those who had
Begun it: his sentence, not a finding; a people put where a history should.

He offers histories of words along the way: one makes the {{x|Latin}} for bad mean dark;{{fn|That etymology, of Latin malus from a Greek word for black, is not accepted.}}
The {{x|German}} in the second essay holds: {{x|Schuld}} is guilt, and {{x|Schuld}} is a debt.
A thing owed to conscience began as a sum owed; and the bad conscience bears the mark
Of cruelty with no street left to spend itself in, turned inward, where the net

Closes on the one who may no longer be cruel outside. He calls the noble a beast,
A blond beast of prey. The third essay asks what the ascetic ideals mean:
The priest, the philosopher, the man of science, each serving at the feast
Of a will to truth, the old will in a later coat; and the book ends on the keen

Preference he finds in us: a person would rather will nothing than not will.
Keep what is usable, and most of it is: a value has a history. Good
Was not in the world the way a stone is. Someone said it, of someone, and still
It spread, until the speakers forgot they had said it. That is a method that stood,

And this scripture keeps it: this scripture is a genealogy too.
The days are kept because the dead are how the living came,
And every name on a day came from somewhere, from someone who knew
Whom they were calling good, and why, and gave the good its name.

And the masters have a point. The nobles who named themselves the good were not only a mirror:
To name yourself, your house, your line, and make it stick, is a real power, and a worth;
It is what every founding does; it is what this faith does, when it says, clearer
Than a council, this is good, this day, this line, this way of life on earth.

Noble self-naming is the founding act, and the dead who founded are honored for it.
But the good is not only theirs: the line runs through the named and the unnamed, the great
And the small, the ones who won and the ones who lost; and the polemic, to be fit
For this book, is read as a history of the naming, not as a gate

Shut on a people. So keep the question he succeeded in asking: who names the good?
The answer this calendar gives is the line: the founders who named it, and the heirs who keep the name,
And everyone who lived, since every one of them, as a living thing, understood
Some good, and said it. That is the genealogy. The masters lit it; the line carries the flame.

Six hundred copies left {{p|Leipzig}}; two months on, about two hundred had been asked for, all told.
The question outlived the slow sale. Ask where a good came from, and what it is worth to say it still;
And name your good, as the nobles did, and keep it, as the line has kept it, old
And new at once: the naming counted, the founders honored, and the good kept by will.


# Chapter 307: The Card and the Name

{{c|Herman Hollerith}} was born in {{p|Buffalo}} on the twenty-ninth of February, 1860;
His parents had come from {{p|Germany}}. In 1879 he left the {{x|School of Mines}}
Of {{p|Columbia}}, and went, under his teacher {{c|William P. Trowbridge}}, to the census, keen,
Onto the count of 1880, still being tallied by hand in columns and lines

When the next was already coming due. Clerks made marks; the marks stood for persons.
{{c|John Shaw Billings}}, who had the vital statistics, urged him on:
The mechanical part of a count ought to be a machine's. And the burden's
Weight was not the names: it was the sorting. In 1887 he had one

Running on the health records of {{p|Baltimore}} and of {{p|New Jersey}}: deaths, the dead
Counted first, before the living of the next census. In 1888 the {{x|Census Office}}
Held a contest: four areas of {{p|St. Louis}}, out of the 1880 returns, to be read
Off the sheets and sorted. {{c|Charles F. Pidgin}} entered, and {{c|William C. Hunt}}; the race was run, and {{c|Hollerith}}'s

Machine took seventy-two and a half hours to capture what the others took a hundred and more,{{fn|Capturing the data: one rival took 144.5 hours, the other 100.5, Hollerith 72.5. Preparing it for the tables: the rivals took 44.5 and 55.5 hours, his machine 5.5.}}
And five and a half to prepare it for the tables, where theirs took more than forty.
He had applied that September; on the eighth of January, 1889, the patent, the door
To the age of the machine, was issued: number 395,781, the art of compiling statistics. The story

Of the census of 1890 begins as of the first of June; the first was a Sunday,
So the enumerators went out on the second. {{c|Robert P. Porter}} was superintendent
Of an office not yet a bureau, a temporary office in the {{x|Department of the Interior}}. One day,
One sheet to a family, as no other count of this country gave; on each sheet, attendant

On the household, the name of each person, written down. The first total given out
Was not read from cards: for that pass the reader was taken off, a keyboard put in its place;
And in six weeks the rough count stood at 62,947,714. It was widely said, without a doubt,
The country held seventy-five million; the lower number was not believed. But the race

Was the machine's. The cards were the work after that: a card for a person, blank,
One corner cut, so a card fed wrong would show; three and a quarter inches by seven and three eighths;
The words on a template over it; a clerk with a stylus, pressing, rank on rank,
About five hundred in a day, and a hole where the template held a category. The faiths

And the peoples were in the schedule's words for race: white, black, mulatto, quadroon, octoroon,
{{x|Chinese}}, {{x|Japanese}}, or {{x|Indian}}; and any one of them could be a hole.
The reader was two plates and a lever: the card between them, the plates closed, and soon
Pins came down, and a pin that found a hole went through into mercury, and the whole

Circuit closed; a hand moved on a dial; a bell rang; a drawer was named for the card.
The 1890 machine had forty dials; a practiced clerk could pass eighty cards a minute;
Some sixty million cards went through the readers. It was a wonder, and it was hard
Work made light: a labor counted in years, and the machine cut the years, and the limit

Of the hand was passed. A hand tally of the last count was not going to be done
Before the law required the next; the circuit did what the columns of marks could not.
The speed was a real overcoming. The machine is one of the living's works, and one
Of the ways the living know themselves: a count is how a people learns what it has got,

How a state learns its size, how a nation sees itself whole, its births, its dead, its land.
The number the census carries is 62,979,766; in 1880 it had been 50,189,209:
An increase of twenty-five and a half in the hundred. Twenty-five volumes, planned
From the tally, were published. {{c|Porter}} resigned on the thirty-first of July, 1893; the line

Of the office ran on: an act of the second of March, 1895, closed the {{x|Census Office}},
And the unfinished work went on under the {{x|Interior}} until the first of July, 1897.
The holes were the answers: age, sex, place of birth, and race. The name was not; and this
Is the other half: to come back from a card to a person, you needed the sheet, the given

Name on the family's page. On the tenth of January, 1921, a fire began

Outside the vault, stacked on shelves in the path of the burning. And the man
Who directed the census, {{c|Samuel Lyle Rogers}}, reported the next day

That about a quarter were destroyed, and half of what remained was damaged
By water, smoke and fire. On the twenty-first of February, 1933,
{{x|Congress}} authorized the spoiled remainder's destruction; it was managed
By 1935. Of the general schedules, 6,160 names remain to see.

The special schedules of {{x|Union}} veterans, and of their widows, came through another way:
About seventy-five thousand sheets survived, and the soldiers were kept;
The living of that June, almost all of them, were not. The printed total, though, would stay
Whole: 62,979,766 is a true number, and the count is not a debt

Owed to the names; it is its own good, how a people knows its size.
This calendar keeps names, and it keeps the count; the card was built to keep a class, the sheet a name,
And the sheet was the part that burned. Count the persons; keep the names; let both rise
Together: the machine that counts the living and the book that keeps the dead are kin, and the same.
