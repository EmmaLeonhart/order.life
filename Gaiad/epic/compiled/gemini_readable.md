# Gemini (7宮) - Days 169-196


# Chapter 169: Confucius and Laozi

In {{p|China}}, during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE—the same
Centuries that produced Zarathustra's reforms, the {{p|Babylonian}}
Exile, and {{p|Ionian}} philosophy—a parallel civilizational reimagining
Was taking place. The {{p|Zhou Dynasty}}, established four centuries

Earlier, had fragmented. Its feudal vassals had become effectively
Independent kingdoms, warring among themselves for dominance.
Central authority was nominal. The {{p|Zhou}} king at {{p|Luoyang}} presided
Over the ritual calendar but could not enforce order. This period—

The {{p|Spring and Autumn}} period, from seven-seventy-one to four-
Seventy-six BCE, to be followed by the even more chaotic {{p|Warring States}}
Period—was politically a disaster. But it was culturally a
Flowering. Out of the disorder came the {{p|Hundred Schools of Thought}}:

{{p|Confucianism}}, {{p|Daoism}}, {{p|Legalism}}, {{p|Mohism}}, the {{p|School of Names}},
The {{p|Yin-Yang School}}, and others. {{p|Chinese}} philosophy was born in
The breakdown of political order. And two of those schools—
{{p|Confucianism}} and {{p|Daoism}}—would prove to be the most enduring.

They would shape {{p|East Asian}} civilization for the next twenty-five
Centuries. Their founders, Confucius and Laozi, stand at the
Root of everything that followed.

                                      Confucius. In {{p|Chinese}},
Kong Fuzi or Kongzi—"Master Kong." Born in five-fifty-one BCE
In the small state of {{p|Lu}}, in what is now {{p|Shandong Province}}. His
Family was of minor aristocratic descent but had fallen on hard

Times; his father died when Confucius was three. He was raised
By his mother in relative poverty. He received whatever education
He could, studying the classical texts of the {{p|Zhou}} ritual tradition.
He served in minor government posts in {{p|Lu}}, rising to become

Minister of Justice or something equivalent, but his reforms
Alienated powerful families and he was forced out. He then spent
Fourteen years wandering from state to state, trying to find a
Ruler who would implement his political philosophy. He found

None. He returned to {{p|Lu}} and spent his last years teaching his
Disciples and editing the classical texts. He died in four-seventy-nine
BCE, considering himself a failure. He had accomplished nothing
Politically. His ideas had been rejected by every ruler he

Approached. His disciples were few. And yet, from these unpromising
Materials, arose the most influential ethical philosophy ever
Developed in {{p|East Asia}}. Because Confucius's disciples preserved
His teachings in the {{p|Analects}} ({{p|Lunyu}}), a collection of his sayings

And brief anecdotes, compiled probably several generations after
His death. And subsequent generations of {{p|Confucian}} philosophers—
Mencius, Xunzi, the {{p|Han}} dynasty scholars, the great
{{p|Neo-Confucians}} of the {{p|Song}} dynasty, the {{p|Ming}} thinkers, the

{{p|Korean}}, {{p|Japanese}}, and {{p|Vietnamese}} {{p|Confucians}}—would elaborate
His insights into one of the world's great philosophical systems.
A system that would eventually become the state ideology of every
{{p|Chinese}} dynasty from the {{p|Han}} onward and of every {{p|East Asian}}

Polity that adopted {{p|Chinese}} political culture. The rejected
Wanderer became, posthumously, the teacher of half of humanity.

                                                                 What
Did Confucius teach? At the core, an ethic of virtuous human

Conduct grounded in five key relationships: ruler-subject, father-
Son, husband-wife, elder brother-younger brother, friend-friend.
Each relationship has its specific duties and virtues. The ruler
Should govern with benevolence ({{p|ren}}, sometimes translated as

"Humaneness" or "humanity"); the subject should serve with loyalty.
The father should nurture; the son should exhibit filial piety
({{p|xiao}}). The husband should lead righteously; the wife should
Support dutifully. The elder brother should care for the younger;

The younger should respect the elder. Friends should practice
Mutual trustworthiness. These are not abstract principles; they
Are concrete social obligations embedded in daily conduct. And the
Whole social order depends on each person fulfilling their role

Properly. When rulers rule with benevolence and subjects respond
With loyalty, when fathers are good fathers and sons are good
Sons, the whole society is in harmony. When these relationships
Are disrupted—when rulers are tyrants, when sons abandon filial

Obligation, when wives disrespect husbands—the society falls into
Chaos. The political disorder of the {{p|Spring and Autumn}} period was,
In Confucius's analysis, the symptom of ethical disorder. And
The remedy was to restore proper ethical conduct, starting with

The ruler and extending outward through the society. A ruler who
Was personally virtuous, who cultivated {{p|ren}} and practiced it
Consistently, would naturally attract loyal subjects and set an
Example that would spread through the social fabric. "The virtue

Of the wind bends the grass," Confucius said. Political reform
Begins with moral self-cultivation. This is a very distinctive
Ethical vision. It does not appeal to divine commands. It does
Not rely on reward and punishment in the afterlife. It does not

Invoke abstract moral principles derived from reason alone. It
Grounds morality in specific, role-based human relationships and
In the cultivation of personal virtue through learning, ritual,
And reflection. The good life, for Confucius, is the life of

The {{p|junzi}}—the "gentleman" or "exemplary person"—who has
Cultivated himself (and it is usually "himself," though Confucius
Was occasionally more inclusive than his later tradition) through
Study of the classics, mastery of ritual propriety, and practice

Of benevolent conduct. The {{p|junzi}} is not born but made, through
Continuous self-cultivation. And this self-cultivation is both
An ethical project and an aesthetic one. Confucius emphasized
Music, poetry, and ritual as essential to the cultivated life.

Beauty and morality were linked. A properly cultivated person
Would be sensitive to beauty and grace in human interactions and
In artistic expression. The {{p|Book of Songs}}, the {{p|Book of Rites}},
The {{p|Book of Changes}}, the {{p|Spring and Autumn Annals}}—Confucius

Urged study of these {{p|Five Classics}} not as dry scholarly exercise
But as formation of sensibility. One became a better person by
Immersion in the cultural heritage. This emphasis on education
Would become one of the most distinctive features of {{p|Chinese}}

Civilization: the sense that moral cultivation requires extensive
Study of canonical texts, that the scholar-official is the ideal
Human type, that political power should be exercised by educated
Persons rather than by hereditary aristocrats or warrior elites.

Over the following centuries, this vision would be institutionalized
In the {{p|Chinese}} civil service examination system, which for
Fifteen hundred years selected officials based on their mastery
Of {{p|Confucian}} classics rather than their birth. The examination

System produced a meritocratic elite—or something closer to one
Than any other pre-modern society achieved. It shaped the character
Of {{p|Chinese}} bureaucracy and, by extension, {{p|Chinese}} government,
More profoundly than any other single institution. And it rested

Entirely on the {{p|Confucian}} conviction that moral and intellectual
Cultivation should be the primary qualification for political power.

                                                                     And
Now Laozi. The historical existence of Laozi is disputed. The
Traditional account, in Sima Qian's {{p|Records of the Grand Historian}}
(Written in the first century BCE, several centuries after the
Presumed date), places Laozi as an older contemporary of Confucius,

An archivist at the {{p|Zhou}} court whom Confucius once visited for
Advice. In this telling, Laozi—"the Old Master"—was older, wiser,
And more withdrawn than Confucius. When Confucius sought him out,
Laozi rebuked him for his worldly ambition and urged him to let

Go of his attempts to reform society. Laozi eventually left the
{{p|Zhou}} court, disillusioned with its decay, and rode a water-
Buffalo westward toward the borderlands. At a mountain pass, the
Gate-keeper Yinxi recognized him and asked him to write down his

Teachings before leaving. Laozi agreed and composed the five
Thousand characters of the {{p|Dao De Jing}}, then departed into the
West never to be seen again. This is a lovely story. It is also
Almost certainly a composite myth. The actual composition of the

{{p|Dao De Jing}} probably happened in stages between the fifth and
Third centuries BCE, with multiple authors and editors contributing.
The text we have is a synthesis rather than the work of a single
Historical figure. But the figure of Laozi—whether real or

Representative—has functioned as the patron sage of {{p|Daoism}} for
The tradition that gathered around the text. So we may speak of
Laozi as the traditional founder, with appropriate caveats about
The historical evidence.

                             What does the {{p|Dao De Jing}} teach? The
Title translates approximately as "The Classic of the Way and Its
Power"—the {{p|Dao}} being "the Way" (the cosmic principle of how
Things fundamentally are and flow), and the {{p|De}} being "power" or

"Virtue" (the specific quality of being that emerges from alignment
With the {{p|Dao}}). The text is short, dense, often paradoxical, and
Sometimes willfully cryptic. Its central insights might be
Summarized as follows. The {{p|Dao}}, the fundamental principle of

Reality, cannot be fully captured in language or concepts. The
Very first line of the text acknowledges this: "The {{p|Dao}} that can
Be spoken is not the eternal {{p|Dao}}." Language and thought are
Useful tools but they are not adequate to the full reality they

Point toward. The {{p|Dao}} operates through spontaneous, effortless
Unfolding—{{p|wu wei}}, "action without forcing." When we impose our
Will on things through forceful intervention, we distort the
Natural flow of reality and often produce the opposite of what we

Intend. The wise person ({{p|sheng ren}}) learns to act in accordance
With the {{p|Dao}} rather than against it, achieving much through
Apparently little. "The softest things in the world overcome the
Hardest," says the {{p|Dao De Jing}}. Water, which yields to every

Obstacle, wears away stone over time. Flexibility is stronger than
Rigidity. Receptivity is more powerful than aggression. Silence
Is more effective than shouting. The political implications are
Radical. A ruler who tries to control everything exhausts himself

And produces rebellion. A ruler who governs lightly, who allows
The natural patterns of human life to flourish, produces harmony
Without effort. "Govern a large state as you would cook a small
Fish"—do not over-manipulate, do not interfere constantly, let

Things be. The less a government does, the better it governs.
This is a radical critique of bureaucratic and activist government—
A critique directly opposed to the {{p|Confucian}} emphasis on virtuous
Rulers and careful ritual management. Where Confucius urges the

Ruler to cultivate himself and act upon the society with benevolent
Example, Laozi urges the ruler to cultivate non-action and let
The society manage itself. These are different political visions.
Different ethical visions. Different conceptions of the good life.

And yet both emerge from the same cultural matrix, responding to
The same civilizational crisis of late {{p|Zhou}} political disorder.

                                                                   And
In practice, the two traditions have not been mutually exclusive

But complementary. {{p|Chinese}} civilization has, for millennia, held
Both together. Confucianism provides the public ethics of social
Life—the rules of propriety, the rituals of court and family, the
Moral education that shapes the gentleman-official. {{p|Daoism}} provides

The private counterweight—the mystical turn inward, the appreciation
For nature's spontaneous patterns, the willingness to step back
From worldly ambition, the aesthetic sensibility that valued the
Informal and the natural over the formal and the contrived. A

{{p|Chinese}} scholar-official spent his public life performing
{{p|Confucian}} duties—serving in government, managing his family,
Participating in ritual occasions. But his private life, his
Poetry, his painting, his interior cultivation, often drew on

{{p|Daoist}} sensibilities—solitary walks in the mountains, drinking
Tea by a stream, writing verse that captured fleeting natural
Moments, finding wisdom in lightness and emptiness. The two
Traditions together produced the distinctive sensibility of

{{p|Chinese}} high culture: ethically {{p|Confucian}}, aesthetically
{{p|Daoist}}, with a capacity to hold both orientations simultaneously
Without seeing them as contradictory. This complementarity would
Later be supplemented by {{p|Buddhism}}, when it arrived in {{p|China}} from

{{p|India}} in the early centuries CE. {{p|Chinese}} intellectual life would
Come to be described as a "three-teachings" civilization:
{{p|Confucian}}, {{p|Daoist}}, and {{p|Buddhist}}—three teachings that each
Provided something distinctive and that together made a complete

Cultural system. An educated {{p|Chinese}} person could simultaneously
Be {{p|Confucian}} in civic life, {{p|Daoist}} in aesthetic life, and {{p|Buddhist}}
In spiritual life, without experiencing this as intellectual
Inconsistency. The syncretic genius of {{p|Chinese}} civilization is

One of its most distinctive features, and its roots are in this
{{p|Spring and Autumn}} period moment when multiple philosophical
Traditions emerged from the same crisis and learned to coexist.

                                                                 The
{{x|Gaiad}} reads Confucius and Laozi together as the twin poles of
{{p|East Asian}} civilizational thought. Each is incomplete without
The other. Confucius gives us the ethics of social engagement,
The seriousness of moral cultivation, the dignity of institutional

Life. Laozi gives us the counterweight: the skepticism toward
Institutions, the awareness that reality exceeds our concepts, the
Turn toward nature, the recognition that sometimes not acting is
The wisest action. Together, they are one of the great civilizational

Philosophies. Separately, each would be impoverished. The fact that
{{p|Chinese}} culture held them together, rather than forcing a choice
Between them, is a sign of its intellectual sophistication. And
The {{x|Gaiad}} honors this. The {{x|Gaiad}}'s own religious vision, with its

Commitment to polytheistic plurality and its resistance to
Reductive monotheism, draws sustenance from this {{p|Chinese}} model
Of holding multiple truth-traditions together in productive
Tension. {{p|Confucianism}} and {{p|Daoism}} are not rivals but collaborators.

Not contradictions but complements. The civilization that produced
Them knew how to live in the space between them. That skill is
Worth learning.

                  And both traditions spread beyond {{p|China}}. {{p|Confucianism}}
Spread to {{p|Korea}} (where it would become even more deeply institutional

Than in {{p|China}} itself, particularly under the {{p|Joseon Dynasty}}), to
{{p|Vietnam}} (where it shaped the mandarin-bureaucratic system), to
{{p|Japan}} (where it was adapted to the samurai culture of {{p|Bushido}}).
{{p|Daoism}} spread more diffusely but was also carried along the same

Routes. Together they formed the intellectual infrastructure of
The {{p|East Asian}} cultural sphere—a sphere that by the nineteenth
Century CE included perhaps a quarter of the world's population.
The ideas worked out by impoverished teachers in the chaotic

Small states of sixth- and fifth-century BCE {{p|China}} would shape
The lives of billions for two and a half millennia.

                                                         Confucius. Laozi.
{{p|Ren}} and {{p|wu wei}}. Filial piety and spontaneous flow. The {{p|junzi}}

And the {{p|sheng ren}}. The {{p|Analects}} and the {{p|Dao De Jing}}. Moral
Cultivation and natural spontaneity. Public virtue and private
Mysticism. Two poles of {{p|Chinese}} civilizational thought.

The {{p|Spring and Autumn}} philosophers in an age of disorder. The
{{p|Hundred Schools of Thought}}. The foundation of {{p|East Asian}}
Intellectual tradition. The twin traditions that, together, would
Shape a civilization.

Confucius and Laozi. Paired pillars of the {{p|East Asian}} mind.


# Chapter 170: The Buddha

In the {{p|Gangetic}} plain of northern {{p|India}}, in the sixth and fifth
Centuries BCE, the same {{x|Axial Age}} ferment that produced Zarathustra,
Confucius, Laozi, the {{p|Judean}} exile, and {{p|Ionian}} philosophy was
Also transforming {{p|Indian}} religious thought. And at the heart of

That transformation was a single figure whose teachings would
Spread across {{p|Asia}} and eventually the world, shaping the lives
Of hundreds of millions. Siddhartha Gautama. The Buddha—"the
Awakened one."

                  Begin with context. The {{p|Indian}} religious landscape
In the sixth century BCE was in turmoil. The {{p|Vedic}} religion
Brought by the {{p|Aryans}} had evolved over centuries into an
Elaborate sacrificial system dominated by the {{p|Brahmin}} priestly

Caste. {{p|Brahmins}} performed increasingly complex {{p|fire sacrifices}}
That were thought to maintain cosmic order and benefit their
Patrons. But this system was under critique. Philosophical
Movements called the {{p|Shramana}} traditions had arisen—ascetic,

Wandering, often celibate traditions that rejected {{p|Brahmin}}
Authority and sought liberation through individual spiritual
Practice rather than through sacrificial ritual. The {{p|Upanishads}},
The final layer of the {{p|Vedic}} corpus, were being composed during

This period, and they represented a shift within {{p|Vedic}} thought
Itself—from outer ritual to inner contemplation, from the efficacy
Of sacrifice to the realization of the identity between Atman
(The individual self) and Brahman (the universal reality). The

Concept of {{p|karma}} (action and its consequences), the concept of
{{p|samsara}} (the endless cycle of rebirth), and the concept of
{{p|moksha}} (liberation from that cycle) were being elaborated. The
Question that drove this entire religious ferment was: how can

A human being achieve liberation from suffering and the endless
Cycle of death and rebirth? Different traditions gave different
Answers. The {{p|Upanishadic}} answer was philosophical insight into
The identity of Atman and Brahman. The {{p|Jain}} answer—developed by

Mahavira, a near-contemporary of the Buddha—was extreme asceticism,
Including practices of such severity that some {{p|Jain}} monks
Eventually starved themselves to death in the ultimate renunciation.
The {{p|Ajivika}} answer (now lost but significant in its time) was

A fatalistic determinism in which all things unfold according to
A cosmic pattern that could not be altered by human action. The
{{p|Materialists}} (the {{p|Charvaka}} school) denied the whole framework
Of rebirth and karma, arguing that there was only this life and

Death ended it. And the {{p|Brahmins}} continued to perform their
Sacrifices and insist that their tradition was the proper path.
Into this contested field stepped Siddhartha Gautama.

                                                                 He
Was born around five-sixty-three BCE (traditional dating) or
Four-eighty BCE (recent scholarly dating; the {{x|Gaiad}} will use the
Traditional date for narrative convenience) in {{p|Lumbini}}, a small
Village in what is now southern {{p|Nepal}}. His father Shuddhodana

Was a chieftain of the {{p|Shakya}} clan, a minor kshatriya (warrior)
Lineage. His mother Maya died shortly after his birth, and he
Was raised by her sister Mahapajapati. The stories of his
Childhood that survive are mostly later hagiography, but they

Emphasize two themes: that he was raised in extraordinary luxury,
Shielded from the harsh realities of ordinary life; and that he
Was destined, according to the sages who read his birth signs,
To become either a great king or a great spiritual teacher. His

Father, hoping for the former, tried to keep him insulated from
Anything that might turn his mind to spiritual questions. He was
Married at sixteen to Yashodhara. They had a son, Rahula. He
Lived a life of sheltered privilege. And then, according to the

Tradition, at age twenty-nine, he insisted on venturing outside
The palace walls. He saw "Four Sights" that changed his life: an
Old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering ascetic. Each of
The first three confronted him with realities he had been shielded

From. Age, disease, and death—the fundamental facts of embodied
Existence. The fourth, the ascetic, suggested a different way of
Living in the face of those realities. That night, Siddhartha
Left his palace, his wife, and his infant son. He cut off his

Hair, put on the robes of a wandering seeker, and began his search
For a way out of suffering.

                                He first studied with two famous
Meditation teachers, Alara Kalama and Udaka Ramaputta. He

Mastered their techniques quickly—mastering the attainment of
Deep meditative absorption—but he realized that these attainments,
While profound, did not eliminate suffering. Something deeper was
Needed. He then joined a group of five ascetics and practiced

Severe self-mortification for six years: extreme fasting, breath
Retention, exposure to extremes of heat and cold. He became so
Emaciated that, according to tradition, he could feel his spine
When he pressed on his stomach. And still, liberation did not

Come. He realized that extreme asceticism was as much a dead end
As luxury had been. Both extremes—indulgence and self-torture—
Kept the mind fixed on the body rather than freeing it. There must
Be a middle way between them. He accepted a bowl of rice pudding

From a young village woman named Sujata, recovering some strength.
His five ascetic companions, disgusted by what they saw as his
Weakness, abandoned him. He was alone. And he sat down beneath a
Fig tree at {{p|Bodh Gaya}} in what is now {{p|Bihar}}, and he resolved not

To move until he had found what he was seeking. He sat through
The night. According to the tradition, he was assailed during
This night by Mara, the personification of death and illusion,
Who tempted him with pleasure, fear, and the claim that Siddhartha

Had no right to the awakening he sought. Siddhartha touched the
Earth as his witness—the famous "earth-touching" gesture preserved
In countless {{p|Buddhist}} statues—and Mara was defeated. As dawn
Broke, Siddhartha understood. He saw the nature of suffering and

Its causes. He saw the path to its cessation. He became the
Buddha, "the awakened one." He was thirty-five years old.

                                                                 What
Did he realize? At the core of the Buddha's enlightenment was

What would become known as the {{p|Four Noble Truths}}. First: there
Is suffering ({{p|dukkha}}). All conditioned existence is characterized
By unsatisfactoriness—not just obvious pain and misery, but also
The subtle dissatisfaction that shadows even our pleasures, the

Knowledge that they will end, the impermanence that makes every
Attainment temporary. Second: suffering has a cause. Its cause is
Craving ({{p|tanha}}), the grasping for pleasant experiences and the
Aversion to unpleasant experiences. Craving arises from ignorance—

Ignorance of the true nature of reality. Third: suffering can end.
When craving is eliminated, suffering ceases. This cessation is
{{p|nirvana}}, literally "blowing out" (as a flame is blown out when
Its fuel is exhausted). Fourth: there is a path to the ending of

Suffering. This is the {{p|Noble Eightfold Path}}: right view, right
Intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right
Effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. The path is
Ethical, psychological, and contemplative. It is neither ascetic

Self-torture nor sensual indulgence but the middle way. And the
Path is practical. It can be walked by anyone who commits to it.
Another key insight: the {{p|doctrine of no-self}} ({{p|anatta}}). Unlike
The {{p|Upanishadic}} tradition, which identified an eternal Atman

(True self) as the key to liberation, the Buddha taught that there
Is no permanent, unchanging self. What we call the self is a
Constantly changing flux of physical and mental processes—form,
Feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness—which

Together constitute the {{p|five aggregates}} ({{p|skandhas}}) of personhood
But none of which, singly or collectively, is a permanent self.
The illusion of a permanent self is one of the root causes of
Craving and suffering. When this illusion is seen through, craving

Falls away naturally. This {{p|anatta}} doctrine is arguably the most
Radical of the Buddha's insights, and it is the one that most
Sharply distinguishes {{p|Buddhism}} from the other {{p|Indian}} religious
Traditions. It is also one of the most counterintuitive teachings

In world religion, because it challenges the foundational
Assumption of almost every spiritual tradition: that there is a
Real self whose welfare we are pursuing. The Buddha says there
Is no such self. There is only the flux. And the pursuit of

Liberation is not the saving of a self but the seeing-through of
The self-illusion.

                      Buddha spent the rest of his life—forty-five
Years—teaching. He attracted disciples. He founded the {{p|sangha}},

The monastic community. He traveled through the {{p|Gangetic}} plain,
Teaching to anyone who would listen. Kings came to him. Beggars
Came to him. Brahmins came to him. {{p|Untouchables}} came to him.
He taught them all. He did not distinguish by caste. This was

Itself a radical position in {{p|Indian}} society. His {{p|sangha}} included
Men of every caste, and eventually women as well (though his
Admission of women, at the urging of his foster mother Mahapajapati
And his disciple Ananda, came reluctantly and with special

Conditions that would keep nuns subordinate to monks). He taught
Not in {{p|Sanskrit}}—the sacred language of the {{p|Brahmins}}—but in
{{p|Magadhi Prakrit}} or similar vernacular, the ordinary language of
The people. This democratization of religious access was another

Radical feature of the early {{p|Buddhist}} community. And the
Buddha's teaching style was pragmatic. He refused to answer
Metaphysical questions that he considered useless for liberation—
Questions like "is the universe eternal?" or "is the self the

Same as the body or different?" or "does the enlightened one exist
After death?" He compared such questions to a man wounded by a
Poisoned arrow who refuses to let the arrow be removed until he
Knows the name of the archer, the material of the bow, and the

Fletching of the arrow. By the time he has his answers, he is
Dead. The Buddha's concern was always immediate: the alleviation
Of suffering, the path to liberation. Metaphysical speculation was
A distraction. This pragmatic approach made early {{p|Buddhism}} quite

Different from religious philosophies that centered on metaphysical
Doctrines. It was a path more than a creed. Later {{p|Buddhism}} would
Develop elaborate metaphysics and cosmology, but the historical
Buddha himself seems to have resisted such elaboration.

                                                               He
Died around four-eighty-three BCE at {{p|Kushinagar}}, at the age of
Eighty. His last words, according to tradition, were: "All
Conditioned things are impermanent. Strive on with diligence." He

Had founded no institution but the {{p|sangha}}. He had left no written
Scripture—his teachings were preserved orally by his disciples and
Would not be committed to writing for several centuries. He had
Not appointed a successor. The {{p|sangha}} itself was to be the ongoing

Community, with the {{p|Dharma}} (his teaching) as its guide. And the
{{p|sangha}} carried forward his teachings, elaborating, systematizing,
Sometimes disputing about their interpretation. Within a couple
Of centuries of his death, {{p|Buddhism}} had begun to spread beyond

Its original region. Under Emperor Ashoka in the third century
BCE—a {{p|Mauryan}} ruler who converted to {{p|Buddhism}} after the
Horrors of the {{p|Kalinga War}}—{{p|Buddhism}} became effectively the state
Religion of most of the {{p|Indian}} subcontinent. Ashoka sent missions

Westward to the {{p|Hellenistic}} world, southward to {{p|Sri Lanka}},
Eastward toward {{p|Southeast Asia}}, and likely northward across the
{{p|Himalayas}}. {{p|Buddhism}} began its transformation from an {{p|Indian}}
Regional tradition into a pan-{{p|Asian}} religion. Subsequent centuries

Would see {{p|Buddhism}} split into multiple schools—the {{p|Theravada}}
Of {{p|Sri Lanka}} and {{p|Southeast Asia}}, the {{p|Mahayana}} of {{p|Central Asia}},
{{p|China}}, {{p|Korea}}, {{p|Japan}}, and {{p|Vietnam}}, and later the {{p|Vajrayana}} of
{{p|Tibet}} and the {{p|Himalayas}}. Each would develop its own elaborate

Textual traditions, ritual practices, and philosophical doctrines.
{{p|Buddhism}} would eventually decline in {{p|India}} itself, partly
Absorbed back into {{p|Hindu}} traditions (with the Buddha reimagined
As an avatar of Vishnu), partly disrupted by {{p|Islamic}} conquests

That destroyed major {{p|Buddhist}} centers like {{p|Nalanda}} university
In the twelfth century CE. But by that time, {{p|Buddhism}} was firmly
Established across the rest of {{p|Asia}}, where it remains a major
Civilizational force to this day. Some five hundred million

{{p|Buddhists}} now live worldwide, with major populations in {{p|China}},
{{p|Japan}}, {{p|Korea}}, {{p|Thailand}}, {{p|Myanmar}}, {{p|Sri Lanka}}, {{p|Cambodia}}, {{p|Laos}},
{{p|Vietnam}}, {{p|Tibet}}, {{p|Mongolia}}, and—increasingly since the twentieth
Century—{{p|Western}} countries. The Buddha's teaching, which began

As the reflection of a single wandering ascetic under a fig tree,
Has become one of the most widely practiced religions in the world.

                                                                    And
The {{x|Gaiad}} honors the Buddha as one of the supreme figures of the

{{x|Axial Age}}. His insights into the nature of suffering and its
Causes, his articulation of ethical conduct, his recognition of
The constructed nature of the self, his refusal to privilege any
Caste or class in access to liberation—these are among the most

Profound contributions to human understanding ever made. The
{{p|Buddhist}} tradition has produced some of the greatest philosophers
In human history—Nagarjuna, Vasubandhu, Asanga, Dogen,
Hui-neng, Chandrakirti, Dharmakirti—whose subtle explorations

Of consciousness, emptiness, and causation rival anything in
Western philosophy. The {{p|Buddhist}} contemplative traditions—the
Various schools of meditation that trace back to the Buddha's
Own practice—have produced detailed empirical investigations of

The mind that are now being integrated with {{p|Western}} psychology
And neuroscience. The {{p|Buddhist}} ethic of compassion has inspired
Humanitarian movements worldwide. The {{p|Buddhist}} cosmology, with
Its vast scales of time and space and its teaching of rebirth,

Has expanded human imagination about the structure of existence.
The {{p|Buddhist}} art and architecture—from the {{p|Ajanta Caves}} to
{{p|Borobudur}} to the {{p|Great Buddha}} of {{p|Kamakura}}—is among the greatest
Religious art produced anywhere. The {{p|Buddhist}} tradition is vast,

Diverse, and enormously rich. And it all traces back to the
Young prince who left his palace and sat under the fig tree and
Found the middle way.

                         One more point worth making. The Buddha's
Teaching is often contrasted with monotheistic traditions on the
Question of divine existence. {{p|Buddhism}} is sometimes described as
Atheistic, because the Buddha did not affirm a creator god and
Because {{p|Buddhist}} practice does not depend on relationship with

Such a god. But the reality is more nuanced. {{p|Buddhism}} acknowledges
The existence of many gods ({{p|devas}}) who inhabit the various
Heavenly realms of the {{p|Buddhist}} cosmology. These gods are
Long-lived and powerful but they are still subject to the cycle

Of rebirth and, like humans, must eventually work out their own
Liberation. The Buddha is above them in spiritual attainment
Because he has achieved liberation while they have not. The
{{p|Buddhist}} position is not atheism but a refusal to grant ultimate

Importance to any god or gods. The gods exist; they are not
Ultimately relevant. What is ultimately relevant is each sentient
Being's own progress toward liberation. This is a position with
Some affinity to the {{x|Gaiad}}'s own deflationary polytheism—a

Recognition of divine pluralism combined with a refusal to treat
Any particular divinity as the absolute center of everything. The
{{x|Gaiad}} can appreciate the {{p|Buddhist}} framework as one genuinely
Compatible articulation of a non-dogmatic, non-imperialist religious

Orientation. {{p|Buddhism}} and the {{x|Gaiad}}'s own vision are not the
Same, but they share family resemblances.

                                              Siddhartha Gautama. The
Prince who left his palace. The ascetic who rejected both luxury

And self-torture. The seeker under the fig tree at {{p|Bodh Gaya}}.
The Buddha who saw the {{p|Four Noble Truths}}. The teacher of the
{{p|Eightfold Path}}. The founder of the {{p|sangha}}. The wanderer who
Taught for forty-five years. The one who died at {{p|Kushinagar}}

With the last words, "Strive on with diligence." The one who
Left no writings, no institution beyond the community, no
Successor—and yet whose teaching spread across a continent and
A world.

            Buddha. One of the supreme sons of the {{x|Axial Age}}.
The one who showed that liberation from suffering was accessible
Through disciplined practice rather than through priestly mediation
Or ecstatic grace. The one whose compassion extended to all

Sentient beings without distinction of caste or species. The one
Whose teaching is, still today, a living path for hundreds of
Millions.

             The Buddha. Awakened one.


# Chapter 171: The Mahabharata

The {{p|Mahabharata}}. One hundred thousand verses. Nearly two million
Words. The longest epic poem in world literature, seven times
Longer than the {{p|Iliad}} and {{p|Odyssey}} combined, four times longer
Than the {{p|Bible}}. A text that {{p|India}} has carried at the center of

Its cultural imagination for two and a half thousand years. A
Text that has been read, recited, performed, adapted, and reinterpreted
In every generation. A text that, more than any other, has shaped
The {{p|Hindu}} moral and religious world. A text, finally, of almost

Unfathomable depth—containing within itself dharma treatises,
Philosophical dialogues, creation myths, hymns, legal codes,
Political theory, love poetry, and—at its absolute center—the
{{p|Bhagavad Gita}}, which has become for {{p|Hinduism}} something like

What the {{p|Sermon on the Mount}} is for {{p|Christianity}}: a densely
Compressed articulation of the tradition's core vision, accessible
In isolation from the larger corpus and yet illuminating it
Entirely. This chapter of the {{x|Gaiad}} will treat the {{p|Mahabharata}}

In the same way the previous chapter treated the {{p|Ramayana}}: as
A singular event in the history of human narrative imagination,
Worthy of extended contemplation, standing at the center of
{{p|Indian}} civilization as Homer stands at the center of {{p|Greek}}

Civilization.

                  The traditional author is Vyasa—literally "the
Compiler"—a sage who is also a character in the epic itself.
Within the narrative, Vyasa is the biological grandfather of the

Main protagonists: he is the father of Dhritarashtra, Pandu, and
Vidura, whose lines generate the {{p|Kauravas}} and {{p|Pandavas}} whose
Conflict is the epic's central action. Vyasa witnesses the events
He then recounts. This recursive structure—in which the author is

Both teller and participant—is characteristic of the {{p|Mahabharata}}'s
Narrative complexity. It is a story that contains stories, that
Reflects on itself, that invites its reader into an endless
Hermeneutic process. Historically, of course, no single author

Produced the {{p|Mahabharata}}. It grew over centuries through multiple
Hands, probably beginning with a shorter core epic in the early
Centuries BCE and expanding through additions and interpolations
Until it reached something like its final form around four-hundred

CE. Its compositional history is as complex as any text in world
Literature. But traditional {{p|Hindu}} attribution gives the work to
Vyasa, and the {{x|Gaiad}} will use this convention for narrative ease.

The core story. The {{p|Kuru}} dynasty, ruling from {{p|Hastinapura}} near
What is now {{p|Delhi}}, is split between two branches of cousins: the
{{p|Pandavas}}, five brothers born to Pandu; and the {{p|Kauravas}}, one
Hundred brothers born to Dhritarashtra. Both groups have legitimate

Claims to the throne—Dhritarashtra was born first but is blind,
Which disqualifies him; Pandu therefore became king but died
Young. Now Dhritarashtra rules as regent for the generation of
Cousins, and tensions escalate between his son Duryodhana (the

Eldest {{p|Kaurava}}) and Yudhishthira (the eldest {{p|Pandava}}). The five
{{p|Pandava}} brothers are Yudhishthira (dharma-embodied, righteous,
The future rightful king), Bhima (enormously strong, impulsive,
Loyal), Arjuna (the greatest archer, a warrior of unmatched

Skill), Nakula and Sahadeva (twins, handsome, skilled in various
Arts). They share a single wife, Draupadi, who is won by
Arjuna in an archery contest and, through an unusual misunderstanding
Involving their mother Kunti, becomes wife to all five brothers.

This polyandrous marriage is one of the most famously anomalous
Features of the {{p|Mahabharata}}—it fits no standard {{p|Indian}} cultural
Pattern, and its presence in the epic has generated endless
Interpretation. The hundred {{p|Kauravas}}, led by Duryodhana, envy

And resent the {{p|Pandavas}}. A series of attempts on {{p|Pandava}} lives
Ensue—a house of lac built to be burned around them, from which
They barely escape; a poisoned sweet; various ambushes. The
{{p|Pandavas}} survive. Then comes the fatal dice game. Duryodhana,

With the aid of his uncle Shakuni, a skilled player with loaded
Dice, invites Yudhishthira to a gambling match. Yudhishthira,
Addicted to dice-play but committed to playing when invited,
Loses everything: his kingdom, his wealth, his brothers' freedom,

His own freedom, and finally Draupadi herself. She is dragged
Into the assembly hall, where the {{p|Kauravas}} attempt to strip her
Publicly—an act of extreme humiliation. At the last moment, the
God Krishna miraculously extends her sari to infinite length

So she cannot be disrobed. Her honor is preserved, but the
Humiliation is never forgotten. Eventually a compromise is reached:
The {{p|Pandavas}} will go into exile for twelve years, plus a
Thirteenth year in which they must remain incognito. If they

Complete this exile successfully, they will recover their kingdom.
They complete it. But Duryodhana refuses to honor the agreement.
He will not give back their kingdom. War becomes inevitable. The
Great {{p|Kurukshetra War}}—the climactic event of the epic—is fought

Between the {{p|Pandava}} and {{p|Kaurava}} coalitions over eighteen days.
Every major ruling house of {{p|Indian}} civilization takes sides. The
Carnage is apocalyptic. By the end, almost every major warrior is
Dead. Duryodhana, mortally wounded, lies dying on the battlefield.

All his brothers are dead. Most of the great teachers and heroes
Are dead. The victorious {{p|Pandavas}}, who have won but at unbearable
Cost, are left to survey a field of corpses that includes their
Uncles, cousins, teachers, friends. Yudhishthira is crowned king.

But the victory is ashes. He reigns for thirty-six years, then
Abdicates and walks with his brothers and Draupadi toward the
{{p|Himalayas}} in a final pilgrimage to death. One by one they fall,
Until only Yudhishthira and a dog that has accompanied them

Reach the peak. The dog, it turns out, is the god of dharma
Testing Yudhishthira. He passes the test by refusing to abandon
The dog. He enters heaven. The epic ends with the soul-wrenching
Final passages in which Yudhishthira faces the moral ambiguity

Of finding Duryodhana in heaven and his virtuous kinsmen
Apparently in hell—a revelation that turns out to be a final test
Of his equanimity. Reality is more complex than moral categories
Allow. The {{p|Mahabharata}}'s final word is not triumphant vindication

But sober recognition that righteousness does not guarantee
Happy outcomes and that moral judgments must always be held with
Humility before the complexity of the cosmos. This is a different
Ending from the {{p|Ramayana}}'s. The {{p|Ramayana}} ends with restoration;

The {{p|Mahabharata}} ends with ambiguity. Both endings carry deep
Wisdom.

          At the center of the epic, just before the battle begins,
Comes the {{p|Bhagavad Gita}}—"the {{x|Song of the Lord}}." Arjuna, looking

Across the battlefield at his cousins, his teachers, his kinsmen,
Is overcome by despair. How can he fight? How can he kill these
People he loves? His grief paralyzes him. He sinks down in his
Chariot, unwilling to fight. His charioteer—who is in fact the god

Krishna, an avatar of Vishnu serving voluntarily as Arjuna's
Driver—engages him in dialogue. Over seven hundred verses,
Krishna instructs Arjuna on the deepest principles of {{p|Hindu}}
Philosophy. The body dies but the soul does not; what Arjuna

Thinks he is killing is only the body, and the soul passes on.
Action in the world is necessary, but it must be performed
Without attachment to the fruits of action—performed as an offering
To Krishna, without grasping for reward or avoiding loss. The

Three paths to liberation are {{p|karma yoga}} (the path of action),
{{p|jnana yoga}} (the path of knowledge), and {{p|bhakti yoga}} (the path of
Devotion); all three are legitimate, and the {{p|Gita}} harmonizes them
Rather than privileging one. Krishna reveals his full divine form

Briefly to Arjuna, a vision so overwhelming that Arjuna can
Barely endure it—the god containing all worlds, all beings, all
Times in himself; devouring warriors past, present, and future;
The source from which all things emerge and to which all things

Return. And Arjuna, after this vision, is restored to action. He
Takes up his bow. The battle begins. This discourse—the {{p|Bhagavad Gita}}—
Is a philosophical and religious text of extraordinary power. It
Has been read and commented on for two millennia. Shankara, Ramanuja,

Madhva, and the other great classical {{p|Hindu}} philosophers each
Wrote major commentaries on it. In the modern period, Gandhi
Carried a copy with him constantly and considered it his spiritual
Guide. Thoreau and Emerson read it with wonder. Oppenheimer

Quoted from it at the first atomic test: "Now I am become Death,
Destroyer of worlds." Its influence on modern religious and
Philosophical thought, within and beyond {{p|India}}, is vast. And yet
It is only one section of the {{p|Mahabharata}}—a small section, perhaps

Two percent of the total. The {{p|Mahabharata}} as a whole is a larger
And more encompassing work. But the {{p|Gita}} is the concentrated
Jewel. Within a few hundred verses, it distills the philosophical
And devotional essence of {{p|Hindu}} thought. Understanding the {{p|Gita}}

Is understanding much about what {{p|Indian}} religious civilization
Has valued.

                 The {{p|Mahabharata}} treats {{p|dharma}}—righteous duty—
As its central concern. But it treats dharma in a much more

Complicated way than the {{p|Ramayana}} does. The {{p|Ramayana}}'s Rama
Embodies dharma with apparent simplicity: he always knows what
The right thing is and does it. The {{p|Mahabharata}}'s protagonists
Are not so clear. They face situations in which the right course

Of action is obscure, in which multiple dharmic obligations
Conflict, in which any choice produces some evil. This is called
{{p|dharmasankata}}, "the crisis of dharma," and the {{p|Mahabharata}} is
Full of it. Should Yudhishthira tell a lie that will win the

War? (Krishna urges him to.) Should Arjuna fight his teachers
And kinsmen? (Krishna tells him yes.) Should Karna—who is
Actually a {{p|Pandava}} by birth but was raised among {{p|Kauravas}}—side
With his biological brothers or with the benefactor who raised

Him? (He chooses his benefactor, at tragic cost.) Should Bhishma,
The grand old patriarch of the {{p|Kuru}} dynasty, fight against the
{{p|Pandavas}} whom he loves, because his oath of loyalty requires him
To fight for whoever holds the throne of {{p|Hastinapura}}? (He does,

And is killed by Arjuna.) The {{p|Mahabharata}} is full of such moral
Conundrums. It refuses to give easy answers. It shows dharma as
A genuinely difficult problem, subject to interpretation,
Contextual, and sometimes tragic. This is why it has remained so

Fertile for {{p|Indian}} moral imagination. Every generation finds in
It material for its own ethical reflection. Every commentator
Finds a new angle. Every adaptation brings out different themes.
The text is inexhaustible.

                               And consider its theology. Krishna,
Who appears as a character throughout the {{p|Mahabharata}} and
Centrally in the {{p|Bhagavad Gita}}, is an avatar of Vishnu—a
Descent of the supreme god into human form to participate in the

Historical drama. He is simultaneously fully human (with emotions,
Relationships, historical specificity) and fully divine (capable
Of miraculous interventions, possessor of cosmic knowledge,
Embodying the absolute). This doctrine of {{p|avatar}} ("descent")

Is one of the central features of {{p|Vaishnava}} {{p|Hindu}} theology.
Vishnu descends to earth periodically when cosmic order ({{p|dharma}})
Is threatened, to restore balance. Ten major {{p|avatars}} are
Traditionally recognized: the fish (Matsya), the tortoise (Kurma),

The boar (Varaha), the man-lion (Narasimha), the dwarf (Vamana),
Parashurama, Rama (of the {{p|Ramayana}}), Krishna (of the {{p|Mahabharata}}
And separately of the {{p|Bhagavata Purana}}'s stories of his childhood),
The Buddha (included here as an avatar, a classic {{p|Hindu}} move to

Absorb {{p|Buddhism}} into the {{p|Vaishnava}} framework), and Kalki (the
Future avatar who will appear at the end of the current dark age).
Each avatar embodies divine engagement with human history. Each
Represents a different form divine action can take. And the {{p|avatar}}

Doctrine has deep structural parallels to the {{p|Christian}} doctrine
Of incarnation, the {{p|Buddhist}} doctrine of bodhisattvas, and other
Religious conceptions of divine beings taking on embodied form for
The sake of sentient beings. These parallels are not identities,

But they are striking resemblances. The {{x|Gaiad}} reads them as
Different cultural elaborations of a common religious insight:
That divinity is not remote from human affairs but engages with
Them in various modes, sometimes taking on the limitations of

Embodied existence in order to be present among creatures.

                                                                 The
{{p|Mahabharata}} also contains, as subsidiary material, enormous
Amounts of philosophical, religious, and practical content. The

{{p|Shanti Parva}} ("{{x|Book of Peace}}") and the {{p|Anushasana Parva}} ("Book
Of Instruction")—two of the epic's eighteen books—are essentially
Compendia of moral and philosophical teaching delivered by the
Dying Bhishma to Yudhishthira about kingship, dharma, and

The good life. These sections alone are as long as major classical
{{p|Indian}} philosophical texts and contain substantial treatments of
Ethics, political theory, theology, and practical wisdom. The epic
Also preserves numerous side stories, genealogies, creation myths,

Proverbs, hymns. It functions, in part, as an encyclopedia of
Classical {{p|Hindu}} civilization. To know the {{p|Mahabharata}} deeply is
To know much of what traditional {{p|India}} knew about itself.

And the epic remains alive. {{p|Indian}} television produced a serialized

Adaptation in the late nineteen-eighties that was watched by
Hundreds of millions of viewers, grinding the country to a halt
Each Sunday morning. Multiple film versions have been made in
Various {{p|Indian}} languages. Comic books, children's retellings,

Dance performances, theatrical productions in every regional
Tradition—the {{p|Mahabharata}} is constantly being re-made. It is
Not a dead classical text. It is a living cultural substance that
{{p|Indians}} continue to think with, argue about, and return to for

Wisdom. The {{p|Bhagavad Gita}} is, for many {{p|Hindus}}, a daily spiritual
Companion. For all {{p|Hindus}}, the {{p|Mahabharata}}'s characters are
Familiar as family members—the dharmic Yudhishthira, the mighty
Bhima, the pure warrior Arjuna, the tragic Karna, the scheming

Shakuni, the proud Duryodhana, the suffering Draupadi, the
Wise Krishna, the oath-bound Bhishma. These figures populate
The moral imagination of {{p|Indian}} civilization as Achilles, Hector,
Odysseus, and Agamemnon populate the moral imagination of the

Classical {{p|Mediterranean}}. Except the {{p|Mahabharata}}'s characters are
More psychologically complex, the moral situations more ambiguous,
The philosophical depth greater, the span of narrative time and
Thematic concern vaster. If Homer's {{p|Iliad}} is the foundational

{{p|Greek}} text, the {{p|Mahabharata}} is the {{p|Indian}} equivalent raised to
A higher power. It is not just a great epic. It is the epic of
An entire civilization.

                             The {{x|Gaiad}} reads the {{p|Mahabharata}}—like
The {{p|Ramayana}}—as another major articulation of the shared
{{p|Indo-European}} narrative imagination, elaborated by the {{p|Indian}}
Branch into a work of singular and unrivaled scope. The {{p|Trojan War}}
Parallel is less direct here than in the {{p|Ramayana}}—there is no

Stolen queen, no siege of a fortified foreign city—but the
Structural parallel with the {{p|Iliad}} is still visible. Two related
Groups of aristocratic warriors, driven by pride and conflicting
Claims, fight an annihilating war that destroys most of the

Aristocratic class. The aftermath is not triumphant but mournful.
Both epics end with death and reflection rather than with
Victorious celebration. The {{p|Iliad}} closes with Priam ransoming
Hector's body; the {{p|Mahabharata}} closes with Yudhishthira's

Pilgrimage of death. Both are, fundamentally, works about the cost
Of heroic warfare and the moral ambiguity of human glory. The
{{p|Indo-European}} imagination produced, at opposite ends of its
Geographical spread, two of the supreme literary meditations on

The price of greatness.

                              The {{p|Mahabharata}}. {{p|Kurukshetra}}. The
{{p|Pandavas}} and the {{p|Kauravas}}. Yudhishthira, Bhima, Arjuna,
Nakula, Sahadeva. Draupadi. Duryodhana and the hundred brothers.

Bhishma on his bed of arrows. Karna the unrecognized brother.
Krishna the divine charioteer. The {{p|Bhagavad Gita}} at the heart of
The battle. The {{p|dharmasankata}}—the crisis of dharma—woven through
Every decision. The cosmic vision of Krishna's true form.

The hundred thousand verses. The longest poem in the world. The
Epic of an entire civilization.

{{p|Mahabharata}}. {{p|Indian}} reflex of the great {{p|Indo-European}} war epic.
The text that has shaped {{p|Hindu}} moral and religious imagination

For twenty-five hundred years.


# Chapter 172: Classical Greece, Part Two

Return now to {{p|Greece}}, where we left it at the end of the
{{p|Persian Wars}}. The invasions of Darius and Xerxes have been
Defeated. The {{p|Greek}} city-states, against unreasonable odds, have
Preserved their independence. And now, in the victorious glow,

{{p|Athens}} rises to a cultural ascendancy the world had not seen
Before and has rarely matched since. The fifth century BCE. The
Age of Pericles. The age of the great tragedians. The age of the
Sophists and Socrates. The age in which democracy, drama,

Philosophy, and history became what they have remained.

                                                            {{p|Athens}}
Emerged from the {{p|Persian Wars}} with enormous prestige. The naval
Victory at {{p|Salamis}} had been principally an {{p|Athenian}} victory—the

{{p|Athenian}} fleet was the largest contingent, commanded by the
{{p|Athenian}} Themistocles. {{p|Athens}} also had the distinction of being
The only major {{p|Greek}} city that had been physically destroyed by
The {{p|Persians}} (their first acts on return were to rebuild their

Burned city). The {{p|Athenians}} had therefore suffered more than
Most and contributed more than most, and they claimed the leading
Role in the subsequent {{p|Greek}} response. In four-seventy-eight
BCE, {{p|Athens}} formed the {{p|Delian League}}—a coalition of {{p|Greek}}

City-states, initially based on the sacred island of {{p|Delos}}, that
Would coordinate defense against any future {{p|Persian}} aggression.
The smaller members contributed ships or money; {{p|Athens}} provided
The central fleet and leadership. Over the next several decades,

The {{p|Delian League}} expanded. It conducted campaigns against
{{p|Persian}} holdings in the {{p|Aegean}} and liberated {{p|Greek}} cities
Still under {{p|Persian}} control. But over time, it became increasingly
Clear that the {{p|League}} was really an {{p|Athenian}} empire. Member cities

That tried to withdraw from the {{p|League}} were prevented from doing
So by {{p|Athenian}} military force. The {{p|League}} treasury, originally
At {{p|Delos}}, was moved to {{p|Athens}} in four-fifty-four BCE. {{p|Athens}}
Began using {{p|League}} funds for {{p|Athenian}} purposes—including the

Massive building program on the {{p|Acropolis}}. Member cities that
Had once contributed as equals became subject peoples contributing
Tribute to an imperial power. The {{p|Delian League}} had become, in
Effect, the {{p|Athenian Empire}}. This transformation generated deep

Resentment, especially in {{p|Sparta}} and her allies, who saw {{p|Athenian}}
Imperialism as a threat to {{p|Greek}} freedom (or at least to their
Own position). The tensions would eventually explode in the
{{p|Peloponnesian War}}. But before that war, there was the golden

Age. Between roughly four-sixty and four-thirty BCE, {{p|Athens}}
Experienced the most concentrated cultural efflorescence the
World had yet seen. The democratic constitution, reformed and
Extended, reached its most developed form under the leadership

Of Pericles. Male citizens—perhaps fifty thousand in a city of
Perhaps three hundred thousand total inhabitants—participated
Directly in the {{x|Assembly}}, served on juries, held public office
By lot. The {{x|Assembly}} met regularly on the {{p|Pnyx}} hill, where any

Citizen could speak. Executive functions were performed by boards
Of ten officials (one from each tribe, selected by lot or elected).
Judicial functions were performed by large citizen juries, often
Hundreds strong. Financial oversight, foreign policy, military

Strategy—all these were debated in public assembly and decided
By majority vote. The system was radical and, by the standards
Of most human political systems, surprisingly stable. Pericles
Himself was elected general ({{p|strategos}}) year after year for

Decades, giving him continuous leadership despite the formal
Constraints of democracy. His funeral oration for {{p|Athenian}} war
Dead, preserved by Thucydides (possibly in reconstructed form),
Is one of the supreme articulations of democratic ideology ever

Composed. "Our constitution is called a democracy because power
Is in the hands not of a minority but of the whole people." The
Periclean vision was of a city in which free citizens, governed
By laws they themselves had made, engaged openly in public life,

Celebrated beauty and culture, and defended their freedom against
Enemies both external and internal. That vision was real, even
Though imperfect. {{p|Athenian}} democracy did have limits—slaves,
Women, {{p|metics}} (resident foreigners) were excluded. The empire

That funded the cultural glory rested on coercion of other
{{p|Greek}} states. But within these limits, the {{p|Athenian}} political
Experiment was revolutionary. It was the first substantial
Democracy in history, and its self-conception would inspire

Democratic movements for twenty-five centuries afterward.

                                                                And
Under this democratic constitution, the cultural achievements
Came. The theatrical festivals—the {{p|Dionysia}} in particular—were

Civic institutions in which citizens competed in the production
Of tragedies and comedies. Playwrights wrote for these festivals
Plays that would be performed once and (if they were lucky)
Awarded a prize. The competitive structure produced extraordinary

Artistic energy. Out of this system came the three great tragedians
Whose work survives: Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Each
Represents a different stage and sensibility in the development
Of {{p|Greek}} drama. Aeschylus (five-twenty-five to four-fifty-six

BCE), the earliest, wrote plays of mythic grandeur in which the
Characters are larger than life and the themes cosmic. His
{{p|Oresteia}} trilogy—{{p|Agamemnon}}, {{p|Libation Bearers}}, {{p|Eumenides}}—
Is the only complete tragic trilogy to survive from antiquity.

It tells the story of the {{p|House of Atreus}}: Agamemnon's return
From {{p|Troy}}, his murder by his wife Clytemnestra, the revenge of
Their son Orestes, and finally the trial of Orestes before a
Court established by the goddess Athena to resolve the blood-

Vengeance cycle. The trilogy ends with the transformation of the
Furies into the Eumenides ("the kindly ones"), marking the
Founding of civic justice to replace tribal revenge. It is a
Meditation on the evolution of human civilization itself, from

Primal retribution to institutional law. Sophocles (four-ninety-six
To four-oh-six BCE), the middle tragedian, wrote plays in which
Individual characters of extraordinary moral weight struggle
Against inexorable fate. His seven surviving plays include

{{p|Oedipus Rex}}, {{p|Oedipus at Colonus}}, {{p|Antigone}}, and {{p|Electra}}. The
Figure of Oedipus—the king who solved the riddle of the Sphinx
But was blind to the riddle of his own life, who had murdered
His father and married his mother without knowing it, who

Discovered the truth and blinded himself in horror—is one of the
Permanent archetypes of {{p|Western}} tragedy. Sigmund Freud would,
In the twentieth century CE, adopt Oedipus's name for the
Central complex of his psychological theory. The figure is that

Powerful. Antigone, who defies the king's decree in order to
Bury her brother according to divine law, embodies the conflict
Between political authority and moral conscience in a form that
Has never been surpassed. She is one of the great heroes of

Civil disobedience, an ancestor of every subsequent figure who
Has insisted that there is a higher law than the law of the state.
Euripides (four-eighty to four-oh-six BCE), the youngest, wrote
Plays that were more psychologically modern, more skeptical of

Traditional values, more concerned with the suffering of marginal
Characters—women, slaves, the defeated. His {{p|Medea}}, {{p|Bacchae}},
{{p|Trojan Women}}, and {{p|Hippolytus}} are harrowing examinations of
Human passion and institutional violence. Euripides was less

Popular in his own time than Sophocles but came to be regarded
In later antiquity as perhaps the greatest of the three. His
Willingness to show ugly emotions, to question pieties, to give
Voice to the voiceless, made him a model for subsequent dramatic

Traditions. Together, these three playwrights established the
Terms of tragic drama. Every subsequent tragedy—Shakespeare's,
Racine's, Ibsen's, O'Neill's—stands in relation to them. The
Category of tragedy itself as a literary form is their invention.

And alongside tragedy, comedy. Aristophanes (four-forty-six to
Three-eighty-six BCE) wrote comedies of savage political satire
And bawdy humor that are as alive today as anything from antiquity.
His {{p|Lysistrata}}, in which the women of {{p|Athens}} refuse sexual

Relations with their husbands until the men stop fighting the
{{p|Peloponnesian War}}, has been revived countless times as an
Anti-war statement. His {{p|Clouds}} savagely mocks Socrates and the
{{p|Sophists}}, presenting Socrates as a ridiculous charlatan floating

In a basket and teaching his students to make the worse argument
Seem better. The play is unfair to Socrates but brilliantly
Funny, and Socrates, for what it is worth, attended the premiere
And stood up in the audience so the crowd could compare the

Real philosopher to Aristophanes's caricature. It is the kind
Of anecdote that captures {{p|Athens}} at its most characteristic:
Intensely public, intellectually ferocious, with playwrights and
Philosophers all part of the same small, argumentative city.

                                                                   And
Sculpture and architecture. Phidias, the supreme sculptor of the
Age, executed the Athena Parthenos for the {{p|Parthenon}}—a forty-
Foot gold-and-ivory statue of the goddess holding a smaller figure

Of Nike in her hand. The statue is lost, but descriptions and
Small replicas survive. He also sculpted the Zeus at {{p|Olympia}},
One of the seven wonders of the ancient world. Phidias's
Students and imitators produced much of the sculptural work that

Has come to define {{p|Greek}} classical art: the balance of idealized
Beauty and restrained emotion, the sense of bodies as mathematically
Perfect yet infused with vital presence. The {{p|Parthenon}} itself,
Built on the {{p|Acropolis}} between four-forty-seven and four-thirty-two

BCE under Pericles's sponsorship, designed by Ictinus and Callicrates
With sculptural supervision by Phidias, was the supreme
Architectural achievement of classical antiquity. Its Doric
Columns, subtly curved to correct optical distortions, its

Sculpted metopes depicting mythic battles, its pediments with
Gods and heroes, its interior frieze showing the {{p|Panathenaic}}
Procession—every aspect was a calibration of proportion and
Artistic meaning. The {{p|Parthenon}} influenced {{p|Western}} architecture

For the next two and a half millennia. The classical column orders,
The symmetrical pediment, the mathematical harmony of proportions—
All of this became the vocabulary of {{p|Western}} monumental building.
Courthouses, museums, banks, legislative buildings—the form

Invented by Ictinus on the {{p|Acropolis}} would be replicated in
{{p|Washington}}, {{p|London}}, {{p|Paris}}, and everywhere else that aspired
To classical dignity. The {{p|Parthenon}} was not just a building. It
Was a template.

                   And now to the philosophical revolution. We
Discussed the {{p|pre-Socratics}} in the previous {{p|Greek}} chapter. In
The second half of the fifth century BCE, philosophy took a
New turn. The {{p|Sophists}}—traveling teachers of rhetoric and

Wisdom—came to {{p|Athens}} and offered instruction for fees. Men like
Protagoras of Abdera (famous for declaring "Man is the measure
Of all things"—a relativist proposition), Gorgias of Leontini (a
Brilliant rhetorician), and Hippias of Elis (a polymath) taught

Wealthy young {{p|Athenians}} the techniques of public speaking and
Argument that were essential for political success in the
Democratic assembly. The {{p|Sophists}} were influential but also
Controversial. Their willingness to teach both sides of any

Argument (and thereby, critics said, to teach that truth is
Whatever you can make people accept) made them seem morally
Dubious. Their fees made them seem mercenary. And their radical
Philosophical positions—especially the relativism and skepticism

Of Protagoras—challenged traditional values. Into this intellectual
Ferment stepped Socrates. Socrates (four-seventy to three-ninety-nine
BCE) was an {{p|Athenian}} citizen, apparently of modest means, who
Spent his days engaging other citizens in philosophical conversation

In the public spaces of the city. He did not charge fees. He did
Not teach any positive doctrine. He claimed only that he knew
Nothing—and that this knowledge of his own ignorance was itself
A kind of wisdom that his interlocutors usually lacked. His

Method was dialogue: he would ask seemingly innocent questions
About important topics (what is justice? what is courage? what is
Piety?), and his interlocutors would confidently offer definitions,
And Socrates would then examine those definitions until their

Inadequacies were exposed. The interlocutor, initially confident
Of his knowledge, would come to realize that he did not know
What he had thought he knew. This is the {{p|Socratic method}}, and
It is one of the most influential pedagogical techniques ever

Developed. It assumes that genuine knowledge is rare, that most
Human "knowledge" is actually uncritical opinion, and that the
Path to real understanding requires rigorous examination of our
Beliefs. Socrates took this method as a divine mission. He

Believed he had been charged by the gods with the task of examining
Himself and others, helping them to recognize their ignorance
And thereby opening the possibility of genuine wisdom. He pursued
This mission relentlessly for decades. In the process, he made

Many enemies. The politically powerful did not enjoy being publicly
Exposed as ignorant. The pious did not enjoy having their religious
Assumptions questioned. In three-ninety-nine BCE, Socrates was
Formally charged with impiety (failing to worship the proper gods

And introducing new gods) and with corrupting the youth of {{p|Athens}}.
He was tried by a jury of five hundred citizens. He defended
Himself not by minimizing his activity but by insisting that his
Philosophical mission was a gift to the city. The jury, unimpressed,

Found him guilty. When asked what penalty he should receive, he
Suggested that the city should honor him at public expense for
The services he had rendered. The jury sentenced him to death by
Hemlock. His trial and execution, described in Plato's {{p|Apology}},

{{p|Crito}}, and {{p|Phaedo}}, became one of the foundational narratives
Of {{p|Western}} intellectual history. The philosopher, calmly drinking
Poison rather than abandoning his mission, became the archetype
Of the thinker who will die for truth. His final words, as he

Felt the hemlock's numbness climbing his legs, were reportedly
A request that his friend Crito pay a small debt: "Crito, we
Owe a rooster to Asclepius. Pay it, don't forget." A pedestrian
Comment, perhaps, but somehow perfect—the philosopher's last

Thought was of a routine obligation, not of himself.

                                                         Socrates
Wrote nothing. What we know of him comes primarily from two of
His students: Plato and Xenophon. Plato (four-twenty-seven to

Three-forty-seven BCE) was by far the more philosophically
Significant. He founded the {{p|Academy}}—the first institution we
Might call a university—and wrote dozens of philosophical dialogues
In which Socrates typically appears as the main speaker. The

Relationship between the historical Socrates and Plato's literary
Socrates is debated; in the earlier dialogues Plato probably
Preserves his teacher's views, while in later dialogues he uses
The figure of Socrates to present his own developed philosophy.

Plato's philosophical system—the theory of the forms, the tripartite
Soul, the philosopher-king, the immortality of the soul—is one of
The most influential in human history. It would shape {{p|Neoplatonism}},
{{p|Christian}} theology, {{p|Islamic}} philosophy, and countless later

Intellectual movements. Alfred North Whitehead, in the twentieth
Century CE, famously remarked that all subsequent {{p|Western}}
Philosophy is "a series of footnotes to Plato"—an overstatement,
But capturing something important. And Plato's student Aristotle

(Three-eighty-four to three-twenty-two BCE) would take Plato's
Philosophical project in a different direction, more empirical,
More systematic, more concerned with the study of concrete things
Rather than abstract forms. Aristotle's treatises on logic, physics,

Biology, psychology, ethics, politics, rhetoric, and poetics would
Define the disciplines of philosophy and science for nearly two
Thousand years. Medieval {{p|Islamic}} and {{p|Christian}} thinkers would
Call him simply "the Philosopher." His works, preserved and

Transmitted through {{p|Byzantine}} and {{p|Arabic}} scholars, would shape
The intellectual revival of medieval {{p|Europe}}. He would be student,
In turn, to Alexander the Great—linking the philosophical tradition
Of Socrates directly to the {{p|Macedonian}} conquest that would spread

{{p|Hellenistic}} culture across three continents. But we are, again,
Getting ahead of ourselves. In the span of this chapter, the
Classical age is still unfolding. We will return to Aristotle and
Alexander in the next chapter.

                                   And meanwhile, the {{p|Peloponnesian War}}.
The tensions between {{p|Athens}} and {{p|Sparta}} that had been building
For decades finally exploded in four-thirty-one BCE. For the next
Twenty-seven years, the {{p|Greek}} world was convulsed by war.

Pericles died of plague in four-twenty-nine BCE, leaving {{p|Athens}}
Without strategic leadership in the war's middle phase. {{p|Athens}}
Made disastrous military blunders—most catastrophically the
{{p|Sicilian Expedition}} of four-fifteen to four-thirteen BCE, in

Which an enormous {{p|Athenian}} fleet and army were annihilated in
An attempt to conquer {{p|Syracuse}}. {{p|Athens}} never fully recovered.
In four-oh-four BCE, after a final naval defeat, {{p|Athens}}
Surrendered. The city's long walls were torn down. The democratic

Constitution was briefly replaced by an oligarchy (the {{p|Thirty Tyrants}}),
Though democracy was soon restored. The {{p|Athenian}} empire ended.
{{p|Sparta}} was the nominal victor, but the war had exhausted all the
{{p|Greek}} states. The golden age of classical {{p|Greece}} ended with

This war. The {{p|Greek}} cities would never again be so powerful or
So creative. They would eventually fall under the rule of
{{p|Macedon}} to the north. And while some later cultural achievements
Would still come—Aristotle's work, the {{p|Hellenistic}} diffusion

Of {{p|Greek}} culture—the specifically classical city-state
Civilization was over. Its distinctive creativity had been the
Product of a specific political moment: free citizens of small
Competitive cities, engaged in direct democratic politics, with

Enough wealth from empire to support cultural production and
Enough freedom from external domination to think and argue openly.
That moment lasted about a century. It produced more foundational
Cultural achievements than any other comparable period in human

History. And then it was gone.

                                     Classical {{p|Greece}}, part two.
The {{p|Delian League}} and the {{p|Athenian Empire}}. Pericles's
Leadership. Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes—

The great playwrights. Phidias and the {{p|Parthenon}}. The {{p|Sophists}}
And Socrates. Plato and the {{p|Academy}}. Aristotle in the wings.
The {{p|Peloponnesian War}} that destroyed everything. The supreme
Concentration of cultural achievement in one small city in one

Short century.

                      Classical {{p|Athens}}. The fifth-century
Civilization that taught the {{p|West}} what cities, what democracies,
What philosophy, and what theater could be.


# Chapter 173: Classical Greece, Part Three

Take up the thread again. The fifth century BCE has ended. {{p|Athens}}
Has lost the {{p|Peloponnesian War}}. The golden age of classical
{{p|Greece}} is over. But {{p|Greek}} civilization does not end in four-oh-four
BCE. It continues through the fourth century BCE with a different

Character. Less politically dominant, certainly—the city-states
Are weaker, the cultural energy is more diffuse. But in some
Ways more philosophically rigorous, more systematically organized,
More intellectually mature. And the fourth century would produce

The two philosophers whose influence would eclipse even that of
Their {{p|Athenian}} predecessors: Plato and Aristotle. And it would
Produce Alexander the Great, who would take {{p|Greek}} civilization
Itself, via conquest, across three continents.

                                                    Begin with Plato
In his mature phase. The {{x|Gaiad}} introduced him in the previous
Chapter as Socrates's student and the founder of the {{p|Academy}}.
His early dialogues preserve, or appear to preserve, Socrates's

Method and characteristic concerns: questioning people about
Ethical terms (justice, courage, piety), exposing their confident
Ignorance, leaving the conversation without arriving at firm
Definitions. These "elenctic" dialogues—{{p|Euthyphro}}, {{p|Laches}},

{{p|Charmides}}, parts of {{p|Gorgias}}—are small masterpieces of
Philosophical drama. Then come the middle dialogues, in which
Plato begins to develop his own philosophical positions. {{p|Republic}},
{{p|Phaedo}}, {{p|Symposium}}, {{p|Phaedrus}}. These are the works most commonly

Associated with "{{p|Platonism}}." They present the theory of the
{{x|Forms}}—the claim that behind every particular instance of a
Quality (this beautiful statue, this just action, this triangular
Shape) lies an ideal {{x|Form}} (Beauty itself, Justice itself, the

Triangle itself) that exists more truly and eternally than any
Material instantiation. The {{x|Forms}} are apprehended by reason
Rather than sense. They constitute ultimate reality. And the
Philosopher's task is to ascend from the world of particulars to

The contemplation of the {{x|Forms}}—a process metaphorically depicted
In the {{p|Allegory of the Cave}}, perhaps the most famous passage
In Plato's corpus. Most people, Plato writes, are like prisoners
Chained in a cave, watching shadows on the wall and believing

Them to be reality. The philosopher is the one who escapes the
Cave, climbs up into the sunlight, sees things as they really
Are, and then—painfully—returns to the cave to try to educate
The other prisoners about the truth. The philosopher's task is

To drag others out of illusion into knowledge, despite their
Resistance. This allegory has obvious autobiographical resonance:
Plato's teacher Socrates was executed for attempting just such
An educational mission. It also has political implications.

Plato's {{p|Republic}} argues that ideal political societies would
Be ruled by philosopher-kings—by those who had made the
Ascending journey to the {{x|Forms}} and therefore knew what was truly
Good, just, and beautiful. Democracy, Plato argued, produces only

The rule of opinion and self-interest; it elevates demagogues
Rather than wise rulers. The ideal city would have a strict
Social hierarchy: philosophers at the top, warrior-guardians
Below them, and ordinary producers (farmers, craftsmen, traders)

At the base, each performing the function for which they were
Best suited. This is a profoundly anti-democratic political
Philosophy, and it is worth noting that Plato wrote it after
His teacher had been executed by an {{p|Athenian}} democratic jury.

Plato's political vision should be read in light of that trauma.
He did not trust democratic judgment. He wanted the wise to rule.
His vision has influenced authoritarian and elitist political
Traditions across the centuries—sometimes for better (elite

Expertise in technocratic government) and sometimes for worse
(The various "philosopher kings" of political history tend, in
Practice, to be tyrants). The late dialogues—{{p|Parmenides}}, {{p|Sophist}},
{{p|Statesman}}, {{p|Philebus}}, {{p|Laws}}, {{p|Timaeus}}—show Plato revising

And complicating his earlier positions. In {{p|Parmenides}}, he
Subjects his own theory of the {{x|Forms}} to searching self-criticism.
In {{p|Laws}}, his last great work, he abandons the philosopher-king
Ideal and describes a more practical constitutional framework

With extensive legal codification. In {{p|Timaeus}}, he offers a
Cosmological speculation about the structure of the universe—
A text that would prove enormously influential on later
{{p|Neoplatonism}}, {{p|Christian}} theology, and even early modern science.

Plato's work is vast, varied, and endlessly generative. It would
Be the single most-read philosophical corpus in human history
Until the late modern period. And the {{p|Academy}} he founded in
{{p|Athens}} continued to operate, in various forms, for nearly nine

Centuries—until the {{p|Christian}} emperor Justinian closed it in
Five-twenty-nine CE. It was the longest-lived institution of
Higher learning in antiquity, and its closure symbolically marked
The end of classical {{p|Greek}} philosophy's institutional continuity.

                                                                       Into
The {{p|Academy}} as a young student came a remarkable {{p|Macedonian}}:
Aristotle (three-eighty-four to three-twenty-two BCE), born in
{{p|Stagira}} in northern {{p|Greece}}, son of the court physician to the

{{p|Macedonian}} royal family. He studied with Plato for twenty years,
Until Plato's death. He was passed over for leadership of the
{{p|Academy}} and left {{p|Athens}}. He spent several years traveling and
Studying—on the island of {{p|Lesbos}}, where he conducted pioneering

Biological research; at the court of {{p|Assos}}, where he worked
With other philosophers. Then, around three-forty-three BCE, he
Was summoned back to {{p|Macedon}} to tutor the young Alexander,
Heir to the {{p|Macedonian}} throne. For several years Aristotle

Served as the prince's teacher—one of the most extraordinary
Pedagogical relationships in history, though we have only
Fragmentary evidence of what Aristotle actually taught Alexander
Or how it influenced him. After Alexander's accession, Aristotle

Returned to {{p|Athens}} and founded his own school, the {{p|Lyceum}} (also
Called the {{p|Peripatetic}} school, from the covered walkway where
Teaching took place). He spent the next twelve years at the {{p|Lyceum}},
Producing and teaching from an enormous body of philosophical

And scientific work. Upon Alexander's death in three-twenty-three
BCE, anti-{{p|Macedonian}} sentiment in {{p|Athens}} turned against
Aristotle (who was seen as too close to the {{p|Macedonian}} court).
He fled to {{p|Chalcis}}, where he died the following year.

                                                               Aristotle's
Surviving works—mostly in the form of lecture notes rather than
Polished treatises—cover nearly the entire range of ancient
Knowledge. Logic (the {{p|Organon}}—{{p|Categories}}, {{p|On Interpretation}},

{{p|Prior Analytics}}, {{p|Posterior Analytics}}, {{p|Topics}}, {{p|Sophistical Refutations}}).
Natural philosophy (the {{p|Physics}}, {{p|On the Heavens}}, {{p|Meteorology}},
{{p|On Generation and Corruption}}). Biology (the {{p|History of Animals}},
{{p|Parts of Animals}}, {{p|Movement of Animals}}, {{p|Generation of Animals}}).

Psychology (the {{p|De Anima}} and the {{p|Parva Naturalia}}). {{x|Metaphysics}}
(The work that gave us the very term "metaphysics"—literally
"After the physics," because it was placed after the physical
Works in the ancient edition). Ethics (the {{p|Nicomachean Ethics}} and

The {{p|Eudemian Ethics}}). Politics. Rhetoric. {{x|Poetics}}. Each of these
Works would be foundational for its respective discipline.
Aristotle's influence on the development of {{p|Western}} thought is,
Along with Plato's and Augustine's, one of the three or four

Most important. His logic would remain essentially unchanged for
Two thousand years, until Frege and Russell in the late
Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries CE developed modern
Formal logic. His biology would remain the foundation of natural

History until Darwin. His ethical theory—virtue ethics grounded
In the concept of the {{p|telos}} or end of human life, flourishing
({{p|eudaimonia}}) as the realization of human potential—would
Structure moral philosophy until the early modern period, and

Would be revived in the late twentieth century by philosophers
Like Alasdair MacIntyre and Martha Nussbaum as an alternative
To Kantian and utilitarian approaches. His {{p|Poetics}} would define
The terms of literary theory through the {{p|Renaissance}}—the concepts

Of mimesis, catharsis, unity of action, tragic hero, the three
Unities—are all Aristotelian. And his {{p|Metaphysics}} would be the
Primary template for ontological inquiry in both the {{p|Christian}}
And {{p|Islamic}} philosophical traditions. Medieval {{p|Muslim}} philosophers

Like al-Farabi and Averroes produced extensive commentaries on
Aristotle. Medieval {{p|Christian}} theologians like Thomas Aquinas
Constructed their entire theological systems as syntheses of
Aristotle with {{p|Christian}} revelation. Dante placed Aristotle at

The head of the philosophers in {{p|Limbo}}, "the master of those
Who know." For seven hundred years, across multiple civilizations,
Aristotle was the Philosopher. His dominance was so complete
That the beginning of the modern period was partly defined as

The overthrow of Aristotelian authority—Galileo, Descartes,
Bacon all had to break with Aristotle to establish their new
Science and philosophy. Breaking with him took effort because
His systematic coverage was so complete. He had provided

Answers—plausible answers—to so many questions that moving beyond
Him required developing entirely new frameworks. The effort to
Do so is substantially the effort that produced modern thought.
And even now, in philosophical ethics, in biology, in logic,

In literary theory, Aristotle's work continues to be read and
Engaged with. He is not a closed book. He is a living interlocutor.

And alongside Plato and Aristotle, the fourth century produced
Other major philosophical schools. {{p|Cynicism}}, founded by Antisthenes

And made famous by Diogenes of Sinope, taught radical simplicity,
Self-sufficiency, and contempt for social conventions. Diogenes,
Who lived in a large barrel in the marketplace of {{p|Athens}} and
Made a career of shocking social propriety, was the archetype of

The philosophical provocateur. When Alexander the Great reportedly
Visited him and asked if there was anything he could do for the
Great philosopher, Diogenes supposedly replied, "Yes, stand a
Little out of my sunlight." The story is almost certainly apocryphal,

But it captures the {{p|Cynic}} ethos: the world's greatest conqueror
Has no power over the philosopher who has chosen to need nothing.
{{p|Cynic}} philosophy would evolve into {{p|Stoicism}}, which we will
Meet in a later chapter. {{p|Skepticism}}, founded by Pyrrho of Elis

(Who, by tradition, traveled with Alexander's expedition to {{p|India}}
And was influenced by {{p|Indian}} philosophy he encountered there),
Taught the suspension of judgment on all claims to knowledge.
The {{p|Pyrrhonist}} argued that for every proposition one might

Believe, equally persuasive arguments could be offered on either
Side, and therefore the wise course was to withhold assent and
Live in tranquil indifference. This radical epistemological
Position would influence {{p|Hellenistic}} philosophy and later

Intellectual traditions. {{p|Epicureanism}}, founded by Epicurus
(Three-forty-one to two-seventy BCE, working at the very edge of
Our fourth-century frame), taught that pleasure—understood as
Freedom from physical pain and mental disturbance ({{p|ataraxia}})—

Was the highest good. Epicurus's philosophy was a materialistic,
Atomistic view of nature derived from Democritus, combined with
An ethical program aimed at serene happiness through simple
Living, friendship, and the avoidance of needless fears (especially

Fear of the gods and fear of death). {{p|Epicureanism}} would flourish
For centuries and be given classical expression by the {{p|Roman}}
Poet Lucretius in the first century BCE. The school survived
Until late antiquity before being suppressed by {{p|Christianity}}.

These are only the most prominent schools. The philosophical
Diversity of fourth-century {{p|Greece}} was astonishing. It was a
Landscape of competing intellectual traditions, each with its
Own masters, its own texts, its own disciples, its own practices.

And it would continue to be so for centuries. The {{p|Hellenistic}}
And {{p|Roman}} philosophical traditions all emerged from this fourth-
Century matrix.

                    And now, Alexander. We will devote a full
Chapter to him shortly. Here we note only that his career
Belongs, in certain senses, to the end of classical {{p|Greece}}. He
Was born in {{p|Macedon}}—a kingdom on the northern periphery of the
{{p|Greek}} world, whose rulers claimed {{p|Greek}} descent but who were

Considered half-barbarian by the southern {{p|poleis}}. His father
Philip II had transformed {{p|Macedon}} from a peripheral kingdom
Into the dominant power of the {{p|Greek}} world, defeating a
Coalition of southern {{p|Greek}} cities at the {{p|Battle of Chaeronea}}

In three-thirty-eight BCE. With this victory, the independence
Of the {{p|Greek}} city-states effectively ended. They remained
Nominally sovereign but were forced into the {{p|League of Corinth}}
Under {{p|Macedonian}} hegemony. When Philip was assassinated in

Three-thirty-six BCE, the twenty-year-old Alexander inherited
The throne and the {{p|League}}. He suppressed early rebellions, then
Turned his attention to the {{p|Persian}} Empire. The campaign Philip
Had planned to launch against {{p|Persia}} became Alexander's campaign.

And from three-thirty-four to three-twenty-three BCE, Alexander
Would conquer an empire larger than anything before. But that is
For the next chapter. Here we note the philosophical-political
Context: classical {{p|Greece}} as a civilization of independent

City-states ended with Philip's victory and Alexander's
Inheritance. What followed was the {{p|Hellenistic}} world—{{p|Greek}}
Culture as an imperial phenomenon, spreading across three
Continents through Alexander's conquests, but no longer the

Distinctive city-state civilization that had produced Sophocles
And Socrates. The {{p|Hellenistic}} age would be extraordinarily
Productive in its own way—scientific advance, literary innovation,
Religious syncretism, philosophical elaboration—but it would be

A different kind of civilization. Larger, more cosmopolitan,
More bureaucratic, less politically intense. The specific conditions
That had produced classical culture—small free cities, direct
Democratic politics, intense civic competition—were gone, and they

Would not return.

                          So the {{x|Gaiad}} closes this three-part
Treatment of classical {{p|Greece}}. We began with the rise from the
Dark Age, the early philosophers, the {{p|Persian Wars}}. We continued

With the golden age of fifth-century {{p|Athens}}, Pericles, the
Tragedians, Socrates. We end here with the fourth-century
Maturation—Plato, Aristotle, the philosophical schools, the
End of city-state independence under {{p|Macedonian}} hegemony. Three

Hundred years from the first tentative philosophical speculations
Of Thales in the sixth century BCE to Aristotle's death in
Three-twenty-two BCE. Three hundred years in which a small region
Of the {{p|Mediterranean}} produced more foundational cultural

Achievements than any other comparable area in any other comparable
Period. Democracy, philosophy, drama, history, classical
Architecture, classical sculpture, the foundations of mathematics
And natural science—all of these, or their decisive {{p|Western}}

Articulations, came out of this three-century {{p|Greek}} efflorescence.
It is difficult to explain why. Many have tried. The usual
Answers—political freedom, competitive pluralism, leisure class
Supported by slave labor, favorable geography, contact with older

Civilizations—are all partial explanations but none is fully
Satisfying. The {{p|Greek}} achievement remains, in the last analysis,
Somewhat mysterious. It was the happy conjunction of many factors
In a particular place and time, and it produced results that

Continue to shape our civilization twenty-five hundred years later.

                                                                     And
The {{x|Gaiad}} honors this achievement without idealizing it. Classical
{{p|Greece}} was also a slaveholding society, a misogynistic society,

A society that exploited its imperial subjects, a society whose
Achievements were limited to a small elite of free adult males.
Every student who stood at the feet of Plato or Aristotle was
Supported by slave labor. Every citizen who voted in the {{p|Athenian}}

Assembly did so while women, foreigners, and slaves were excluded.
These facts must be acknowledged. They do not erase the
Achievements, but they do qualify them. Classical {{p|Greek}} democracy
Was real democracy—for those who counted. Classical {{p|Greek}} reason

Was real reason—developed within a society that depended on forms
Of domination invisible to it. The subsequent history of {{p|Western}}
Civilization has been, in part, the project of extending the
{{p|Greek}} ideals to groups the {{p|Greeks}} themselves excluded—of

Universalizing the logic of democracy and reason to all humans.
That project continues. And in that project, we are still working
With the {{p|Greek}} inheritance. We are still debating the same
Foundational questions: what is justice? What is the best regime?

What is the nature of the good life? What can we know? How should
We live? These questions were articulated in classical {{p|Greece}}
With a clarity they had never before possessed. They have remained
Central to {{p|Western}} intellectual life ever since.

{{p|Greece}}, part three. Plato and the {{p|Academy}}. Aristotle and the
{{p|Lyceum}}. {{p|Cynics}}, {{p|Skeptics}}, {{p|Epicureans}}. The end of city-state
Independence at {{p|Chaeronea}}. The {{p|Macedonian}} hegemony. The

Transition to the {{p|Hellenistic}} world. The closing of the classical
Age.

         The {{p|Greek}} achievement: foundational, flawed, immortal.


# Chapter 174: Alexander the Great

Alexander III of Macedon. Thirty-two years old at his death. In
Thirteen years of campaigning—from his accession in three-thirty-six
BCE to his death in three-twenty-three BCE—he conquered the
Largest contiguous land empire the world had ever seen. From

{{p|Greece}} across the {{p|Hellespont}}, through {{p|Anatolia}}, into {{p|Syria}} and
{{p|Palestine}}, south into {{p|Egypt}}, east across {{p|Mesopotamia}} to {{p|Persia}}
And beyond, all the way to the {{p|Indus River}} in what is now
{{p|Pakistan}}—Alexander's armies marched and fought and won. The

{{p|Persian}} Empire, which had stood for two centuries as the largest
Political entity on earth, collapsed before him in a few years.
And when he died—of fever, in {{p|Babylon}}, possibly complicated by
Malaria or typhoid or assassination by poison (the cause is still

Debated)—he left behind an empire that his generals would fight
Over for decades but that would never again be unified. The
{{p|Hellenistic}} kingdoms that emerged from his conquests—the {{p|Ptolemaic}}
In {{p|Egypt}}, the {{p|Seleucid}} in the {{p|Near East}}, the {{p|Antigonid}} in

{{p|Macedon}}—would dominate the {{p|Mediterranean}} and {{p|Near East}} for the
Next three centuries, until their gradual absorption into the
{{p|Roman}} empire. And along the way, {{p|Greek}} language and culture would
Spread across a vast territory, creating the cosmopolitan

{{p|Hellenistic}} civilization in which all the subsequent developments
Of antiquity—{{p|Christianity}}'s rise, the translation of the
{{p|Hebrew Bible}} into {{p|Greek}}, the {{p|Stoic}} and {{p|Epicurean}} philosophies,
The mathematics of Euclid and Archimedes—would take place. The

{{p|Hellenistic}} world is Alexander's legacy.

                                                 His background.
{{p|Macedon}} was a kingdom in northern {{p|Greece}}, ethnically and
Linguistically {{p|Greek}} but culturally distinct from the southern

City-states. The {{p|Macedonians}} spoke {{p|Greek}} with a distinctive dialect,
Retained archaic practices like warrior-kings and extensive
Drinking feasts, and were regarded by the southern {{p|Greeks}} as
Rough cousins rather than proper peers. Alexander's father Philip II

Came to the throne in three-fifty-nine BCE and in less than a
Generation transformed {{p|Macedon}} from a weak periphery into the
Dominant power of the {{p|Greek}} world. Philip reorganized the army
Around the {{p|Macedonian phalanx}}—a formation of heavy infantry with

Enormously long pikes ({{p|sarissas}}) held in coordinated ranks,
Combined with heavy and light cavalry and various specialized
Troops. This {{p|combined-arms}} approach was more sophisticated than
The hoplite warfare of the southern {{p|Greeks}}, which had relied

Primarily on phalanx against phalanx. Philip's army could handle
More varied enemies in more varied terrain. He used it to defeat
{{p|Thrace}}, {{p|Illyria}}, and eventually the southern {{p|Greeks}} at the
{{p|Battle of Chaeronea}} in three-thirty-eight BCE. After {{p|Chaeronea}},

Philip organized the {{p|League of Corinth}}, with most of the {{p|Greek}}
States as members under {{p|Macedonian}} leadership. He announced his
Intention to launch a war against {{p|Persia}}—framed as a pan-{{p|Greek}}
Retaliation for the invasions of a century and a half earlier.

Preparations were well underway when, in three-thirty-six BCE,
Philip was assassinated at his daughter's wedding by one of his
Bodyguards. The motives remain unclear—personal grievance,
Political conspiracy, or perhaps the involvement of Alexander

Himself or his mother Olympias (rumors of which circulated even
In antiquity). Whatever the cause, Philip was dead and his twenty-
Year-old son Alexander inherited the throne, the army, and the
Planned {{p|Persian}} campaign.

                                   Alexander's early years as king
Were consumed with consolidating his position. Northern barbarians
Tested him; he campaigned against them and established his
Authority. Southern {{p|Greek}} cities rebelled, most notably {{p|Thebes}};

Alexander besieged {{p|Thebes}}, captured it, and—as an example to
Other potential rebels—destroyed the city utterly. {{p|Thebes}}'s
Population was massacred or enslaved. Only the house of the poet
Pindar was spared out of respect for his literary memory. This

Was the {{p|Alexander}} who would later be remembered for clemency and
Cosmopolitanism, but he was also capable of extraordinary violence
When he thought it served his political goals. The destruction of
{{p|Thebes}} pacified {{p|Greece}}. In three-thirty-four BCE, Alexander

Crossed the {{p|Hellespont}} with an army of perhaps thirty-five
Thousand soldiers. He defeated a {{p|Persian}} force at the {{p|Battle of
The Granicus}} and moved into {{p|Anatolia}}. He liberated the {{p|Greek}}
Cities of the {{p|Ionian}} coast from {{p|Persian}} rule. He moved south and

Defeated the main {{p|Persian}} army under Darius III himself at the
{{p|Battle of Issus}} in three-thirty-three BCE. Darius fled, leaving
His family captive—his mother, wife, and daughters fell into
Alexander's hands. Alexander treated them with notable chivalry,

A detail widely remarked on both in antiquity and since. Darius
Offered peace—a substantial ransom, a marriage alliance, and the
Cession of all territory west of the {{p|Euphrates}}. One of Alexander's
Generals, Parmenion, advised him to accept: "I would, if I were

Alexander." "So would I," the young king replied, "if I were
Parmenion." The exchange is probably legendary but captures
Alexander's refusal to settle for less than total victory.

Then south into the {{p|Levant}}. The coastal city of {{p|Tyre}}, on its
Fortified island, resisted. Alexander besieged it for seven months.
He built a causeway from the mainland to the island—an enormous
Engineering feat, still visible in the silted-up peninsula that

{{p|Tyre}} occupies today. When the city finally fell, he massacred
Or enslaved its population. Farther south, {{p|Gaza}} resisted; he
Reduced it similarly. Then he entered {{p|Egypt}} in three-thirty-two
BCE. The {{p|Egyptians}}, who hated their {{p|Persian}} overlords, welcomed

Him as a liberator. Alexander was crowned pharaoh. He visited
The oracle of Ammon at {{p|Siwa}}—a remote desert shrine—and was
Reportedly told by the oracle that he was the son of Ammon
Himself, or of Zeus-Ammon (the two deities were identified in

The syncretic {{p|Hellenistic}} imagination). This proclamation of
Divine sonship became central to Alexander's self-image for the
Rest of his life. He founded the city of {{p|Alexandria}} on the
{{p|Mediterranean}} coast—the first and greatest of the many cities

He would found bearing his name. {{p|Alexandria}} would become the
Greatest city of the {{p|Hellenistic}} world, home of the {{p|Great Library}}
And the {{p|Musaeum}}, the intellectual capital of the {{p|Mediterranean}}
For the next several centuries. Alexander did not live to see its

Development, but he chose the site with geographical acumen. Its
Position at the intersection of {{p|Mediterranean}} sea lanes and
{{p|Nile}}-{{p|Red Sea}} trade routes made it perfectly positioned for
Commerce, culture, and political control.

                                               Then east into
{{p|Mesopotamia}}. At the {{p|Battle of Gaugamela}} in three-thirty-one BCE,
Alexander defeated Darius decisively—despite being outnumbered
Perhaps three or four to one. The {{p|Persian}} army broke; Darius

Fled again. Alexander entered {{p|Babylon}}, {{p|Susa}}, and finally {{p|Persepolis}},
The great {{p|Persian}} capital. In {{p|Persepolis}}—the ceremonial heart of
The empire, where Darius I and Xerxes had conducted their most
Magnificent displays of imperial power—he allowed (or ordered)

The palace complex to be burned. Sources disagree on whether this
Was a drunken act of revenge (for the {{p|Persian}} burning of {{p|Athens}}
A century and a half earlier) or a calculated political statement
(Marking the end of {{p|Achaemenid}} rule). Either way, {{p|Persepolis}}

Burned. The ruins that remain today were charred by Alexander's
Fires. Meanwhile Darius was murdered by one of his own satraps,
Bessus, who then declared himself the new king. Alexander pursued,
Caught Bessus, and had him executed for regicide—positioning

Himself, very pointedly, as the legitimate successor to the
{{p|Achaemenid}} throne rather than as a foreign conqueror who had
Destroyed it. This was Alexander's political genius at work. He
Did not want {{p|Persia}} merely as a conquered territory. He wanted

To become its {{p|Great King}}. He adopted elements of {{p|Persian}} court
Ceremony. He took {{p|Persian}} wives. He encouraged his officers to
Take {{p|Persian}} wives. He began to blend {{p|Greek}} and {{p|Persian}} court
Cultures, imagining a combined empire in which both traditions

Would contribute. This cultural policy alienated many of his
{{p|Macedonian}} officers, who had fought to defeat {{p|Persia}}, not to
Become {{p|Persian}}. Tensions grew. Conspiracies were uncovered or
Alleged. Alexander had several senior {{p|Macedonian}} officers

Executed, including Parmenion's son Philotas and eventually
Parmenion himself. He had his companion Cleitus, who had saved
His life at {{p|Granicus}}, murdered in a drunken quarrel at a feast.
These events marked the darkening of Alexander's character in his

Later years. The young idealistic conqueror was becoming a
Paranoid autocrat. He continued east nonetheless. Through {{p|Central
Asia}}—{{p|Bactria}}, {{p|Sogdiana}}—where he married the princess Roxana.
Across the {{p|Hindu Kush}} into what is now {{p|Afghanistan}} and {{p|Pakistan}}.

Into the {{p|Indus}} Valley. At the {{p|Battle of the Hydaspes}} in three-
Twenty-six BCE, he defeated the {{p|Indian}} king Porus, who fought on
Elephants—a new kind of enemy for the {{p|Macedonian}} army. Porus,
After his defeat, was asked by Alexander how he wished to be

Treated. "Like a king," he reportedly replied. Alexander, impressed,
Restored Porus to his kingdom as a vassal. The encounter is
Another of the scenes—likely embellished—that defined the
Alexander myth: the gracious conqueror recognizing nobility in

His enemies. But Alexander's troops had had enough. They refused
To march farther east. The {{p|Indian}} subcontinent stretched on—
Alexander wanted to reach the {{p|Ganges}}—but the {{p|Macedonians}} had
Been campaigning for eight years, far from home, and they were

Exhausted. At the river {{p|Hyphasis}} (now the {{p|Beas}}), they mutinied.
Alexander sulked in his tent for three days, waiting for them to
Change their minds. They did not. He turned back. He sailed down
The {{p|Indus}} to the {{p|Arabian Sea}} and then marched his army westward

Through the {{p|Gedrosian Desert}} (now {{p|Balochistan}})—a terrible
Journey in which thousands died of thirst and exhaustion. His
Fleet, under Nearchus, sailed parallel along the coast. When
Alexander finally reached {{p|Babylon}} in three-twenty-three BCE,

He was planning his next campaign—perhaps to {{p|Arabia}}, perhaps to
The western {{p|Mediterranean}}. He was also throwing increasingly
Grand feasts and making increasingly grand claims about his own
Divinity (he had demanded that the {{p|Greek}} cities recognize him as

A god—a controversial innovation that his own veterans regarded
With uncomfortable skepticism). Then, at age thirty-two, after a
Particularly heavy drinking bout, he developed a fever and within
Two weeks was dead. His body, embalmed, was eventually transported

To {{p|Alexandria}}, where it was interred in a magnificent tomb that
Survived until late antiquity (its current location is unknown,
Having probably been destroyed by earthquake or hostile action).

                                                                     His
Empire fragmented. He had no clear successor—Roxana was pregnant,
But a posthumous son would take years to grow. Three of his
Generals—Ptolemy, Seleucus, and Antigonus One-Eye—eventually
Carved out the three great {{p|Hellenistic}} kingdoms from his empire.

Ptolemy took {{p|Egypt}} and became founder of a dynasty that would
Last three centuries and produce the famous Cleopatra VII.
Seleucus took the {{p|Near East}} and became founder of a dynasty that
Would rule from {{p|Anatolia}} to {{p|Bactria}} until weakened by {{p|Roman}} and

{{p|Parthian}} pressure. Antigonus's descendants ruled {{p|Macedon}} itself
For several generations. Other successor states included the
{{p|Attalid}} kingdom of {{p|Pergamon}}, the {{p|Greco-Bactrian}} kingdom in
{{p|Central Asia}} and {{p|India}}, and various smaller polities. None

Reunited Alexander's empire. None lasted indefinitely. But while
They did last, they presided over one of the most culturally
Creative periods in antiquity. {{p|Hellenistic}} science, literature,
Religion, and philosophy flourished in their courts and cities.

And the legacy. Alexander's conquest was political, yes, but it
Was even more culturally significant. He did not merely rule over
Conquered peoples; he spread {{p|Greek}} culture across three continents.
The cities he founded—somewhere between twenty and seventy

{{p|Alexandrias}}—became nuclei of {{p|Greek}} language and institutions.
Their populations were partly {{p|Macedonian}} and {{p|Greek}} settlers,
Partly local peoples {{p|Hellenized}} through contact. Over the
Following centuries, {{p|Greek}} became the lingua franca of the

Eastern {{p|Mediterranean}} and {{p|Near East}}—the language of government,
Of commerce, of high culture. When the authors of the {{p|New Testament}}
Wrote their gospels and epistles three centuries later, they wrote
In {{p|Greek}}—because {{p|Greek}} had become the universal language of the

{{p|Hellenistic}} world and would remain so even after {{p|Roman}} political
Conquest. Alexander's conquest made possible the cultural koine
That would shape all subsequent {{p|Mediterranean}} and {{p|Near Eastern}}
Civilization. It made possible the translation of the {{p|Septuagint}}

(The {{p|Greek}} version of the {{p|Hebrew Bible}}, translated in {{p|Alexandria}}
In the third century BCE). It made possible the spread of
{{p|Christianity}}, which traveled on {{p|Greek}}-language cultural pathways.
It made possible the transmission of {{p|Buddhist}} ideas westward

Into the {{p|Hellenistic}} world and of {{p|Greek}} ideas eastward into
{{p|Indian}} and {{p|Central Asian}} cultures—producing cultural hybrids
Like {{p|Greco-Buddhist}} art, where statues of the Buddha are carved
In {{p|Greek}} classical style. Alexander opened pathways that would

Carry cultural traffic for millennia. He was, in this sense, one
Of the most consequential individuals in world history—not despite
But because of his apparent failures (dying young, leaving a
Disintegrating empire). His political achievement was temporary.

His cultural achievement was permanent.

                                           And the {{x|Gaiad}} must also
Assess him critically. Alexander killed hundreds of thousands
Of people. He destroyed ancient cities. He enslaved populations.

He executed political opponents on flimsy charges. He became,
In his later years, a paranoid autocrat claiming divinity. He
Expended the lives of his soldiers in ambitious campaigns whose
Political purpose, beyond his own glory, was unclear. He was

Not a humane man. He was not a democratic ruler. He was a warlord
Of extraordinary talent and ambition whose successes were built
On massive violence. The myth of Alexander—the young philosopher-
King spreading enlightenment across the world—is partly true and

Partly propaganda. His tutor Aristotle did influence him, and he
Did carry scientific instruments and scholars on his campaigns
To document new lands and species. But he also had Callisthenes,
Aristotle's nephew and one of the campaign's historians, executed

On trumped-up charges of conspiracy. He was, in the last analysis,
A conqueror. His achievements are real but so are his costs. The
Ancient world regarded him with a mixture of admiration and
Horror that was probably more accurate than the pure adulation

Of later romanticized traditions. The {{x|Gaiad}} reads him accordingly.
Magnificent, consequential, and terrible. A figure who reshaped
World history in thirteen years and who left behind him a world
That was permanently different—in ways both illuminating and

Oppressive. The {{p|Hellenistic}} world was a dramatic cultural
Flowering. It was also, for many of its inhabitants, a harsh
Political reality. Both are true. Alexander's legacy is both.

                                                                        Alexander.
Alexander the Great. {{p|Macedonian}} king, {{p|Persian}} shah, {{p|Egyptian}} pharaoh,
{{p|Indian}} conqueror. {{p|Granicus}}, {{p|Issus}}, {{p|Gaugamela}}, {{p|Hydaspes}}. {{p|Tyre}}'s
Siege and {{p|Thebes}}'s destruction. {{p|Persepolis}} burning. The claim of

Divine sonship at {{p|Siwa}}. {{p|Alexandria}} founded. The marriage to
Roxana and the Persian-{{p|Macedonian}} synthesis. The mutiny at the
{{p|Hyphasis}}. The desert march through {{p|Gedrosia}}. The death at
{{p|Babylon}} at age thirty-two.

The empire that fragmented but the culture that lasted. The
Twenty {{p|Alexandrias}} as nuclei of {{p|Hellenization}}. {{p|Greek}} as lingua
Franca of three continents. The pathway opened for {{p|Christianity}}
To spread and for {{p|Buddhism}} to meet {{p|Hellenism}}.

Alexander. The conqueror whose conquests lasted thirteen years
And whose cultural consequences have lasted two thousand three
Hundred.


# Chapter 175: The Maurya Empire

In the wake of Alexander's withdrawal from the {{p|Indus}}, a new power
Rose in northern {{p|India}}—the greatest empire the subcontinent had
Yet seen. The {{p|Maurya Empire}}, founded by Chandragupta Maurya
Around three-twenty-two BCE and reaching its peak under his

Grandson Ashoka a century later. The {{p|Maurya}} empire would unify
Almost the entire {{p|Indian}} subcontinent—something no prior {{p|Indian}}
Polity had achieved, and something that would not be repeated
Until the {{p|Mughals}} and, arguably, not truly until modern {{p|India}}

Itself in nineteen-forty-seven CE. For roughly one hundred forty
Years, one dynasty ruled from the {{p|Hindu Kush}} to the {{p|Deccan}},
From {{p|Bengal}} to {{p|Gujarat}}. It established administrative structures,
Promoted a specific form of {{p|Buddhist}}-inflected ethics, and

Projected {{p|Indian}} cultural influence across {{p|Asia}}.

                                                                 Chandragupta.
The founder. His origins are obscure—possibly low-born, possibly
A minor noble, the sources disagree. What is clear is that he

Rose to power through political and military acumen, apparently
With the guidance of an older statesman and philosopher,
Chanakya (also known as Kautilya), whose treatise the
{{p|Arthashastra}} is one of the greatest works of political realism

Ever composed—the {{p|Indian}} counterpart of Machiavelli's {{p|Prince}}
But more systematic and centuries earlier. The {{p|Arthashastra}}
Treats statecraft as a technical discipline: how to collect taxes,
How to run espionage services, how to manage foreign relations,

How to handle conspiracies, how to suppress rebellions. It is
Unsentimental, thorough, and unsparing. Chandragupta apparently
Implemented its principles successfully. He overthrew the
{{p|Nanda Dynasty}} that had ruled much of northern {{p|India}} and

Established himself at {{p|Pataliputra}} (near modern {{p|Patna}}). Then
He faced {{p|Seleucus}}, one of Alexander's successors, who was
Attempting to reassert {{p|Hellenistic}} authority over the {{p|Indus}}
Valley. Chandragupta defeated Seleucus around three-oh-five BCE

And negotiated a peace that ceded substantial territory in
{{p|Afghanistan}} and {{p|Balochistan}} to the {{p|Mauryans}}. As part of the
Settlement, Chandragupta received five hundred war elephants
From Seleucus (which Seleucus would use effectively in his

Own subsequent western campaigns), and possibly a {{p|Seleucid}}
Princess as a wife. Seleucus also sent an ambassador, Megasthenes,
To Chandragupta's court. Megasthenes's account of {{p|Maurya}} {{p|India}}
Is one of the most important external descriptions of ancient

{{p|India}} to survive—preserved in fragments quoted by later {{p|Greek}}
Writers. It describes a wealthy, populous, well-organized realm
With a systematic bureaucracy, extensive roads, caste divisions,
And elaborate court ceremony. Chandragupta himself, Megasthenes

Reports, lived in constant fear of assassination, changing his
Sleeping quarters nightly and being surrounded by female bodyguards.
The realities of absolute power. Late in life, Chandragupta
Is said to have abdicated, converted to {{p|Jainism}}, and ended his

Life as an ascetic through the ritual fasting known as {{p|Sallekhana}}.
Whether this is historically accurate or a pious later legend is
Debated. But the story captures something: the {{p|Maurya}} ruler who
Had built an empire through calculated violence finally turning

Inward toward spiritual renunciation.

                                           His son Bindusara inherited
The empire and extended it southward, deep into the {{p|Deccan}}. We
Know relatively little about him directly, though {{p|Greek}} sources

Mention him as "Amitrochates" (a garbled form of {{p|Sanskrit}}
"{{p|Amitraghata}}," "slayer of foes"). He had diplomatic relations with
The {{p|Hellenistic}} kingdoms—apparently requesting sweet wine and
Dried figs from Antiochus I of {{p|Syria}}, and also requesting that

Antiochus send a sophist to teach him philosophy. Antiochus
Replied that {{p|Seleucid}} law did not permit the export of philosophers.
The anecdote is probably embellished but charming—the {{p|Indian}}
Emperor asking {{p|Greece}} to export a thinker as if it were a

Commodity. Bindusara died around two-seventy-two BCE. And then
Came Ashoka.

                    Ashoka the Great. One of the most remarkable
Figures of the ancient world. He came to the throne around two-

Sixty-eight BCE after a contested succession in which he is said
(In later traditions) to have killed several of his brothers. His
Early reign was ruthless, following the pattern set by his
Grandfather and father. He expanded the empire. He suppressed

Rebellions. And in two-sixty-two BCE, he launched a war of
Conquest against the state of {{p|Kalinga}} on the eastern coast. The
{{p|Kalinga War}} was brutal. According to Ashoka's own later
Inscriptions, a hundred thousand people were killed, a hundred

Fifty thousand were deported, and many more died from wounds and
Starvation. Ashoka won. And then something astonishing happened.
Looking at the devastation he had caused, Ashoka was overcome
With remorse. He converted—gradually, it seems—to {{p|Buddhism}}, and

Dedicated the rest of his reign to promoting what he called
"{{p|Dhamma}}"—a personal ethical and philosophical vision drawn from
{{p|Buddhist}} teaching but adapted for imperial purposes. He renounced
Further wars of conquest. He promoted vegetarianism and

Non-violence. He sponsored religious and philosophical missions
Across {{p|Asia}}. He founded hospitals (reportedly the first in human
History to provide free care to the poor). He planted trees and
Dug wells along the roads to benefit travelers. He built stone

Pillars throughout his empire, each topped with a sculpted lion
Or wheel, inscribed with edicts explaining his {{p|Dhamma}} policy and
Urging his subjects to treat each other with kindness, respect
Elders, honor priests of all religions, and so forth. Over thirty

Of these {{p|Ashokan}} pillars and rock edicts survive. They are
Remarkable documents—the only substantial collection of first-
Person imperial communication to survive from ancient {{p|India}}. They
Show us Ashoka's actual voice, earnest, self-critical, committed

To an ethical vision that transcends mere political authority.
He promoted religious tolerance: his edicts instruct subjects to
Respect every religion, because every religion contains wisdom,
And because attacking one's own faith by disparaging another's

Brings shame on both. This is an astonishingly pluralistic
Position for an ancient ruler. He sponsored the third {{p|Buddhist}}
Council at {{p|Pataliputra}}, which codified {{p|Theravada}} {{p|Buddhism}} and
Dispatched missionary monks to various kingdoms: {{p|Sri Lanka}} (where

His son Mahendra and daughter Sanghamitra led the mission),
The {{p|Hellenistic}} kingdoms of the {{p|Mediterranean}}, {{p|Central Asia}},
And likely {{p|Southeast Asia}} and beyond. The spread of {{p|Buddhism}}
From its original {{p|Indian}} homeland into the rest of {{p|Asia}} largely

Begins with Ashoka's missionary sponsorship. {{p|Buddhism}}, as it
Exists today—a religion of several hundred million adherents
Across multiple {{p|Asian}} civilizations—would be unthinkable without
Ashoka's political patronage. He was the ruler who took a

Regional ascetic movement and launched it into the world.

                                                                Ashoka's
{{p|Dhamma}} was not conventional {{p|Buddhism}}. It was a kind of civic
Ethics drawn from {{p|Buddhist}} teaching but addressed to the entire

Population of his empire, regardless of their specific religious
Affiliation. His edicts rarely explicitly privilege {{p|Buddhism}} over
Other traditions. They emphasize universal virtues: non-violence,
Truthfulness, generosity, gentleness toward all creatures,

Tolerance among religious communities. This was a new kind of
Political rhetoric. The king claimed legitimacy not through
Conquest or dynastic descent but through his commitment to
Ethical governance. His power was to be used for the welfare of

His subjects, including his non-human subjects—Ashoka placed
Extensive restrictions on animal killing and established what
Would now be called animal sanctuaries. No prior ruler had made
Such claims or undertaken such policies at such scale. Ashoka's

Reign marks a moment when political authority attempted to be
Grounded in ethical rather than purely coercive principles. The
Attempt is not entirely successful—the {{p|Maurya}} empire was still
An autocracy, Ashoka still maintained a substantial army, and

After his death the ethical framework he had established could
Not be maintained by his successors. But the attempt itself is
Historically significant. It set a precedent for the possibility
Of ethical imperial rule that would resonate through {{p|Asian}}

Political thought for millennia. In twentieth-century {{p|India}},
Jawaharlal Nehru would explicitly look back to Ashoka as a
Model. The modern {{p|Indian}} flag bears the Ashokan wheel at its
Center. The lion capital from Ashoka's pillar at {{p|Sarnath}} is

{{p|India}}'s national emblem. The {{p|Maurya}} emperor's legacy is
Woven into {{p|India}}'s modern self-conception.

                                                  After Ashoka's
Death around two-thirty-two BCE, the empire declined. His

Successors were weaker; the vast realm fragmented; provincial
Governors became autonomous. By one-eighty-five BCE, the last
{{p|Maurya}} king had been overthrown in a coup by his own general,
Pushyamitra Shunga, who founded the {{p|Shunga Dynasty}}. The

{{p|Mauryan}} moment was over. But its imprint would last. {{p|India}}
Had been shown that unification was possible. The pattern of
Centralized imperial administration would be revived by later
Dynasties—the {{p|Gupta Empire}} several centuries later, the

{{p|Mughal Empire}} in the early modern period, modern {{p|India}} itself.
The template was {{p|Mauryan}}. And {{p|Buddhism}}, launched across {{p|Asia}}
By Ashoka's missions, would reshape civilizations from {{p|Sri Lanka}}
To {{p|Japan}}. Ashoka is arguably one of the dozen most consequential

Individuals in human history. And yet, astonishingly, he was
Almost entirely forgotten in {{p|India}} itself for more than a
Millennium. The Ashokan inscriptions were unreadable (the
{{p|Brahmi}} script had been lost); the identity of the emperor who

Had commissioned them was unknown; he was not even mentioned in
Later {{p|Hindu}} chronicles. It was only in the nineteenth century CE
That James Prinsep, a {{p|British}} colonial official and linguist,
Deciphered {{p|Brahmi}} and read Ashoka's edicts. Only then was the

Emperor's identity recovered. Like the {{p|Hittites}}, Ashoka is a
Figure whose greatness was substantially rediscovered by modern
Archaeology and philology. What had been a blank in {{p|Indian}}
Memory was filled in by scholarly detective work. And once

Ashoka was rediscovered, he became one of the defining figures
Of modern {{p|Indian}} identity—an astonishing case of a historical
Figure moving from obscurity to foundational importance through
The efforts of nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship.

                                                                     The
{{x|Gaiad}} honors Ashoka as one of the genuine moral exemplars of
Political history. A conqueror who renounced conquest. A king
Who dedicated his reign to ethical governance. A patron who

Launched {{p|Buddhism}} across {{p|Asia}}. A man whose remorse at his own
Violence led to a transformation of imperial purpose. Most
Political histories offer few such figures. Most ancient empires
Were machines of extraction and violence, governed by rulers whose

Ethical concerns were limited or hypocritical. Ashoka is an
Exception, and a striking one. His example is not perfect—
Absolute power always retains its coercive dimension, and Ashoka
Maintained an empire based on prior conquest. But within the

Constraints of ancient imperial possibility, he seems to have
Genuinely tried to rule well. The {{x|Gaiad}} reads him as one of the
Brightest spots in the political history of antiquity.

# Chapter 176: Qin and Han

In {{p|China}}, the {{p|Warring States}} period came to its violent climax.
For two centuries seven major states had fought for dominance.
Finally, in two-twenty-one BCE, the state of {{p|Qin}}—under its
Ruthless king who would call himself the First Emperor—completed

The conquest of all rival states and unified {{p|China}} for the first
Time.

       Qin Shi Huang. The First Emperor. He came to the throne
Of {{p|Qin}} as a boy of thirteen in two-forty-seven BCE. For years

He ruled under a regent. Once he came into his own, he conducted
Methodical military campaigns that eliminated the rival states
One by one: {{p|Han}} in two-thirty, {{p|Zhao}} in two-twenty-eight, {{p|Wei}} in
Two-twenty-five, {{p|Chu}} in two-twenty-three, {{p|Yan}} in two-twenty-two,

{{p|Qi}} in two-twenty-one. The {{p|Warring States}} were ended. {{p|China}} was
Unified. And Qin Shi Huang declared himself the First Emperor
Of a dynasty that, he anticipated, would last ten thousand
Generations. His reforms were radical. He imposed a single writing

System—the "{{p|small seal script}}"—abolishing regional variations.
He standardized weights, measures, and the axle-widths of carts
(So that ruts worn in one region would fit carts from any other).
He standardized currency. He abolished the old feudal aristocracy

And replaced it with a centralized bureaucracy of appointed
Officials answerable directly to the emperor. He divided the
Country into thirty-six {{p|commanderies}}, each run by a civil governor,
A military commander, and an inspector—a tripartite structure

That would become the template for {{p|Chinese}} provincial administration
For two millennia. He built roads. He built canals. He built the
First unified version of the {{p|Great Wall}} by connecting earlier
Regional walls along the northern frontier, protecting against

{{p|Xiongnu}} nomadic raids. He conducted a campaign of intellectual
Suppression—the famous "burning of books and burying of scholars"
In which he ordered the destruction of most philosophical texts
Not useful to his political program (preserving {{p|Legalist}} works,

Agricultural manuals, and medical texts) and reportedly buried
Alive four hundred sixty {{p|Confucian}} scholars. The scope of this
Atrocity is debated by historians—some accounts are probably
Exaggerated by later hostile {{p|Han}} historians—but some real

Suppression of {{p|Confucian}} and other traditions did occur. Qin Shi
Huang's governing philosophy was {{p|Legalism}}—a school that had
Developed during the {{p|Warring States}} period, articulated most
Clearly by Han Feizi. {{p|Legalism}} held that human nature was

Essentially selfish and that effective government required strict
Laws impartially enforced, with clear rewards for compliance and
Harsh punishments for violation. Unlike {{p|Confucianism}}, which
Trusted in the moral cultivation of rulers, {{p|Legalism}} trusted in

The power of institutional design. The emperor did not need to
Be virtuous; he needed to administer his laws consistently. This
Philosophy justified the authoritarian severity of Qin Shi Huang's
Rule. His state was efficient, centralized, and intensely oppressive.

And of course he was obsessed with death. The man who had founded
The "first" dynasty of "ten thousand generations" was preoccupied
With his own mortality. He sent expeditions in search of the
{{p|elixir of immortality}}. He took mercury-based preparations that

His court physicians claimed would grant long life (and that
Probably contributed to his premature death by mercury poisoning).
He built for himself, over thirty-six years, the most extensive
Tomb complex in human history—covering tens of square kilometers

At {{p|Lintong}} near modern {{p|Xi'an}}. The main tomb mound remains
Unexcavated, but its periphery contains the famous {{p|Terracotta Army}}:
Some eight thousand life-sized pottery warriors, complete with
Horses and chariots, buried in pits to guard the emperor in the

Afterlife. Each warrior is individually modeled with distinctive
Features; together they constitute one of the greatest archaeological
Finds of the twentieth century CE. Their discovery in nineteen-
Seventy-four by local farmers drilling a well is one of the

Serendipitous archaeological stories of the modern era. The
Terracotta Army stands as silent testimony to the emperor's
Ambition, wealth, and paranoid preparation for what might come.
Qin Shi Huang died in two-ten BCE, aged forty-nine, on a tour

Of the eastern provinces. His death was kept secret by the chief
Minister Li Si and the eunuch Zhao Gao, who did not want the
Emperor's death to be announced until they had secured the
Succession. They engineered the murder of the crown prince and

Installed a weaker son as the second emperor. The dynasty's
Vaunted stability collapsed within three years of the First
Emperor's death. Peasant rebellions broke out in two-oh-nine BCE.
By two-oh-six BCE, the {{p|Qin}} capital had fallen. The dynasty that

Was to last ten thousand generations lasted fifteen years.

                                                                 But
The {{p|Qin}} reforms survived. Even as the dynasty collapsed, the
Institutional structures it had created continued to operate.

The standardized writing, the bureaucratic provinces, the road
And canal networks, the legal codes—all of these were retained
By the successor dynasty. The {{p|Han}}. Founded by Liu Bang, a
Peasant rebel who had risen through the chaos of the {{p|Qin}}

Collapse and emerged as one of two contenders for supreme power.
His rival was Xiang Yu, an aristocratic warrior of enormous
Personal prowess but poor political judgment. In a four-year
Civil war ({{p|Chu-Han Contention}}), Liu Bang—using patient political

Maneuvering where Xiang Yu used brutal force—prevailed. At the
{{p|Battle of Gaixia}} in two-oh-two BCE, Xiang Yu was defeated.
Liu Bang declared himself Emperor and founded the {{p|Han Dynasty}}
As Emperor Gaozu. The {{p|Han}} would rule, with one brief interruption,

For four hundred years—from two-oh-two BCE to two-twenty CE. It
Was one of the longest and most influential dynasties in {{p|Chinese}}
History. {{p|Han}} emperors inherited the {{p|Qin}} administrative apparatus
But softened its harshness. They retained the centralized state

But also restored (gradually) some elements of {{p|Confucian}} political
Ethics, eventually adopting {{p|Confucianism}} as the official ideology
During the reign of Emperor Wu (one-forty-one to eighty-seven BCE).
The {{p|Han}} civil service examination, beginning under Emperor Wu,

Would select officials based on classical education rather than
Hereditary status—the first step toward the fully developed
Meritocratic examination system of later dynasties. {{p|Han}} {{p|China}}
Was vast, populous, and culturally productive. Its population

Reached perhaps sixty million—a fifth of the human population of
The time. Its capital at {{p|Chang'an}} was one of the largest cities
In the world, rivaled only by contemporary {{p|Rome}}. And the {{p|Han}}
Was expansionist. Emperor Wu conducted campaigns against the

{{p|Xiongnu}} nomads to the north, the {{p|Nanyue}} kingdom to the south
(Conquering northern {{p|Vietnam}}), the {{p|Korean}} peninsula ({{p|Wiman Joseon}}
Was conquered in one-oh-eight BCE, establishing {{p|Chinese}} colonies
That would influence {{p|Korean}} civilization for centuries), and

{{p|Central Asia}} (reaching as far as the {{p|Ferghana Valley}} in what is
Now {{p|Uzbekistan}}). These campaigns opened the overland trade routes
That would become known as the {{p|Silk Road}}. For the next five
Centuries, {{p|Chinese}} silk would travel westward across {{p|Central Asia}}

To the {{p|Mediterranean}} world, passing through the {{p|Parthian}} and
Later {{p|Sassanid}} empires. In exchange, {{p|Western}} goods traveled east:
Glass, gold, horses (particularly the prized "heavenly horses"
Of {{p|Ferghana}}), wool, and eventually {{p|Buddhism}}. The {{p|Silk Road}}

Connected {{p|Han}} {{p|China}} to the {{p|Mediterranean}} world in sustained
Long-distance exchange. It was one of the most significant
Developments in human economic and cultural history. And the {{p|Han}}
Itself built up intellectual culture. Sima Qian wrote the

{{p|Records of the Grand Historian}} ({{p|Shiji}}), the first great
Work of {{p|Chinese}} historiography, covering the entirety of
{{p|Chinese}} history from the legendary {{p|Yellow Emperor}} down to
Sima Qian's own time. His work would be the model for all

Subsequent {{p|Chinese}} dynastic histories. Cai Lun, a court eunuch,
Invented (or perfected—predecessors existed) paper around one-
Oh-five CE, revolutionizing record-keeping and information
Distribution. {{p|Chinese}} mathematics, astronomy, and medicine

Developed extensively under {{p|Han}} patronage. The magnetic compass,
Though only systematically used for navigation much later, was
First described during the {{p|Han}}. {{p|Han}} metallurgy produced the
Finest steel of any ancient civilization. And the {{p|Han}} transmitted

To subsequent {{p|Chinese}} civilization the template of what {{p|China}}
Was supposed to be. "{{p|Han}}" became, and remains, the ethnonym
Used by the majority {{p|Chinese}} population to refer to themselves.
The very identity "{{p|Han Chinese}}" is a testimony to the dynasty's

Formative importance. There would be later great dynasties—{{p|Tang}},
{{p|Song}}, {{p|Ming}}, {{p|Qing}}—but each of them, in important ways, was
Trying to live up to the template set by the {{p|Han}}. The {{p|Han}}
Was, for subsequent {{p|Chinese}} civilization, what {{p|Rome}} was for

Western civilization: the classical imperial model against which
All later empires were measured.

                                      The {{p|Han Dynasty}} declined in
Its later centuries. The usual imperial problems: decadence at

Court, rebellions on the frontiers, economic pressures, factional
Struggles among eunuchs, officials, and military men. A brief
Interregnum from nine to twenty-three CE—the reign of Wang Mang,
A usurper who attempted radical reforms but failed—divided the

{{p|Western Han}} (two-oh-two BCE to nine CE) from the {{p|Eastern Han}}
(Twenty-five to two-twenty CE). The {{p|Eastern Han}} moved its capital
From {{p|Chang'an}} to {{p|Luoyang}} and struggled with ongoing challenges.
In the second century CE, peasant rebellions (most famously the

{{p|Yellow Turban Rebellion}} of one-eighty-four CE) weakened central
Authority. Warlords rose. In two-twenty CE, the last {{p|Han}} emperor
Was deposed and {{p|China}} entered the {{p|Three Kingdoms}} period—the
Setting of the great historical-fictional epic {{p|Romance of the

Three Kingdoms}} that would be composed over a millennium later.
We will return to that story. Here we close with the {{p|Han}} at its
Peak: a civilization that matched {{p|Rome}} in population and
Sophistication, that connected to {{p|Rome}} via the {{p|Silk Road}}, that

Established the cultural and political template for the next two
Thousand years of {{p|East Asian}} history. The {{p|Han}} and {{p|Rome}} are
The two great classical empires of the ancient world—peers, even
Rivals, though they rarely dealt directly with each other. Their

Parallel existence framed the classical age of {{p|Eurasia}}.

                                                                     And
Consider what the {{p|Qin}}-{{p|Han}} sequence established. A unified {{p|China}}.
A bureaucratic state. A standardized language and writing. A legal

Framework. A stable agricultural economy. A cultural identity
Based on {{p|Confucian}} classical learning. A political ideology
Combining imperial authority with ethical responsibility. A
Pattern of expansion into surrounding regions that would shape

{{p|East Asian}} geopolitics ever after. These were not minor achievements.
They set the terms within which {{p|East Asian}} civilization would
Operate for the next two millennia. When subsequent dynasties
Rose and fell, when {{p|China}} was repeatedly conquered by nomadic

Peoples ({{p|Xianbei}}, {{p|Khitans}}, {{p|Jurchens}}, {{p|Mongols}}, {{p|Manchus}}),
Each new dynasty adopted the {{p|Qin}}-{{p|Han}} template for ruling {{p|China}}.
The template proved remarkably durable. It persisted because it
Worked—because it provided effective tools for administering a

Vast, populous, agriculturally intensive, ethnically diverse
Realm. The {{p|Qin}}-{{p|Han}} synthesis was one of the great achievements
Of pre-modern political technology. It is worth understanding
In some detail because so much subsequent {{p|East Asian}} history

Operates within its categories.

The {{p|Terracotta Army}}. The {{p|Silk Road}} opening. Sima Qian's
History. The invention of paper. The four centuries of {{p|Han}}
Governance. The model that all subsequent {{p|Chinese}} dynasties
Would try to match.

# Chapter 177: The Punic Wars

In the western {{p|Mediterranean}}, while Alexander's successors were
Dividing up his conquests and the {{p|Han}} was unifying {{p|China}}, two
Powers were rising that would eventually fight each other for
Control of the {{p|Mediterranean}} world. {{p|Rome}} and {{p|Carthage}}.

                                                                      {{p|Rome}}
Had begun as a small city-state on the {{p|Tiber River}} in central
{{p|Italy}}. Its traditional founding date—seven-fifty-three BCE, by
Romulus—is legendary, but archaeological evidence does show

A settlement there from the eighth century BCE. Initially ruled
By kings (traditionally seven of them, ending with Tarquin the Proud),
{{p|Rome}} became a republic in five-oh-nine BCE when the last king was
Overthrown. The {{p|Roman Republic}} had a distinctive constitutional

Structure: two consuls elected annually to executive authority
(Checking each other), a {{x|Senate}} of aristocratic elders providing
Continuity and policy guidance, assemblies of citizens voting on
Laws and electing magistrates, and tribunes of the plebs with

Veto powers to protect the common people from aristocratic abuse.
The system balanced power between aristocratic and popular
Elements and was designed to prevent any one person or family
From becoming dominant. It worked—for about four hundred fifty

Years. During those centuries, {{p|Rome}} gradually conquered {{p|Italy}},
Defeating the {{p|Etruscans}}, the various {{p|Italic}} peoples ({{p|Samnites}},
{{p|Sabines}}, {{p|Latins}}), and the {{p|Greek}} cities of southern {{p|Italy}} and
{{p|Sicily}}. By two-seventy BCE, {{p|Rome}} dominated the entire {{p|Italian}}

Peninsula. And then {{p|Rome}} looked outward, across the {{p|Strait of
Messina}}, at the commercial empire of {{p|Carthage}}.

                                                            {{p|Carthage}}.
A {{p|Phoenician}} colony founded in the ninth century BCE on the

Coast of what is now {{p|Tunisia}}. {{p|Phoenician}} traders from {{p|Tyre}} had
Established it as a commercial outpost, and it had grown over
The centuries into the dominant commercial power of the western
{{p|Mediterranean}}. The {{p|Carthaginians}}—whom the {{p|Romans}} called {{p|Punici}},

Hence "{{p|Punic}}"—controlled trade routes across {{p|North Africa}}, into
{{p|Iberia}} (especially its silver mines), to {{p|Sardinia}} and {{p|Corsica}},
And into parts of {{p|Sicily}}. They had a powerful navy, a merchant
Empire, and substantial wealth. They spoke a {{p|Semitic}} language

({{p|Punic}}, closely related to {{p|Phoenician}} and {{p|Hebrew}}), worshipped
{{p|Semitic}} gods (including Baal Hammon and Tanit), and were
Regarded by the {{p|Romans}} with a mixture of admiration and suspicion.
Their religious practices included, in their most extreme form,

Child sacrifice—the infamous {{p|tophet}}, burial grounds containing
The cremated remains of infants that have been found at {{p|Carthage}}
And other {{p|Punic}} sites. The interpretation of these sites is
Disputed; some scholars argue the burials are of children who

Had died naturally and were dedicated to the gods, rather than
Victims of active sacrifice. But some ancient sources describe
Systematic sacrifice of infants to Baal Hammon in times of civic
Crisis. The {{p|Romans}} regarded {{p|Carthaginian}} religion as particularly

Barbaric—an attitude that would later feed into {{p|Roman}} propaganda
Against their enemies. The two powers could not indefinitely
Coexist. The western {{p|Mediterranean}} was not large enough for both
An expanding {{p|Italian}} land power and an established maritime

Commercial empire. Their clash began over {{p|Sicily}}, an island whose
Eastern side was largely {{p|Greek}}, western side largely {{p|Punic}}, and
Northern side increasingly {{p|Roman}}. Starting in two-sixty-four BCE,
{{p|Rome}} and {{p|Carthage}} fought three wars over the following century.

Together these are known as the {{p|Punic Wars}}.

                                                    The {{p|First Punic War}}
(Two-sixty-four to two-forty-one BCE) was fought primarily over
{{p|Sicily}}. {{p|Rome}} had little naval experience; {{p|Carthage}} was a

Dominant sea power. But the {{p|Romans}}, with characteristic stubbornness,
Built a navy from scratch, imitating a captured {{p|Carthaginian}}
Warship and adding their own innovation: the {{p|corvus}}, a boarding
Bridge that allowed {{p|Roman}} marines to turn naval battles into

Land battles. They won enough engagements to achieve a decisive
Victory. {{p|Carthage}} ceded {{p|Sicily}} to {{p|Rome}} and paid a massive
Indemnity. The peace was uneasy. {{p|Carthage}}, humiliated and
Financially strained, faced a further crisis when its own

Mercenary troops rebelled; it took three years to suppress this
{{p|Mercenary War}}. During that period, {{p|Rome}} seized {{p|Sardinia}} and
{{p|Corsica}}—an action widely regarded as opportunistic and unfair.
{{p|Carthaginian}} resentment festered. The general Hamilcar Barca—

One of {{p|Carthage}}'s most capable commanders—led the family that
Would drive the next war. He had his young son Hannibal swear,
At an altar, eternal enmity against {{p|Rome}}. That oath would shape
The coming conflict. Hamilcar built up {{p|Carthaginian}} power in

{{p|Iberia}}, expanding the empire westward and creating a base from
Which to eventually renew the war. He was succeeded by his son-in-
Law Hasdrubal, and then by Hannibal himself, who took command
In {{p|Spain}} in two-twenty-one BCE.

                                       The {{p|Second Punic War}}
(Two-eighteen to two-oh-one BCE) was perhaps the most dramatic war
Of antiquity. Hannibal, anticipating that {{p|Rome}} would attack him
In {{p|Spain}}, decided instead to strike first. He led an army of

Perhaps fifty thousand men—including war elephants—across the
{{p|Ebro}}, through the {{p|Pyrenees}}, across southern {{p|Gaul}}, over the
{{p|Alps}} in winter (a march that passed into legend for its
Impossibility, and whose exact route is still debated), and into

Northern {{p|Italy}}. He arrived in {{p|Italy}} with perhaps half his original
Force—the crossings had been costly—but he was in the {{p|Roman}}
Homeland. He won three catastrophic victories in succession. At
The {{p|Trebia River}} in two-eighteen BCE. At {{p|Lake Trasimene}} in

Two-seventeen BCE (where he ambushed and destroyed an entire
{{p|Roman}} army in a mountain pass, killing the consul Flaminius).
And at {{p|Cannae}} in two-sixteen BCE—the most devastating {{p|Roman}}
Defeat in centuries. At {{p|Cannae}}, Hannibal used a tactical

Innovation now called the "{{p|double envelopment}}": allowing his
Center to slowly yield while his flanks wheeled around behind
The advancing {{p|Romans}}, encircling them completely. The trapped
{{p|Roman}} army was annihilated. Perhaps fifty thousand {{p|Romans}} died

In a single afternoon—a death toll larger than any single day's
Combat casualties in {{p|Western}} history until the twentieth century.
After {{p|Cannae}}, the {{p|Roman}} state faced existential crisis. Many
Of their {{p|Italian}} allies defected to Hannibal. The war seemed

Lost. But {{p|Rome}} did not break. The {{x|Senate}}, showing the discipline
For which it became famous, refused to negotiate with Hannibal.
{{p|Rome}} would fight on. And it did. The general Quintus Fabius Maximus
Adopted what became known as "Fabian tactics": refusing to engage

Hannibal directly, shadowing his army, cutting his supply lines,
Slowly wearing him down. Hannibal could win battles but could
Not force {{p|Rome}} to surrender. Meanwhile, {{p|Roman}} armies operated
Elsewhere: in {{p|Spain}}, where Publius Cornelius Scipio (later

Called Africanus) defeated {{p|Carthaginian}} forces and eventually
Conquered the entire peninsula; in {{p|Sicily}}, where the {{p|Greek}} city
Of {{p|Syracuse}} (which had defected to {{p|Carthage}}) was besieged and
Taken (an event famous for the death of the mathematician

Archimedes, who was killed by a {{p|Roman}} soldier during the sack
Despite orders to spare him); in {{p|North Africa}}, where Scipio
Ultimately carried the war to {{p|Carthage}} itself. In two-oh-two BCE,
At the {{p|Battle of Zama}}, Scipio defeated Hannibal on Hannibal's

Home ground. The {{p|Second Punic War}} ended. {{p|Carthage}} was stripped
Of its overseas territories, forbidden to maintain a navy without
{{p|Roman}} permission, and required to pay a massive indemnity. It
Was reduced to a shadow of its former power. But it still existed.

And for two generations, {{p|Carthage}} remained—a commercial city
Confined to its {{p|African}} hinterland, paying its indemnity, gradually
Recovering economically. {{p|Rome}} watched with increasing distrust.
The old senator Cato the Elder ended every speech in the {{x|Senate}},

Regardless of its topic, with the phrase "{{p|Carthago delenda est}}"—
"{{p|Carthage}} must be destroyed." In the mid-second century BCE,
{{p|Roman}} hawks found a pretext for war. The {{p|Third Punic War}}
(One-forty-nine to one-forty-six BCE) was less a war than a

Systematic destruction. {{p|Rome}} besieged {{p|Carthage}}. After three
Years, the city fell. It was sacked, burned, and razed. Survivors
Were enslaved. The ground was reportedly sown with salt to make
It barren (though this detail may be later embellishment). The

{{p|Carthaginian}} civilization ended. {{p|North Africa}} became the {{p|Roman}}
Province of {{p|Africa}}, which would become one of {{p|Rome}}'s most
Productive grain-producing regions. {{p|Carthage}} itself would be
Refounded as a {{p|Roman}} colony a century later, but the original

{{p|Punic}} civilization was erased.

                                          And the {{p|Punic Wars}}
Transformed {{p|Rome}}. Before the wars, {{p|Rome}} was a regional {{p|Italian}}
Power. After them, it was the dominant state of the western

{{p|Mediterranean}}. The wars had required {{p|Rome}} to build a navy, to
Conduct operations across vast distances, to manage provinces
Outside of {{p|Italy}}, and to handle enormous economic and social
Strains. All these experiences accelerated {{p|Rome}}'s evolution

From city-state to empire. The conquest of {{p|Iberia}} brought huge
Wealth to {{p|Rome}}. The acquisition of {{p|Sicily}} and eventually {{p|Africa}}
Gave it a grain supply that would feed {{p|Rome}}'s growing urban
Population. The wars also concentrated power in the hands of

Successful generals like Scipio Africanus, beginning a pattern
Of political-military personality that would eventually undermine
The {{p|Republic}}. The precedent of one man holding exceptional command
For extended periods against military threats would be invoked

By Marius, Sulla, Pompey, and eventually Julius Caesar—each
Drawing on the institutional flexibility that the {{p|Punic Wars}}
Had established. The wars also militarized {{p|Roman}} society. Most
Adult male {{p|Romans}} served for years as soldiers. Veterans returned

To find their farms ruined by neglect or bought up by wealthy
Landowners (the long wars having ruined small farmers while
Concentrating land in the hands of those who could buy slaves
To work it). The resulting social displacement created a class

Of landless poor who crowded into {{p|Rome}} and other cities—a
Volatile population that would fuel political instability for
Generations. The social reforms of the Gracchi brothers in the
Late second century BCE tried to address this problem through

Land redistribution; their failure and violent deaths marked the
Beginning of the political breakdowns that would eventually end
The {{p|Republic}}. And meanwhile, {{p|Rome}} continued to expand. Having
Defeated {{p|Carthage}}, it turned east. {{p|Macedon}} was defeated in a

Series of wars; mainland {{p|Greece}} was annexed. The {{p|Seleucid}} empire
Was defeated at {{p|Magnesia}} in one-ninety BCE, becoming effectively
A {{p|Roman}} client. {{p|Anatolia}} was annexed. By the end of the second
Century BCE, the {{p|Mediterranean}} was a {{p|Roman}} lake. Further

Conquests in the first century BCE—{{p|Syria}}, {{p|Gaul}}, {{p|Egypt}}—would
Extend the imperial reach to its maximum extent. But the
Foundations of {{p|Roman}} imperial power were laid in the {{p|Punic Wars}}.
Without them, there would have been no {{p|Roman Empire}}.

                                                                    And
Hannibal himself. Defeated at {{p|Zama}}, exiled from {{p|Carthage}} for
Political reasons, he wandered the eastern {{p|Mediterranean}} in the
Following years, offering his military expertise to anti-{{p|Roman}}

Powers. He eventually took refuge with the {{p|Bithynian}} king
Prusias I. When {{p|Roman}} diplomats demanded that Hannibal be
Turned over to them, he took poison rather than face capture.
He died in exile, probably in one-eighty-three BCE, at around

Age sixty-three. His tactical genius is still studied in military
Academies worldwide. His double envelopment at {{p|Cannae}} has been
Emulated (successfully and unsuccessfully) by commanders from
Frederick the Great to Robert E. Lee to modern military

Theorists. He stands as one of the supreme commanders in history—
A man who crossed the {{p|Alps}} with elephants, won three impossible
Victories against the greatest power of his age, and sustained
A hopeless campaign in hostile territory for fifteen years before

Finally being defeated not by battle but by the systematic attrition
Of superior resources. The {{x|Gaiad}} honors Hannibal's genius even
While recognizing that his cause—the preservation of {{p|Punic}}
Civilization against {{p|Roman}} expansion—was ultimately lost. He

Is one of the tragic figures of ancient history. A great commander
In service of a state that could not, in the end, match the
Manpower and institutional resilience of its enemy.

                                                           {{p|Punic Wars}}.
{{p|Rome}} and {{p|Carthage}}. Three wars across a century. Hamilcar,
Hasdrubal, Hannibal. The {{p|Alps}} crossing. {{p|Trebia}}, {{p|Trasimene}}, {{p|Cannae}}.
The double envelopment. {{p|Roman}} resilience under catastrophic
Defeat. Scipio Africanus's counter-invasion. The victory at {{p|Zama}}.

The {{p|Third Punic War}} and the destruction of {{p|Carthage}}.
Cato's "{{p|Carthago delenda est}}." The refounding of {{p|Carthage}} as
A {{p|Roman}} colony and the absorption of {{p|North Africa}} into {{p|Roman}}
Territory.

              The wars that transformed {{p|Rome}} from {{p|Italian}} power
To {{p|Mediterranean}} superpower. The proving ground of {{p|Roman}}
Institutions. The beginning of the end for the {{p|Republic}}.

{{p|Punic Wars}}. The century-long duel. The emergence of what would
Become the greatest {{p|Mediterranean}} empire.


# Chapter 178: Rome

After {{p|Carthage}} fell, {{p|Rome}} stood
the master of the {{p|Middle Sea}},
and what had been a farmers' town
now ruled from {{p|Spain}} to {{p|Thessaly}}.

The {{x|Republic}} had been built for farms,
for citizen-assemblies small,
for consuls chosen year by year,
for {{x|Senate}} giving voice to all.

But empire cracked the ancient forms.
The legions served the generals now.
The conquered provinces sent wealth
that drowned the old {{p|Italian}} plough.

The Gracchi Brothers, Tiberius
and Gaius, saw the danger clear:
the landless poor, the latifundia,
the veterans with naught to cheer.

Tiberius proposed reform,
to give the soldiers land again.
The {{x|Senate}} killed him in the streets.
His brother fell the same way then.

The {{x|Republic}} learned that reason died
when wealth was threatened at its root,
and after that the path was set
toward civil war and bloody fruit.

Marius reformed the legions whole,
made volunteers the fighting core,
tied soldiers' loyalty to their chief
and not the state they fought for.

Sulla marched his legions in
upon the sacred city's ground,
the first {{p|Roman}} to make such war,
and dictator for life was crowned.

He purged his enemies with lists,
proscriptions stripping wealth and breath,
and when he thought his work was done
he calmly walked away toward death.

But what he'd done could not be back.
The precedent was set in stone:
armies belonged to generals now,
and politics meant swords alone.

Pompey rose, the golden boy,
who cleared the pirates from the sea,
who conquered eastward to the {{p|Nile}}
and gave {{p|Judea}} subsequently.

Crassus rose, the wealthiest man,
who crushed the Spartacus revolt,
whose gold bought armies, bought the {{x|Senate}},
bought whatever he assault.

Julius Caesar rose, the third,
who claimed descent from Aeneas,
who claimed descent from Venus herself,
and was the ablest of the three.

The {{x|First Triumvirate}} was formed,
unofficial, power-sharing pact.
They carved the {{p|Roman}} world between them
and ruled by this unwritten act.

Crassus died in {{p|Parthia}}, shot
with arrows at the battle's close.
His head was used as stage-prop in
a {{x|Greek}} tragedy, or so it goes.

Then Pompey and Caesar turned to war.
Caesar crossed the {{p|Rubicon}} small
with legions, saying "die is cast,"
and brought the {{x|Republic}} to its fall.

He chased Pompey to {{p|Egypt}}, where
the boy-king Ptolemy beheaded
the {{p|Roman}} general, thinking thus
Caesar's gratitude was threaded.

Caesar wept. Then met Cleopatra,
who rolled inside a carpet's pack,
and made alliance with her bed
and put her on the {{p|Egyptian}} track.

He conquered {{p|Gaul}} across the {{p|Alps}},
wrote {{x|Commentaries}} on his war,
"veni, vidi, vici" at {{p|Zela}},
and dictator for life he bore.

The {{x|Ides of March}} came. Brutus struck,
with Cassius and the {{x|Senate}}'s knives.
"Et tu, Brute?" — then Caesar fell,
and {{p|Rome}} was torn among his wives.

Mark Antony gave the funeral speech
("I come to bury, not to praise"),
roused the crowd against the killers,
and started civil war's new phase.

Octavian, Caesar's grandnephew,
adopted as his heir by will,
revealed himself the cold-eyed one
with patience and political skill.

The {{x|Second Triumvirate}} formed:
Antony, Octavian, Lepidus.
They hunted Brutus down at {{p|Philippi}},
then turned upon each other thus.

Lepidus was marginalized.
Antony went east to Cleopatra.
Octavian stayed in {{p|Rome}} to build
his reputation bit by bit.

Antony lost himself in {{p|Egypt}},
living as pharaoh with his queen,
alienating {{p|Roman}} sentiment,
the conqueror gone {{x|Alexandrine}}.

At {{p|Actium}} the fleets collided.
Octavian's admiral Agrippa won.
Antony and Cleopatra fled,
and soon enough their lives were done.

She with the asp, the royal cobra,
the uraeus of {{p|Egyptian}} crown.
He with his sword, the {{p|Roman}} way.
The {{x|Ptolemaic}} line went down.

Octavian stood the sole {{p|Roman}},
and took the title Augustus then,
the "revered one," princeps, first,
and ruled for forty-five years again.

He said he'd restored the {{x|Republic}},
but everyone knew the truth:
the {{x|Senate}} met, the consuls served,
but emperor ruled, and that was sooth.

The {{x|Pax Romana}} had begun.
Two centuries of peace and law.
Roads, aqueducts, courts, and coins.
The greatest empire men ever saw.

From {{p|Britain}} to the {{p|Tigris}} reach,
from {{p|Rhine}} to the {{p|Saharan}} sand,
one law, one coin, one road-network,
one citizenship across the land.

The emperors came, some good, some mad:
Tiberius grew dark on {{p|Capri}}.
Caligula made his horse a consul.
Claudius the limping one was wily.

Nero burned and fiddled — or
perhaps that part was later slander.
But he killed his mother, killed his wife,
killed Christians, killed with grand meander.

The {{x|Year of Four Emperors}} came
when Nero fell upon his sword.
Galba, Otho, Vitellius, Vespasian —
four imperial purples poured.

Vespasian stabilized the state,
built the {{p|Colosseum}} in {{p|Rome}},
and founded the {{x|Flavian}} dynasty
to give the empire fresh new home.

His son Titus sacked {{p|Jerusalem}}
in seventy of the {{x|Common Era}},
destroyed the {{p|Second Temple}} whole,
and changed {{x|Judaism}}'s era.

The menorah was carried west
upon the {{p|Arch of Titus}}' face.
The Jews were scattered through the empire.
The {{p|Temple}} cult had lost its place.

But {{p|Rome}} rolled on, the {{x|Five Good Emperors}}:
Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian,
Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius,
who was both emperor and {{x|Stoic}} man.

Trajan pushed to {{p|Dacia}}, took the gold,
reached the greatest extent of the realm.
Hadrian pulled back to the walls,
built {{p|Hadrian's Wall}} across the helm.

Marcus wrote his {{x|Meditations}}
in {{x|Greek}}, for his own private eye,
a {{x|Stoic}} emperor's private journal,
one of the great books that will not die.

"Begin the morning by saying to thyself:
I shall meet with the busybody, ungrateful,
arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial..."
and yet still serve them all, faithful.

He fought the Marcomanni on the {{p|Danube}},
plague was ravaging the troops,
and when he died, the empire was
already starting to lose its loops.

His son Commodus was the break.
He fought in the arena, thought himself Hercules,
was strangled by a wrestler in his bath,
and after him came crises.

{{p|Rome}} had reached its high plateau.
From here, the slow decline began.
But for two hundred years it held
the greatest empire made by man.


# Chapter 179: Second Temple Period

Return from {{p|Babylon}} to {{p|Jerusalem}},
by grace of Cyrus' decree,
the exiles rebuilt the {{p|Temple}} small
and tried to reclaim their destiny.

But who was Jew was now a question.
The returnees drew tight the line.
The Samaritans were turned away,
the mixed-blood were excluded in time.

Ezra read the {{x|Torah}} aloud
for seven days at the {{p|Water Gate}}.
Nehemiah built the walls again.
The community consolidated late.

The priesthood was now the center,
the high priest as civil head.
Under {{x|Persian}}, then {{x|Macedonian}} rule,
{{p|Judea}} quietly went ahead.

Then Alexander came through the land,
was shown the prophecy of Daniel
according to Josephus' tale,
and spared {{p|Jerusalem}} from peril.

After Alexander came the Seleucids,
the {{x|Greek}} kingdom east in {{p|Syria}},
who ruled {{p|Judea}} as a province
with some degree of imperial delirium.

Antiochus IV Epiphanes came,
who wanted {{x|Greek}} culture spread,
who sacked {{p|Jerusalem}}, banned the {{x|Law}},
and set up Zeus above the holy head.

He sacrificed a pig upon
the altar in the {{p|Temple}}'s court.
The "abomination of desolation"
was what the {{x|Book of Daniel}} reported.

This was too much. Revolt broke out.
Mattathias the priest refused
to make the {{x|Greek}} sacrifice, and killed
the Jew who volunteered, misused.

His son Judas Maccabeus rose,
the Hammer of the Lord, and led
guerrilla war against the Greeks
until the {{p|Temple}} was reconsecrated.

{{x|Hanukkah}} the festival commemorates
the rededication's eight-day rite,
the oil that burned for eight days straight
when only one day's oil was in sight.

The {{x|Hasmonean}} dynasty rose
from this successful {{x|Maccabee}} war,
combining high priest with king,
which some Jews thought was against the law.

The Essenes withdrew to {{p|Qumran}}
to wait for the end of days.
They wrote the {{x|Dead Sea Scrolls}} in caves,
rediscovered in modern ways.

The Pharisees developed oral law,
the synagogue, the rabbinic mode,
the piety of every household's {{x|Torah}},
the democratic learning code.

The Sadducees were the priestly class,
who held only the written {{x|Law}},
denied the resurrection's hope,
and ruled the {{p|Temple}} without awe.

The Zealots wanted armed revolt
against whatever power held sway.
The apocalyptists wrote of ends
when God would break into the fray.

Then {{p|Rome}} arrived. Pompey marched in
in sixty-three before the {{x|Common Era}},
walked into the {{p|Holy of Holies}},
astonished that the room was bare.

(For what had he expected? Some golden idol?
The Jews had no image of their god.
Just an empty room where the {{x|Ark}} had been
before Nebuchadnezzar's rod.)

{{p|Rome}} installed Herod the Great,
an {{x|Idumean}}, half-Jewish at best,
who built magnificent palaces,
expanded the {{p|Temple}}, tried to rest.

He rebuilt the {{p|Second Temple}} as
the grandest structure in the {{p|East}},
Herod's {{p|Temple}}, the one that Jesus
would know, the one the Romans decreased.

But Herod was paranoid, murderous,
killed his own sons, his wife as well,
and the {{x|Gospels}} remember him as
the slaughterer of infants in {{p|Bethlehem}}'s dell.

After Herod, the kingdom split,
the Romans ruled through procurators,
and messianic expectation rose
among the poor, the oppressed debtors.

When would the Messiah come?
The Son of David, the anointed one
who would drive out {{p|Rome}} and restore
the kingdom as in days bygone?

Multiple claimants rose and fell.
Theudas. Judas the Galilean.
The Egyptian prophet at {{p|Olivet}}.
Bar Kochba later, the Judean.

Into this ferment John the Baptist came
preaching repentance at the {{p|Jordan}}'s edge,
baptizing crowds who felt the end
was near, the kingdom's sudden pledge.

He was a voice crying in the wilderness,
clothed in camel-hair, fed on locusts and honey,
an Essene-adjacent prophet figure
urging the people, "turn now, stop running."

He baptized Jesus of {{p|Nazareth}},
and later lost his head to Herod
Antipas, the tetrarch, for denouncing
his marriage to his brother's bride.

The kingdom was about to arrive.
Or so it seemed to those who watched.
The {{p|Temple}} stood magnificent still.
The prophets' words were being matched.

Then came a Galilean teacher
who gathered twelve, who spoke in parables,
who healed the sick, and raised the dead,
and spoke of a kingdom invisible.

The next chapter is his.

But here we pause to mark the world
that shaped him: monotheism hardened
in exile; messianism heightened
by {{p|Rome}}'s weight, by the {{p|Temple}}'s burden.

{{x|Judaism}} was already more
than just the cult of one small nation.
It was a portable faith of text,
of weekly synagogue vocation.

It had rivals, it had debates,
Pharisee against Sadducee,
Essene against Zealot, Hellenized
against the strict traditional trees.

But all agreed: one God, no image,
covenant, {{x|Torah}}, {{x|Sabbath}}, kosher.
And all were waiting for what would come
to change the world and bring it closer.


# Chapter 180: Jesus

In {{p|Bethlehem}}, or {{p|Nazareth}},
(the {{x|Gospels}} disagree on birth),
under Augustus' reign, to Joseph
and Mary, a child came to earth.

The tradition says a virgin birth,
a star, magi from the {{p|East}},
shepherds in the fields at night,
a manger when no room at the feast.

These are the infancy narratives,
theological reflections made
years after, when the community
was thinking what had been conveyed.

The core historical facts are few.
He was a Galilean Jew.
He grew up, probably in {{p|Nazareth}},
learned carpentry as Joseph knew.

At thirty or so, he left his home,
went to John at the {{p|Jordan}}'s flow,
was baptized, heard a voice from heaven:
"You are my beloved son, I know."

Then into the wilderness forty days,
tempted by whatever Satan meant,
and afterward he began to preach
the kingdom's immanent advent.

"Repent, for the kingdom of God
is at hand." The same as John the Baptist.
But where John had preached apocalypse,
Jesus spoke of mercy as the rest.

He called twelve disciples, fishermen,
tax collectors, a zealot too,
and traveled through {{p|Galilee}} preaching
in synagogues and open view.

His teaching had three main features:
parables, beatitudes, love commands.
The parables told stories without resolution,
leaving thought in the listener's hands.

The prodigal son, the good {{x|Samaritan}},
the mustard seed, the sower of fields,
the lost sheep, the lost coin, the lost son,
the pearl of great price, the treasure it yields.

The beatitudes inverted the world:
"Blessed are the poor in spirit,
blessed are those who mourn, who hunger,
who are persecuted for my merit."

The love commands were two and radical:
"Love God with all your heart and soul.
Love your neighbor as yourself."
On these two hung the {{x|Law}}'s whole.

But he added one that no one had said:
"Love your enemies. Pray for those
who persecute you. Be perfect as
your Heavenly Father is, who bestows

sun and rain on the just and unjust both."
This was the new, the radical turn.
Not just mercy to your tribe or friend,
but mercy to those whose hate you earn.

He healed the sick, cast out demons,
raised Jairus' daughter from the dead,
raised the widow of {{p|Nain}}'s son,
raised Lazarus four days in the bed.

He walked on water in the storm.
He fed the five thousand with few loaves.
He turned the water into wine.
He calmed the sea with breath he blows.

The {{x|Gospels}} frame these miracles as signs,
as breakings-in of the kingdom new,
as demonstrations that death and demon
were not the final word, it's true.

He chose twelve for the twelve tribes of {{p|Israel}},
symbolic of restoration complete.
He taught the disciples to pray "Our Father,"
giving the kingdom's prayer to recite.

He reserved his sharpest words not for
the sinners, the prostitutes, the thieves,
but for the religious professionals,
the Pharisees who played with sleeves.

"Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees,
hypocrites, who strain out a gnat
and swallow a camel. Who tithe mint
and cumin, but neglect the fat

weightier matters of the {{x|Law}},
justice, mercy, and faithfulness.
You whitewashed tombs, beautiful outside,
full of dead men's bones and ugliness."

This made enemies. And when he went
to {{p|Jerusalem}} for Passover,
rode in on a donkey to cheers,
overturned the money-changers' tables in {{p|Temple}} stover,

the authorities decided to act.
The high priest Caiaphas said
"it is better for one man to die
than for the whole nation to be red."

Judas Iscariot betrayed him
for thirty pieces of silver, paid.
He kissed him in the {{p|Garden of Gethsemane}}
and showed the soldiers where he stayed.

He was arrested, tried by the {{x|Sanhedrin}},
tried by Pilate, tried by Herod Antipas,
sent back to Pilate, who washed his hands,
and let the crowd choose Barabbas instead.

The crowd chose Barabbas. Jesus was
scourged, crowned with thorns, made mocking king,
forced to carry his cross to {{p|Golgotha}},
the place of the skull, the ugliest thing.

Crucifixion was the {{p|Roman}} method
for rebels, slaves, the lowest of men.
He hung with thieves on either side
for six hours, and then spoke seven "amens":

"Father, forgive them, they know not
what they do." "Today you shall be with me
in {{p|Paradise}}." "Woman, behold your son."
"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"

"I thirst." "It is finished."
"Father, into your hands I commit
my spirit." And with these words he died.
The veil of the {{p|Temple}} was split.

Earthquake. Darkness. The centurion
who watched him die said "Truly this man
was the Son of God." They took him down
at sunset, before the {{x|Sabbath}} ran.

Joseph of Arimathea provided
a new tomb, cut in rock, nearby.
They wrapped him in linen with spices,
and rolled a stone, and said goodbye.

On the third day, the women came
to finish the anointing at dawn.
The stone was rolled away. The tomb
was empty. His body was gone.

Angels said "He is not here, he is risen."
Mary Magdalene met him in the garden
but did not recognize him at first.
He sent the twelve to make the kingdom's pardon

known to all nations, to baptize
in the name of Father, Son, and Holy Ghost,
teaching all he had commanded,
and he would be with them at the post

of every moment until the end.
Then he ascended to heaven's height,
with the promise that he would return
to judge the living and the dead in light.

This is the {{x|Christian}} claim, the core.
Not Jesus the teacher only. Not Jesus
the failed messiah, dead and done.
But Jesus who rose, and reigns, and frees us.

Whether the tomb was empty on that morning,
whether the post-Easter appearances
were objective or subjective vision,
whether what happened was what appears to us,

is the question that divides the ages.
The religion he launched would sweep the world.
The deflationary polytheist
sees what the crucial swerve unfurled:

a {{x|Jewish}} messianic sect went forth
into the {{x|Greco-Roman}} sea,
and merged with Sol Invictus worship,
with mystery religions, gradually,

and became the faith of {{p|Rome}} itself
three centuries later under Constantine.
But for now, a small community
in {{p|Jerusalem}} wondered what they had seen.

They met in upper rooms to pray.
They broke bread together and shared all things.
They waited for the return imminent.
And the spirit came on them on tongues' wings.


# Chapter 181: Buddhism Enters the Han

While Jesus walked in {{p|Galilee}},
the Han Dynasty in the {{p|East}}
was reaching its high mature phase,
and a strange new teaching entered its feast.

Emperor Ming of Han dreamed a dream
of a golden man flying west-to-east,
and his advisors said: this is the Buddha,
the sage from {{p|India}}, the teaching priest.

He sent ambassadors down the {{p|Silk Road}}
to find the monks and the scriptures both.
They returned with two {{p|Indian}} monks,
Kasyapa Matanga and Dharmaratna, of both.

They brought scriptures, statues, and monks
on a white horse, the legend says,
and the first {{x|Buddhist}} temple in {{p|China}}
was the {{p|White Horse Temple}} of those days.

This was the first crossing — around
sixty-seven of the {{x|Common Era}}.
{{x|Buddhism}} entered the {{p|Han Empire}}
by imperial sponsorship without fear.

But it would not take root for centuries.
The {{x|Chinese}} saw it as a curiosity,
a foreign teaching from the western regions,
not yet absorbed into their reality.

It competed with {{x|Daoism}} at first,
and the two borrowed from each other.
Daoists adapted {{x|Buddhist}} concepts.
Buddhists used {{x|Daoist}} terms as brothers.

The translation problem was immense.
How translate "nirvana" into {{x|Chinese}}?
How translate "dharma," "karma," "anatta"?
The monks did their best to seize.

They used {{x|Daoist}} "wu wei" for "nirvana,"
"dao" for "dharma," and hoped it stuck.
Later, better translators came who
coined new terms with better luck.

Meanwhile, to the east, in {{p|Korea}},
a different strand of the Eastern story:
the legend of Heo Hwang-ok, princess
of {{p|Ayodhya}}, sailed for coastal glory.

The legend says she was a princess
of the {{p|Kingdom of Ayuta}} in {{p|India}},
who sailed across the sea to {{p|Korea}}
in the first century, to marry plenty.

She married King Suro of {{p|Geumgwan Gaya}},
became queen of that small kingdom,
bore him ten sons, and two took her surname,
founding the {{x|Kimhae Heo}} lineage wisdom.

Whether the legend is literal or
symbolic of cultural connection made,
the maritime links between {{p|South Asia}}
and {{p|East Asia}} were already there, unafraid.

The {{x|Kimhae Heo}} and {{x|Kimhae Kim}} clans
both trace descent from Heo Hwang-ok.
Millions of modern Koreans trace
their lineage back to this {{p|Indian}} shock.

{{x|Buddhism}} would later come to {{p|Korea}}
through {{x|Chinese}} channels, in the fourth century,
but already in the first, there were
threads of contact, mythic currency.

In {{p|India}} itself, {{x|Buddhism}} was
splitting into schools and lineages,
{{x|Theravada}}, {{x|Sarvastivada}}, {{x|Mahasanghika}},
the {{x|Vibhajyavada}} and other sages.

The Kushan Empire under Kanishka
in the second century united
{{p|Bactria}}, {{p|Gandhara}}, and northwest {{p|India}},
where {{x|Greco-Buddhist}} art ignited.

The {{p|Gandhara}} school carved Buddha statues
in a {{x|Hellenistic}} {{x|Greek}} style,
with flowing robes like Apollo's,
with {{p|Mediterranean}} faces, for a while.

This is the origin of all Buddha images.
Before {{p|Gandhara}}, the Buddha was
represented only by symbols — wheel,
footprint, empty throne, the cause.

After {{p|Gandhara}}, the anthropomorphic
Buddha with long earlobes, topknot, robe
spread across {{p|Asia}} from {{p|India}} east,
became the icon of the globe.

Kanishka convened the {{x|Fourth Buddhist Council}},
where the {{x|Sarvastivada}} school was codified,
and missions went out across the {{p|Silk Road}}
to carry dharma to where it could hide.

{{x|Mahayana}} {{x|Buddhism}} was emerging,
"the {{x|Great Vehicle}}," with its new ideals:
the bodhisattva who forgoes nirvana
to save all beings, who feels what all feels.

The {{x|Lotus Sutra}}, the {{x|Heart Sutra}},
the {{x|Diamond Sutra}} were being written,
expanding the scope of the teaching from
the arhat path to the Buddha's wisdom.

{{x|Mahayana}} would be the form that traveled east
to {{p|China}}, {{p|Korea}}, {{p|Japan}}, {{p|Vietnam}}.
{{x|Theravada}} would survive in the south,
in {{p|Sri Lanka}}, {{p|Burma}}, {{p|Thailand}}, {{p|Cambodia}}'s psalm.

The {{p|Silk Road}} was the great conveyor.
From {{x|Kushan}} {{p|Gandhara}} eastward through
{{p|Tarim Basin}} oasis kingdoms,
monks and scrolls and statues flew.

{{p|Khotan}}, {{p|Kucha}}, {{p|Turfan}}, {{p|Dunhuang}},
the desert stations where caravans paused,
became centers of {{x|Buddhist}} translation,
cave monasteries where the great texts were housed.

{{p|Dunhuang}}'s caves would later shelter
the {{x|Diamond Sutra}}'s earliest print,
the world's oldest dated printed book,
from the ninth century, in preserved stint.

But that was later. For now, in the {{p|Han}},
the new teaching trickled in slowly,
a curiosity, a foreign cult,
not yet the cultural river, wholly.

The {{p|Han}} itself was showing strain.
The empress clans fought palace war.
The Yellow Turbans rebelled in anger.
The {{p|Han}} would fall in the third century, raw.

But that's the next chapter. For now,
mark the crossing: the teaching of
the Shakyamuni had crossed the mountains,
had entered {{p|China}}, the second love.

Two great transmissions. Two great streams
flowing out from the {{p|Indian}} source:
Ashoka's missions south and west;
these silk-road missions east, of course.

The dharma was becoming world-religion,
no longer just an {{p|Indian}} way.
The Buddha's "middle path" had proved
capable of crossing every sway.


# Chapter 182: Elagabalus

The {{x|Severan}} dynasty held {{p|Rome}}
in the early third {{x|Common Era}},
and produced one of the strangest emperors
that ever held imperial ear.

Elagabalus, age fourteen,
became emperor in two-eighteen,
and brought with him from {{p|Emesa}}
a cult that shocked the {{p|Roman}} scene.

He was hereditary priest of
Elagabal, the sun god of his city,
a black stone worshipped at a temple,
a {{x|Semitic}} deity, un-pretty

to {{p|Roman}} eyes that wanted Jupiter,
Mars, the ancestral {{x|Capitoline}} three.
He brought the black stone to {{p|Rome}} itself,
installed it above all others visibly.

He tried to marry it to Athena.
When that failed, he tried Astarte.
He held processions with the stone
in a chariot, barefoot, sweaty, hardly.

He is alleged — with what truth we
do not fully know — to have done many things:
married a {{x|Vestal Virgin}}, violating
the sacred celibacy that the office brings;

married several times to women,
married a charioteer in woman's dress,
preferred to be called "lady" by servants,
sought surgeons to perform a change of sex.

Whether these accounts are accurate,
or hostile propaganda spun
by enemies after his fall,
the modern scholars debate as one.

Some see in Elagabalus
an early transgender figure,
the first recorded ruler who
sought physical transformation with rigor.

Others see only slander, the
standard tropes of {{p|Roman}} invective
used to destroy reputations
of rulers who proved ineffective.

The deflationary polytheist
notes: whatever the truth, the attempt
to raise a {{x|Semitic}} sun god to
{{p|Roman}} supremacy was not exempt

from consequences. The {{p|Roman}} elites
were deeply offended. The {{x|Praetorian Guard}}
assassinated him at eighteen, dragged
his body through streets, threw it hard

into the {{p|Tiber}}, and his mother's body
with him, the sewer their tomb.
The black stone was sent back to {{p|Emesa}}.
The old gods reclaimed their room.

But the seed had been planted.
A sun-centered monotheistic faith,
imported from {{p|Syria}}, had briefly
occupied the imperial wraith.

This was the first attempt to make
solar monotheism {{p|Rome}}'s state cult.
It failed under Elagabalus.
It would succeed under Aurelian's result.

Aurelian fifty years later,
after the {{x|Crisis of the Third Century}},
would make Sol Invictus the state god,
the "Unconquered Sun" of imperial sanctuary.

That syncretic sun-cult would merge
with mystery religions and with others,
would become the cultural matrix
into which {{x|Christianity}} hovers.

December twenty-fifth was Sol Invictus'
birthday, the winter solstice celebration,
the "dies natalis Solis Invicti,"
the unconquered sun's inauguration.

Christians would later fix their {{x|Christmas}}
on this same date, adopting the feast,
merging the birth of the Son of Man
with the rebirth of the Sun in the {{p|East}}.

This is not accidental. The Sol Invictus
cult and the Christ cult were cousins,
sharing iconography, festivals, theology,
the rayed halo, the midwinter reasons.

Elagabalus failed. But the cultural current
he represented — eastern solar monotheism
colonizing the {{p|Roman}} imperial cult —
would continue its strong historical prism.

Three strands had been converging now
toward what would become monotheistic {{p|Rome}}:
{{x|Jewish}} exilic covenant-monotheism,
{{x|Persian}} dualistic cosmic home,

{{x|Egyptian}} Akhenaten's old Aten
(a lost current but not forgotten),
{{x|Syrian}} solar-theologies rising,
{{x|Platonic}} philosophy unforgotten.

All were feeding into the stream
that would break through under Constantine
and become the official faith of {{p|Rome}}
and then of {{p|Europe}}, clean and clean.

But we are not there yet. The third century
still held the old gods in their frame.
Elagabalus was the weird episode

# Chapter 196: Tang and the Great Persecution

The Tang Dynasty (618-907) was
the golden age of imperial {{p|China}}.
{{p|Chang'an}} was the greatest city in the world,
one million people in its central arena.

The founder Li Yuan (Gaozu) had been
a general who seized power from the {{x|Sui}}.
His son Taizong killed his brothers,
forced his father to abdicate on the cue.

Taizong's reign (626-649) is considered
the model of Confucian good rulership,
the era of peace and prosperity,
the golden reign held up as a reference for leadership.

Empress Wu Zetian rose under the third {{x|Tang}} emperor,
eliminated rivals, and became the only woman
to rule {{p|China}} as emperor in her own name,
founding the {{p|Zhou Dynasty}} briefly, a prism human.

She patronized {{x|Buddhism}}, elevated it above
{{x|Confucianism}} and {{x|Daoism}} temporarily.
Under her, the massive Buddha statues at {{p|Longmen}}
were carved, her face modeled, supposedly.

After her reign, the {{x|Tang}} continued
under Xuanzong, whose early reign was glorious
but whose later years fell to the disaster
of the An Lushan Rebellion, devastating notoriously.

An Lushan was a Sogdian general, trusted
and elevated by Xuanzong, who rebelled
in 755 and plunged {{p|China}} into civil war
for eight years. Millions died. The empire propelled

into a long decline. The census before
the rebellion recorded 53 million people.
After, the census recorded 17 million.
Whether this represents actual deaths or administrative steeple

failure, the scale was catastrophic.
The {{x|Tang}} survived but never recovered its former strength.
Xuanzong's beloved concubine Yang Guifei
was killed at his soldiers' demand, the length

of his long reign ending in tragedy.
The great poet Li Bai had been at his court.
The great poet Du Fu had lived through the chaos,
writing war poetry of unmatched import.

Poetry flourished in the {{x|Tang}}. Li Bai, Du Fu,
Wang Wei, Bai Juyi, the Four Greats,
became the classical masters of Chinese verse,
whose works every subsequent scholar debates.

In 845, Emperor Wuzong launched
the Great Anti-Buddhist Persecution
(the {{x|Huichang Persecution}}, named for his era).
It was the most thorough effusion

of state power against {{x|Buddhism}} in Chinese history.
Forty thousand temples and shrines were destroyed.
Two hundred sixty thousand monks and nuns were
returned to lay life, their wealth deployed.

The motives were mixed. Economic — the monasteries
held vast untaxed lands. Ideological — Wuzong was
a Daoist who thought {{x|Buddhism}} was foreign. Xenophobic —
foreign religions were targeted broadly without pause.

{{x|Manichaeism}} was virtually wiped out in {{p|China}}.
Nestorian Christianity, which had entered in the {{x|Tang}},
was also persecuted and largely destroyed.
Zoroastrianism among Sogdian communities sang.

{{x|Buddhism}} recovered somewhat after Wuzong's death
(his successor reversed the policy),
but the intellectual glory days were over.
Chan ({{x|Zen}}) would survive more readily.

Chan {{x|Buddhism}} was less dependent on scriptures,
libraries, and wealthy monasteries.
Its simple meditation-based practice
could survive with minimal institutional stories.

Pure Land {{x|Buddhism}} also survived,
through devotional practice by laypeople,
chanting the name of Amitabha Buddha,
requiring no temples or scholarly steeple.

These two schools — Chan and Pure Land —
would become the dominant forms of East Asian {{x|Buddhism}},
not the scholarly schools like {{x|Huayan}} or {{x|Tiantai}},
which required institutional support for their dualism.

The {{x|Tang}} declined through the ninth century.
Regional warlords, the Jiedushi, became
de facto independent. Eunuchs controlled the court.
Peasant rebellions erupted with blame.

In 907, the {{x|Tang}} was formally deposed.
The {{p|Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms}} period began,
fifty-three years of fragmentation,
until the Song Dynasty reunified in 960, man.

The {{x|Song}} would be more scholarly, less military,
less imperial, more refined.
It would lose the northern frontier to Khitans and Jurchens,
but would be culturally very defined.

The {{x|Song}} would invent movable type printing,
refine gunpowder, develop the compass.
{{x|Song}} painting and ceramics would be exquisite.
The civil service exam would be the crown accomplished.

But the {{x|Tang}} remained the memory, the ideal,
the civilization-peak to look back on.
"{{x|Tang}} poetry" still means the finest Chinese verse.
"{{x|Tang}}" is still how Chinese abroad call their people gone.
