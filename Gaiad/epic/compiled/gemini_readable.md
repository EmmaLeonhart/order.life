# Gemini (7宮) - Days 169-196


# Chapter 169: The Persian Empire

Cyrus the Great: a conqueror, and yet
The conquered thanked him; {{p|Judah}} named him blessed,
God's own anointed; {{p|Babylon}}, in debt,
Hailed him as freer; and the {{p|Greeks}} confessed

That he was just; and from the {{p|Indus}} plain
To the {{p|Aegean}}, {{p|Caucasus}} to {{p|Nile}},
Some thirty million lived beneath his reign
And his heirs', many tongues, and every style.

At {{p|Anshan}} he was born, the son of one,
Cambyses I, king of the {{p|Persian}} race,
Who served the {{p|Medes}}; his mother, he her son,
Was Astyages' daughter, {{p|Median}} grace.

He took his father's throne, and then he rose
Against the {{p|Medes}}, and took Astyages' land;
Then west, to {{p|Lydia}}, whose king, one knows,
Was Croesus, rich; who'd asked, at {{p|Delphi}}'s hand,

If he should war on {{p|Persia}}; and it said:
Cross {{p|Halys}}, and a great realm will be lost;
He crossed; it was his own; at {{p|Thymbra}}, dead
Hopes; {{p|Lydia}}, {{p|Ionia}}, the cost.

Then east he went, and held the plateau wide,
And {{p|Central Asia}}; then, when these were won,
{{p|Babylon}} fell with little fight; inside,
Its priests, who scorned their king, welcomed this one.

And Cyrus let the exiled peoples go
Back to their lands, and build their gods' own halls;
Not {{p|Judah}} only, all: he'd have them so,
Content and loyal, safe within their walls.

He wrote it on a cylinder of clay:
The gods restored, the peoples sent back home;
And then, against the {{p|Massagetae}}, one day,
He died in battle, far from where he'd roam.

His tomb at {{p|Pasargadae}}, of plain stone,
A gabled roof, still stands; and on it read:
O man, I'm Cyrus, who made {{p|Persia}}'s throne,
The king of {{p|Asia}}: grudge me not this bed.

His son, Cambyses II, then took the {{p|Nile}}:
Psamtik III fell there; and, crowned anew,
Cambyses ruled as pharaoh for a while,
And died; and then a crisis came in view:

For he'd had Bardiya, his brother, killed
In secret; and a false Bardiya rose;
Darius, of the royal kin, and skilled,
With {{p|Persia}}'s nobles, cast him down, and those

Who rose against him, nine, he put them down,
And carved it high on {{p|Behistun}}'s cliff face
In three tongues, him above them, with his crown;
And then he ordered all the realm's wide space:

Twenty {{p|satrapies}}, each one ruled, and
A general apart, a scribe to tell
The court of both; one tax in all the land;
One weight and measure; the {{p|daric}} as well,

Of gold; and roads, the {{p|Royal Road}}, with posts
A day's ride each, from {{p|Susa}} to the sea
At {{p|Sardis}}; and {{p|Persepolis}}, whose hosts
Of columns in the {{p|Apadana}}, free;

And round it, carved, the peoples bringing gifts:
The {{p|Medes}}, {{p|Elamites}}, and {{p|Babylon}}'s band,
{{p|Egypt}}, {{p|Ionia}}, by turns, in shifts,
{{p|India}}, {{p|Scythia}}, and {{p|Arab}} land.

Each kept its tongue, its gods, its rulers, rite,
And paid its tribute, sent its troops, and bowed;
No mass deportments, no forced faith, no blight
Of terror like {{p|Assyria}}'s; the crowd

Lived well beneath. Darius took the east,
The {{p|Indus}}, {{p|Thrace}} the west; but lost to {{p|Greece}}
At {{p|Marathon}}; and died, his reign's last feast
Thirty-six years; and Xerxes found no peace

In {{p|Greece}}, but lost at {{p|Salamis}}; and then,
As Ahasuerus, Xerxes would wed
Esther, of {{p|Judah}}'s house, among his men
At {{p|Susa}}; and built where his father led.

And after him, the kings kept what they'd won:
Artaxerxes, Darius II, and more;
{{p|Egypt}} rose often, and was overrun;
And plots and bribes and hired troops grew sore.

And {{p|Aramaic}} was the common tongue;
The stars of {{p|Babylon}} went east and west;
{{p|Greek}} doctors, {{p|Phoenician}} sailors, among
Their servants; {{p|Judah}}'s scribes; and all the rest.

The kings bowed to Ahura Mazda, but
They did not force their faith: in {{p|Babylon}},
Cyrus served Marduk; and in {{p|Egypt}}, what
The pharaohs did, Cambyses did; and on

{{p|Zion}} the temple rose with royal gold;
For the Wise Lord, the maker of all things,
Was served wherever right worship was told,
By any name; and so, as Wise Lord's kings,

They kept all rites: one rite, by many names,
One house, and many altars, and one lord;
And so the realm was home to all its claims,
And none were forced, and none put to the sword.

So honor {{p|Persia}}: Cyrus, who set free;
Darius, of the roads and of the coin;
The king who kept all rites, and let them be,
And let the many peoples, as one, join.


# Chapter 170: Classical Greece, Part Two

And {{p|Persia}} beaten, {{p|Athens}} rose: its fleet,
Themistocles' own fleet, had won the day
At {{p|Salamis}}; its city, burned, complete,
Was raised again; and it would lead the way.

Formed the {{p|Delian League}}, on {{p|Delos}}' isle,
To guard against the {{p|Persians}}: ships and gold
From all, the fleet from {{p|Athens}}; and a while
It freed the {{p|Greeks}} the {{p|Persians}} still would hold.

But those who sought to leave were held by force;
The treasury was moved from {{p|Delos}}' shrine
To {{p|Athens}}, and it built, by that resource,
The {{p|Acropolis}}; so the league's design

Became {{p|Athens}}' rule; others paid it due;
And {{p|Sparta}} feared it, and her friends; and war
Would come of it. But first the golden few
Years, from four-sixty to four-thirty, bore

The richest fruit the world had seen: the rule
Of Pericles; the men who were its own,
Some fifty thousand, met, and, by the rule
Of lot, held office; judged; and spoke alone

Upon the {{p|Pnyx}}; ten boards of ten; the courts
Of hundreds; war and treasure, voted through
By all; and Pericles, of all its sorts,
Was general yearly, the city's due.

And at the graves of {{p|Athens}}' dead he said:
Our rule is called the people's, for it's held
Not by a few, but all; and so they led,
Free men, by laws they made, and so excelled.

At the {{p|Dionysia}} plays were made,
Each poet striving for the prize; and three
Great makers of the tragedies were weighed:
Aeschylus, grand and mythic, first; and he

Wrote {{p|Oresteia}}: Agamemnon, back
From {{p|Troy}}, slain by Clytemnestra, his wife;
Orestes, son, avenging; then the track
Of blood was judged by Athena; the strife

Was ended by a court; the Furies turned
To Eumenides, kindly ones; and so
The vengeance of the clan for law was spurned,
And civic justice founded, long ago.

Sophocles, next, wrote men of weight who fought
Their fate: Oedipus, he who solved the Sphinx,
Yet killed his father, wed his mother, caught
Unknowing, and then blinded at the brinks;

Antigone, who buried, though the king
Forbade it, her own brother, by the law
Of gods above the city's; and the thing
She did stands as the higher law we saw.

Euripides, the last, more doubting, gave
A voice to women, slaves, the beaten side:
{{p|Medea}}, {{p|Bacchae}}, {{p|Trojan Women}}, brave
And harrowing; and later, far and wide,

Held greatest. And the comedy: the bite
Of Aristophanes, whose wives withdrew
Their love in {{p|Lysistrata}} till the fight
Should end; in {{p|The Clouds}} his mockery drew

Old Socrates, a basket in the air,
Who taught the worse to seem the better; and
The real one rose up in the crowd, and there
Stood, so they could compare him, where he'd stand.

Phidias made Athena, gold to see,
Forty feet high, with Nike in her hand,
And the Zeus at {{p|Olympia}}; and he
And his made the calm, balanced bodies grand.

The {{p|Parthenon}}: Ictinus drew its plan,
Callicrates too, and Phidias its stone;
Its columns curved to fool the eye of man,
Its frieze the great procession, gods there shown.

The {{p|Sophists}} came to teach the art of speech
For pay: Protagoras, he who said that man
Is measure of all things; and each to each
They taught both sides, and some feared what began.

And Socrates would take no fee, and taught
No doctrine, but said only that he knew
He nothing knew; he asked, and those he caught
Found that the things they knew were never true:

What's justice? Courage? Piety? He'd ask,
And test what they would answer, till they saw
They did not know; he took it as his task
From gods, to make men know, and find their flaw.

And he made many foes: the mighty, shamed
In public; those who kept the rites, who would
Not hear a man who said a god, unnamed,
Spoke in him; and the quarrel, as it stood,

In three-ninety-nine it was brought to court:
It asked who held the city's altars, who
Might speak for {{p|Athens}}' gods, and of what sort;
Of impiety, they said, and new gods too,

And leading youth astray; and yet he knew
The gods he served were {{p|Athens}}' gods as well.
Five hundred judged him; and he told them true:
His work, a gift; they found him guilty; fell

The sentence: hemlock; and he drank it, calm,
And would not flee; and dying, said, to one,
Crito: we owe Asclepius, for his balm,
A rooster; pay it. And so he was done.

He wrote nothing; Plato, who wrote him down,
Was his; the {{p|Academy}} was his own;
The forms, the threefold soul, the king whose crown
Was wisdom, and the soul, undying, shown.

And Plato's pupil, Aristotle, turned
To things themselves: to logic, physics, life,
The soul, the good, the city; and he learned
And wrote of all of them, and ended strife.

But {{p|Athens}} and {{p|Sparta}} then went to war,
Four-thirty-one; for twenty-seven years;
And Pericles died of the plague; and more,
The fleet at {{p|Syracuse}} was lost, in tears;

In four-oh-four, {{p|Athens}} gave in; its walls
Were torn down; and the {{p|Thirty Tyrants}} ruled
A while, till rule returned to people's halls;
But all were worn; and so the gold age cooled.

So honor {{p|Athens}}: Pericles's rule,
The three who wrote of grief, the one who laughed,
The {{p|Parthenon}}; and Socrates, no fool,
Who served his city's gods, and drank the draught.


# Chapter 171: The Buddha

Upon the {{p|Gangetic}} plain, the {{p|Brahmin}} priests
Kept up the {{p|Vedic}} rite of flame and gift,
And thought it held the world; but from the feasts
The {{p|Shramanas}} went out, to wander, shift

For their own freeing, without priest or shrine;
The {{p|Upanishads}} turned the rite within:
That Atman is Brahman; and {{p|karma}}'s line,
{{p|Samsara}}'s wheel, and {{p|moksha}}, freed from sin.

How can a man be free from pain, and death,
And birth again? The {{p|Jains}} would starve, and lore
Of {{p|Ajivikas}} said fate rules each breath;
The {{p|Charvakas}}: this life, and nothing more;

The {{p|Brahmins}} kept their sacrifice; and then
Into the field came one, Siddhartha, born
At {{p|Lumbini}}, of the {{p|Shakya}} men,
Shuddhodana's, the chief's son; Maya, torn

From life, his mother; and the son was raised
By Mahapajapati; the sages said:
King, or teacher; and his father, amazed,
Kept him from grief, and sickness, and the dead.

At sixteen wed Yashodhara, and had
A son, Rahula; and at twenty-nine
He went outside the walls, and saw the sad
Four Sights: the old, the sick, the dead, the sign

Of one who wandered, seeking; and that night
He left his palace, wife, and son, and cut
His hair, and took the robe, and sought the light,
The way from pain; and every door was shut.

He learned from two great teachers, deep and wise,
Absorptions; but they did not end the pain;
Six years with five ascetics, under skies
Of cold and heat, he fasted, and would strain,

Breath held, so thin his spine could be felt through
His belly; and the freedom did not come;
He saw that self-torment, like pleasure, too,
Kept mind on body; and the middle sum

Was the way: he took from Sujata's hand
Rice pudding; and his five friends went away;
And at {{p|Bodh Gaya}}, where the fig trees stand,
He sat, and vowed he would not move till day

Had shown him what he sought; and Mara came,
The lord of death and seeming, with delight
And fear, and said he had no right, no claim;
He touched the earth for witness; and the night

Was ended, Mara beaten; and at dawn
He saw how pain arose, and how it ends;
The Buddha, the awakened, from then on,
At thirty-five; and so his teaching wends.

There's suffering, {{p|dukkha}}: all that's made will pass,
And even joy is shadowed by its end;
Its cause is craving, {{p|tanha}}, grasp and grass
Of wanting, from not seeing; it can end:

{{p|Nirvana}}, blowing out, the flame's last fuel;
And there's a path, the {{p|Eightfold Path}}: right view,
Intention, speech, and action, as a rule,
Livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and true

Concentration; not pain nor pleasure, but
The middle way, for anyone to walk.
No self, {{p|anatta}}: and the door is shut
On any lasting self; the {{p|skandhas}}' stock,

The form, the feeling, sense, the shaping, mind,
Are flux, and none of them a self; and so,
In seeing through it, craving falls behind,
And there is no self saved, but self let go.

He taught for forty-five years; and he made
The {{p|sangha}}; kings and beggars, Brahmins, came,
And those of no caste, and he did not grade
By caste; and women, at his nurse's claim,

And Ananda's; and spoke the common tongue,
Not {{p|Sanskrit}}; and to questions that were vain,
Is the world endless, is the self among
The body, would he live past death, he'd feign

No answer, but he said: a man is hit
With poisoned arrow, and he will not let
It out until he knows who shot, and it,
The bow, the feathers; he'll be dead, and yet

Not know. His care was pain, and how to free.
At {{p|Kushinagar}} he died, eighty, old;
All made things pass; strive on, with care, said he;
And left no book, no heir, but what was told

To the {{p|sangha}}, the {{p|Dharma}} as its guide;
And still today his path is walked by those
Some five hundred million, spread far and wide,
Who sit beneath the fig tree, as he chose.

He did not say there were no gods: on high
The {{p|devas}} live long in heavens, and turn
Upon the wheel as well, and they must die
And be reborn, and free themselves, and learn.

Our song: the gods are many, and live long,
None is the centre; and no second creed
Stands here beside our own; and all along
This book has said the same: the Buddha's seed

Is our own rite; the middle way's our road,
And his the fig tree, and the dawn he'd see;
Kept under names his country's tongue bestowed.
So honor him, the one who woke, and free.


# Chapter 172: Classical Greece, Part Three

And {{p|Athens}} lost the war; and yet the {{p|Greeks}}
Went on, less strong, but sharper in their thought;
The fourth age brought the two whose wisdom speaks
The loudest: Plato, and the one he taught.

Plato's early dialogues asked, as he,
Old Socrates, had asked, what justice is,
Or courage, piety, and left, unfree
Of doubt, the asker: {{p|Euthyphro}}, and his

The {{p|Laches}}, the {{p|Charmides}}; and then he made
His own: {{p|Republic}}, {{p|Phaedo}}, and the feast
Of the {{p|Symposium}}, {{p|Phaedrus}}; and laid
Out there the {{x|Forms}}: behind each thing, the least

Of beauties, justice, shapes, there is a Form,
The Beautiful itself, the Just, more true
And lasting than the thing; and reason, warm,
Not sense, can see them; and the wise man's due

Is climbing to them. In the {{p|Cave}}, he wrote,
Men sit in chains, and watch the shadows play,
And think them real; the wise man, freed, remote,
Climbs to the sun, and sees, and then the way

Back down, to free the rest, though they resist;
As Socrates had done, and died for it.
{{p|Republic}}: let the wise, who've seen and kissed
The {{x|Forms}}, be kings; the many, who'd submit

Opinion, should not rule; the guards below,
And then the farmers, craftsmen, traders, each
To his own work; for {{p|Athens}}, he would know,
Had killed his teacher, by its vote and speech.

And later, in the {{p|Parmenides}}, he tried
His {{x|Forms}} again, and doubted; in his {{p|Laws}},
He gave the kings up, and a code supplied;
And in the {{p|Timaeus}}, sang the world's first cause.

And his {{p|Academy}} in {{p|Athens}} taught;
And there, a young {{p|Macedonian}}, came one,
Aristotle, of {{p|Stagira}}, who sought
Wisdom with Plato twenty years, till done.

Passed over, he went off to {{p|Lesbos}}' shore,
And studied living things; then was called home
To {{p|Macedon}}, to teach the young prince, before
He was king: Alexander, who would roam.

And then to {{p|Athens}}, and the {{p|Lyceum}}, where,
Walking, he taught; and when the prince was dead,
They turned on him; to {{p|Chalcis}} he fled there,
And died there in the next year, sick, in bed.

He wrote of logic, {{p|Organon}}; of all
Of nature, {{p|Physics}}, {{p|Heavens}}, weather, birth;
Of animals; the soul; and what we call
{{x|Metaphysics}}, after the physics' worth;

The {{p|Ethics}}: that the end of man is to
Flourish, {{p|eudaimonia}}, virtue's way;
{{p|Politics}}, and {{p|Rhetoric}}; and he knew
The {{x|Poetics}}, catharsis, and the play.

And there were other schools: the {{p|Cynic}} men,
Who scorned all rules; and Diogenes lived there,
In {{p|Athens}}' market, in a jar; and then
The great Alexander once came to where

He lay, and asked what he could do for him,
Diogenes said: just stand out of my sun.
The conqueror of all could not, by whim,
Give anything to one who needed none.

And Pyrrho went with Alexander's host,
To {{p|India}}, and learned there, and came back
To teach that every claim, the least, the most,
Had arguments both ways; and so the track

Was to hold back, and live in peace; and then
Epicurus then taught that pleasure, freed
Of pain and fear, is good; and that all men,
And gods, and death, are atoms; and the need

Is simple life, and friends, and not to fear
The gods or death. And all these schools would grow,
Each with its masters, texts, and fellows near,
And each from that one {{p|Greek}} root, long ago.

And Philip II of {{p|Macedon}}, in the north,
Half-{{p|Greek}}, as some said, beat the southern towns
At {{p|Chaeronea}}; and the cities, forth
From then, served him, the {{p|League of Corinth}}'s crowns;

Philip died; Alexander, twenty, took
The throne, and crushed the risings one by one,
He turned on {{p|Persia}}, as his father's book
Had planned; and so the free towns' age was done.

So honor {{p|Greece}}'s last age: Plato's cave,
And Aristotle's all; the {{p|Cynic}}'s jar;
The {{p|Skeptic}}'s pause; calm Epicurus gave;
And Alexander, setting out afar.


# Chapter 173: The Mahabharata

Vyasa, the compiler, sage, was there,
And saw it, and he told it: for his own
Were Dhritarashtra, Pandu, and the heir
Vidura; and from them the war was grown

Of cousins came, the {{p|Kuru}} house that ruled
At {{p|Hastinapura}}: Pandu's five sons
Called {{p|Pandavas}}; and hundred, who were schooled
As {{p|Kauravas}}, the blind king's many ones.

The blind one could not rule; and Pandu reigned,
And died; the blind one ruled for all the young;
And then Duryodhana, his eldest, strained
Against Yudhishthira, the first among.

The five: Yudhishthira, the righteous, true;
Bhima, the strong; Arjuna, with the bow;
Nakula and Sahadeva, the two
Twins, handsome; and one wife they'd share and know,

Draupadi, won by Arjuna; and when
Their mother Kunti said: share what you've won,
Not knowing, she was wife to the five men.
And Duryodhana envied them; and none

Of his attempts could kill them: house of lac
They burned; they fled; the poisoned sweet; the snare;
And then the dice: Shakuni, at his back,
Played loaded dice; Yudhishthira sat there,

Who would not turn a game down, and he lost
His kingdom, wealth, his brothers, and himself,
And Draupadi; and she was dragged, the cost,
Into the hall, to strip her, like a shelf;

But Krishna made her sari without end,
And none could strip her; but the deed was done.
They went to exile: twelve years, and to spend
The thirteenth hidden; and when that was run,

Duryodhana would not give back their land;
And war came: at {{p|Kurukshetra}}, the field,
For eighteen days, and every house's hand
Was in it; and the dead lay, none would yield;

And on its eve, Arjuna looked across
At cousins, teachers, kin, and sank in grief:
How could he kill them, bearing such a loss?
His driver: Krishna, Vishnu, come in brief

To earth; in the {{p|Bhagavad Gita}} sang:
The body dies, the soul does not; so fight;
Act, but let go the fruit, and do not hang
On gain or loss; give all as offering right;

The three paths: deed, and knowledge, and the love
Of god; and all are good. And then he showed
His form: all worlds, all beings, all, above
And under, eating warriors, past and owed,

The source of all, and where all things return;
And Arjuna could barely bear the sight;
And then he took his bow up, with no turn
Of doubt; and so began eighteen days' fight.

Yet dharma there was hard: to tell a lie
To win? (Krishna said yes.) And Karna, born
A {{p|Pandava}}, raised by {{p|Kauravas}}: why
Should he leave him who raised him? So, forlorn,

He chose his friend, and died; and Bhishma, old,
Bound by his oath to who would hold the throne,
Fought those he loved, and fell, as he was told,
On arrows, by Arjuna's hand alone.

And all were dead: Duryodhana lay low,
His hundred brothers gone, the teachers, friends;
The {{p|Pandavas}} had won, and stood in woe
Over the field of kin, the bitter ends.

Yudhishthira was crowned, and ruled for long,
Thirty-six years; then left, with brothers, wife,
Up to the {{p|Himalayas}}; and the throng
Fell one by one, and left, along with life,

Him and a dog, which was the god of right;
He would not leave the dog, and he went in
To heaven; there he found, in that strange light,
Duryodhana, and his own kin in sin

Below; and that was the last test; for good
Does not make sure of joy, and judgment's thin
Before the world. And Bhishma, as he could,
Had taught him, dying, peace and law within.

And Krishna was Vishnu, come down to earth,
As he would come when right was threatened: fish,
Matsya; Kurma; Varaha, the boar's birth;
Narasimha, man-lion; dwarf, by wish,

Vamana; Parashurama; the king
Rama; Krishna; and the Buddha; and last,
Kalki, who comes to end the dark age' ring:
The god comes down to us, and holds us fast.

So honor them: the five, the hundred, all,
Draupadi, Karna, Bhishma, arrow-laid;
And Krishna, at the chariot, the call,
Who sang the {{p|Gita}}, and the war was made.


# Chapter 174: Alexander the Great

Great Alexander of {{p|Macedon}}, the third,
In thirteen years, from {{p|Greece}} to {{p|Indus}}' shore,
Took all the lands, and {{p|Persia}}'s throne, and stirred
The world; and died at thirty-two, no more.

His father, Philip II, had made the north
A force: the phalanx, with its pikes held long,
The {{p|sarissas}}, and horse, and so went forth
To {{p|Thrace}}, {{p|Illyria}}, the southern throng,

And beat them at {{p|Chaeronea}}; and made
The {{p|League of Corinth}}; and he planned to go
Against the {{p|Persians}}, to repay the raid
Of Xerxes; but he was slain there, and so,

At his own daughter's wedding, by his guard;
Some said Olympias had willed it, or
Her son; and Alexander, twenty, hard
And young, took up the throne, the host, the war.

The north rose, and he beat it; {{p|Thebes}} rose too,
And he destroyed it, slew and sold its folk,
And spared the house of Pindar, poet; through
That terror, {{p|Greece}} was quiet, under yoke.

He crossed the {{p|Hellespont}} with thirty-five
Thousand, beat {{p|Persia}} at {{p|Granicus}}' stream,
Freed the {{p|Ionian}} {{p|Greeks}}; still alive,
Darius III met him, like a dream,

At {{p|Issus}}, fled, and left his wife and girls
Behind; and Alexander kept them well;
Darius offered land, and gold, and pearls,
And a daughter; Parmenion said: I'd sell,

If I were Alexander. So would I,
He said, if I were Parmenion. And south
To {{p|Tyre}}, which held its isle seven months; by
A causeway that he built from mainland's mouth

He took it, and he slew or sold its men;
And {{p|Gaza}} too; and {{p|Egypt}}, hating those
Who'd ruled it, welcomed him, and crowned him then
As pharaoh; and to {{p|Siwa}}, where there rose

The shrine of Ammon in the desert sand,
He went, and there the oracle said: son
Of Zeus-Ammon; and he held to it, and
He founded {{p|Alexandria}}, the one

The greatest of the cities of his name,
Where sea and {{p|Nile}} and {{p|Red Sea}} trade would meet.
And east, at {{p|Gaugamela}}, there he came
Against Darius, many times his fleet

Of men, and beat him; and Darius fled;
And {{p|Babylon}} and {{p|Susa}} opened wide,
And then {{p|Persepolis}}, where, as he said,
For {{p|Athens}}' burning, or for {{p|Persia}}'s pride,

He let the palace burn. And Bessus, then,
The satrap, slew Darius, and was king;
And Alexander caught him, killed him; when
He'd done it, he was heir, not foreign thing,

The {{p|Great King}}: and he took the {{p|Persian}} court,
Its ways, its wives, and bade his men wed too;
And {{p|Greek}} and {{p|Persian}} he'd join in one sort;
His {{p|Macedonians}} hated it; through

The plots, he slew Philotas, and he then
Slew Parmenion; and Cleitus, who had saved
His life, he killed while drunk; and so, of men,
He grew afraid, and darker, and depraved.

He went on east: to {{p|Bactria}}, and wed
Roxana; through the {{p|Hindu Kush}}; and down
The {{p|Indus}}; at the {{p|Hydaspes}}, there, he led
Against King Porus, elephants, and crown,

And won; and asked him how he'd treat him, then;
Like a king, Porus said; and he was made
A king again, his vassal. But his men
Would go no farther; and so there he stayed

At {{p|Hyphasis}}: they would not march; he sulked
Three days; and then turned back, and sailed the stream
To the sea; and marched, while thirst and dust skulked,
Through {{p|Gedrosia}}; thousands died, a dream;

And Nearchus sailed beside; and back he came
To {{p|Babylon}}, and planned to take the west;
And bade the {{p|Greeks}} call him a god by name;
And drank, and fevered, and was laid to rest

At thirty-two; his body, borne away
To {{p|Alexandria}}, entombed, to lie.
His heir unborn, his generals took sway:
Ptolemy, {{p|Egypt}}; Seleucus, east, high;

Antigonus, {{p|Macedon}}; and many more;
And {{p|Greek}} became the tongue from sea to sea,
In twenty {{p|Alexandrias}}, the store
Of all learning; and the Buddha would be

Carved in the {{p|Greek}} way, in the east; and so
The roads he opened carried west and east.
So honor Alexander: how he'd go,
And take the world, and found it, and the feast.


# Chapter 175: The Maurya Empire

When Alexander turned from {{p|Indus}}' shore,
Out of the north of {{p|India}} there rose
A house that held the land as none before:
{{p|Maurya}}, from {{p|Bengal}} westward it goes,

From the {{p|Hindu Kush}} down to the {{p|Deccan}}:
Chandragupta Maurya founded it,
And by his side, Chanakya, the wise man
Kautilya, whose book of rule was writ

Taught how to rule: the taxes and the spies,
The friends abroad, the plots, the risings crushed,
All cold and whole; and by it he would rise,
And cast the {{p|Nandas}} down, and all was hushed;

And at {{p|Pataliputra}} he set his seat;
And then Seleucus came, to take the west
Of {{p|India}} back, and he met defeat,
Gave up {{p|Afghanistan}} and all the rest,

{{p|Balochistan}}, for elephants; and sent
Megasthenes, who told of the roads and feasts,
Of wealth, of castes; and of the king, who spent
His nights in fear, and slept, as hunted beasts,

Changed beds each night, with women guards; and kept
Them close; and at the last he would lay down
His throne, and took the {{p|Jain}} way, and he stepped
Into the fast, {{p|Sallekhana}}, no crown.

Bindusara, his son, took {{p|Deccan}}'s south;
The {{p|Greeks}} had named him Amitrochates; sent
To Antiochus for wine, figs, the mouth
Of a sophist; and the answer back went:

The wine and figs, yes; but our law forbids
That we should sell philosophers. And then
Came Ashoka, who, as the story bids,
Slew brothers for the throne, and ruled like men

Before him, hard; and then made war upon
{{p|Kalinga}}, on the east coast; and he won;
A hundred thousand slain, and, carried on,
A hundred fifty thousand; and when done,

He saw the dead, and grieved; and he became
Follower of the Buddha, and he made
The {{p|Dhamma}} his: no more war; and the same
Kindness to beasts; and meat no more was laid;

He founded hospitals, and dug the wells,
And planted trees along the roads; and stone
Pillars, with lions, wheels, whose writing tells
His {{p|Dhamma}}: be kind; honor elders; own

Respect for priests of every faith; for who
Speaks ill of others' faith, shames too his own;
And so he wrote, and thirty more stand true,
In his own voice, upon the pillars' stone.

He held the third great council, and sent out
The monks: across the strait went his own son
Mahendra, Sanghamitra, daughter, out
To {{p|Greeks}} and to {{p|Central Asia}}, each one;

And so the Buddha's way went out across
The world. His {{p|Dhamma}} was for all, not one
Faith only: right, and truth, and gifts, no loss
To any beast; and so the king's rule won

Its right by goodness, not by blood or sword;
And beasts had places where they were not slain.
And still today his wheel, his lion, ward
The land of {{p|India}}, on flag and plain.

He died; and after him the realm grew small,
Its governors their own; and, in a coup,
Pushyamitra Shunga made the fall
Of the last {{p|Maurya}}; the {{p|Shunga}}'s new.

Honor them: Chandragupta, and the cold
Wise Chanakya; and great Ashoka, who
Grieved for the dead of war, and then would hold
The world in kindness, and sent the monks through.


# Chapter 176: The Punic Wars

And in the west, two cities rose to fight
For all the sea: {{p|Rome}} on the {{p|Tiber}}'s bank,
Which Romulus had founded; ruled by right
Of seven kings, till Tarquin, proud, then sank,

Cast out; then the {{p|Republic}}: consuls, two,
Each checking each; the {{x|Senate}}, old and wise;
The people's votes; the tribunes, who could do
The veto for the poor; and so to rise

Through all of {{p|Italy}}: {{p|Etruscans}}, all
The {{p|Samnites}}, {{p|Sabines}}, {{p|Latins}}, and the {{p|Greeks}}
Of southern {{p|Italy}}; and then the call
Across the strait, where {{p|Carthage}} ruled the creeks.

For {{p|Carthage}}, from {{p|Phoenician}} {{p|Tyre}}, had grown
Upon {{p|Tunisia}}'s coast, and ruled the trade
Of {{p|Africa}}, {{p|Iberia}}'s ore, sown
{{p|Sardinia}}, {{p|Corsica}}; fleets were made

The greatest; and its tongue was {{p|Punic}}, kin
To {{p|Hebrew}}; and its gods, Baal Hammon and
Tanit; and in the {{p|tophet}}, laid within,
Were infants, given in crisis by land.

And {{p|Sicily}}, half {{p|Greek}} and half {{p|Punic}},
Was where they met; and three wars they would fight.
The first: {{p|Rome}} had no fleet, and, stubborn, quick,
Copied a {{p|Punic}} ship, and, with the might

Of {{p|corvus}}, boarding-bridge, made sea to land;
And won, and {{p|Sicily}} was {{p|Rome}}'s; and gold
Was paid; and while {{p|Carthage}}'s hired band
Rose, {{p|Rome}} seized {{p|Sardinia}}, and the hold

Of {{p|Corsica}}; Hamilcar Barca's son,
Hannibal, swore at the altar, when young,
Undying hate for {{p|Rome}}; and Spain was won
By Hamilcar, Hasdrubal; and among

Them, Hannibal; and then the second war:
He crossed the {{p|Ebro}}, {{p|Pyrenees}}, and {{p|Gaul}},
The {{p|Alps}} in winter, elephants, and more,
And came to {{p|Italy}} with half of all,

And won at {{p|Trebia}}; and by the lake
Of {{p|Trasimene}} ambushed a host and slew
Flaminius; and then, at {{p|Cannae}}, he'd make
The center yield, the wings close round them, through;

Some fifty thousand {{p|Romans}} died that day;
And many allies went to him; but {{p|Rome}}
Would not give in; its {{x|Senate}} would not pay;
Fabius would not fight, but kept at home

The war by waiting, cutting off his bread;
Scipio took {{p|Spain}}; and {{p|Syracuse}} fell,
And Archimedes, by a soldier, dead;
Scipio took war to {{p|Carthage}} as well,

And at {{p|Zama}} beat Hannibal; and lost
{{p|Carthage}}'s fleet, its lands abroad; it paid
A mighty sum; and Cato, at all cost,
Ended each speech: {{p|Carthage}} must be unmade.

The third war: three years' siege; the city fell,
Was burned, and razed, its people sold, and salt
Sown in its ground; and {{p|Africa}} as well
Was {{p|Rome}}'s; and {{p|Punic}} {{p|Carthage}} came to halt.

And {{p|Rome}} was changed: a fleet, and lands abroad,
Grain from {{p|Sicily}}, {{p|Africa}}; and gold
From {{p|Spain}}; and generals great; and, overawed,
The farmers, ruined while away, were sold

Their land to rich men with their slaves; the throng
Of poor men crowded {{p|Rome}}; and then it turned
East: {{p|Macedon}}, and then {{p|Greece}}, were {{p|Rome}}'s ere long,
{{p|Seleucids}} at {{p|Magnesia}}; it learned

To call the sea its own. And he, the great
Hannibal, exiled, went to foes of {{p|Rome}};
With Prusias, when {{p|Rome}} came for him, his fate
Was poison, which he drank, far from his home.

So honor Hannibal, who crossed the snow
With elephants, and won at {{p|Cannae}}'s field;
And {{p|Rome}}, which would not break, though brought so low;
And {{p|Carthage}}, which was razed, and would not yield.


# Chapter 177: Qin and Han

In {{p|China}}, seven warring states had fought
Two hundred years; then {{p|Qin}} took one by one:
{{p|Han}}, {{p|Zhao}}, {{p|Wei}}, {{p|Chu}}, {{p|Yan}}, {{p|Qi}}; and all was brought
To one, and {{p|Warring States}} at last were done.

Young Qin Shi Huang, king at thirteen years, ruled
Through regents first, and then himself; and he
Was First Emperor; thought his line was schooled
To last ten thousand ages; and decree

Made one the script, the {{p|small seal}}; weights, and coin,
And measures, and the axles of the carts;
He cast the old lords down, and made men join
His service by his choice, in all the parts;

He split the land in thirty-six, and set
Each with a governor, a guard, and one
To watch them; built the roads, canals; and yet
Joined the old walls, the {{p|Great Wall}}, northward run,

Against the {{p|Xiongnu}}; he burned the books
Not useful to his rule; buried alive
Four hundred sixty scholars; and he looks
To {{p|Legalism}}, the way that he'd thrive:^[The burning of the books and burying of the scholars is told by Sima Qian, a historian of the Han, the dynasty that overthrew the Qin; some historians think the account exaggerated.]

Han Feizi's way: that men are selfish, and
Hard laws, the same for all, with clear reward
And harsh reproof, alone can rule the land,
Not virtue in the king; and by that cord

He ruled, hard and exact. And he feared death,
And sent out men to find the deathless brew;
And took the mercury, to keep his breath,
Which killed him; and he built a tomb, and through

{{p|Lintong}}'s ground, and all around it stood
The {{p|Terracotta Army}}, thousands strong,
Each face its own, with horse and car, and good
To guard him in the dark; and still, the long

Ranks stand. He died at forty-nine, away;
Li Si and Zhao Gao hid his death, and killed
The heir, and set a weak one up; one day
Men rose; and in three years the land was filled

With war; the capital fell; so the line
That should have lasted ages lasted, all,
Just fifteen years. But roads, and law, and sign
Of script, and provinces, outlived its fall;

And then Liu Bang, a peasant rebel, rose,
Fought Xiang Yu, the noble, strong of arm,
Four years, and won by patience; and the blows
At {{p|Gaixia}} ended him; free from harm,

He was Emperor Gaozu of the {{p|Han}};
And kept the {{p|Qin}}'s whole frame, but made it mild;
And Emperor Wu made {{p|Confucius}}' plan
The rule, and chose by learning, not by child

Of noble birth; and sixty million souls,
A fifth of all the world, and {{p|Chang'an}}, great
As {{p|Rome}}; and Wu sent armies, and their goals:
The {{p|Xiongnu}}, {{p|Nanyue}}, {{p|Korea}}'s state,

And west, to {{p|Ferghana}}, for heaven's horse;
And so the {{p|Silk Road}} opened; silk went west,
And glass and gold came east along its course,
And horses; and Sima Qian, the best

Of histories, the {{p|Records}}, wrote, from the
{{p|Yellow Emperor}} down to his own day;
And steel was made, the finest there could be;
And still the {{p|Chinese}} call themselves, today,

The {{p|Han}}, from that same house. So honor them:
The First Emperor, the wall, script, the clay
Ten thousand strong; and then Liu Bang, the stem
Of {{p|Han}}; and Wu, who sent the silk away.


# Chapter 178: Second Temple Period

From {{p|Babylon}}, back up to {{p|Zion}}'s height,
By Cyrus' leave, the exiles came, and raised
The {{p|Temple}}, small; and then, by blood and right,
Who was a Jew, was asked, and they were fazed:

They drew the line; Samaritans were turned
Away; the mixed were cast out. Ezra read
The {{x|Torah}} seven days, and all men learned,
At {{p|Water Gate}}; and Nehemiah spread

The walls again; the priesthood was the core,
The high priest ruled; and under {{x|Persian}}, then
The {{x|Macedonian}} rule, {{p|Judea}} bore
Its quiet years. And Alexander, when

He came, was shown the words of Daniel, read
Of him, and spared {{p|Jerusalem}}; and then
The Seleucids of {{p|Syria}} were head,
And ruled {{p|Judea}}, province of their men.

Antiochus IV Epiphanes then came,
Who meant the {{x|Greek}} way kept; he banned the {{x|Law}},
And sacked {{p|Jerusalem}}, and in its name,
On the Lord's altar there the people saw

The Lord of Heaven under the king's name:
Zeus of the heights; the same sky, and the sign
The same old altar; but his priests, his frame
Of rite, his swine, laid in the court; the line

The prophet wrote, there punning on that name,
Called the abomination; and the stand
Was at one altar, set in fear and shame:
Who'd stand before it, whose law rule the land.

And then Mattathias the priest refused
To make the {{x|Greek}} rite, and he slew the one
Who came to make it; and his son, who used
The name of Hammer, Judas, was his son,

Made war from hills against the Greeks, and won
The {{p|Temple}} back, and made it clean again;
And {{x|Hanukkah}} keeps the eight days begun
With oil for one day, burning eight days then.

{{x|Hasmoneans}} rose from that war, and were
Both king and high priest; and some held it wrong.
The Essenes went out to {{p|Qumran}}, to stir
No more, but wait the end, and keep their long

Scrolls in the caves; the Pharisees would teach
The oral law, the house of prayer, the home
Where each man read the {{x|Torah}}, each to each;
The Sadducees, the priests, kept but the tome

Of written law, no rising of the dead;
The Zealots wanted war with all who ruled;
And some wrote of the end, when God, they said,
Would break into the world, and all be schooled.

Then {{p|Rome}} came: Pompey marched in, sixty-three
Before the era, walked into the place,
The {{p|Holy of Holies}}, and he could see
Nothing: no god, no image, and no face,

But empty room, where once the {{x|Ark}} had stood
Before Nebuchadnezzar. And {{p|Rome}} set
Herod the Great, the {{x|Idumean}}, who would
Build palaces, the {{p|Temple}} greater yet,

The grandest in the east; but he was mad
With fear, and slew his sons, his wife; and slew
The infants of {{p|Bethlehem}}; what he had
Was split; and {{p|Rome}} sent governors; and through

The poor and those in debt, the hope rose high:
When would the Messiah come, the true son
Of David, drive out {{p|Rome}}, and bring, nearby,
The kingdom back, as in the days long done?

And many claimed it: Theudas, and the one
Judas the Galilean, and then he
From {{p|Egypt}} on the {{p|Olivet}}; and none
Prevailed. And John the Baptist, by the sea

Of reeds, the {{p|Jordan}}, preached: repent, and turn;
The kingdom's near; in camel-hair, he ate
The locusts and wild honey; and would burn
With words; and baptized many at the gate,

And Jesus of {{p|Nazareth}}; and then lost
His head to Herod Antipas, for he
Had cursed his wedding, and his law was crossed.
The kingdom seemed so near, the end so free.

For all agreed: one God, and not his face
In image; covenant, the {{x|Torah}}, rest
Of {{x|Sabbath}}, and clean food; and in each place
A faith that could be carried, east or west.

So honor them, who came back, and who kept
The law in every house, and waited long
For the anointed, while the {{p|Romans}} stepped;
And John, who cried the kingdom in his song.


# Chapter 179: Rome

When {{p|Carthage}} fell, then {{p|Rome}} stood over all
The {{p|Middle Sea}}; the farmers' little town
Now ruled from {{p|Spain}} to {{p|Thessaly}}; and small
Old forms, for farms and voters, broke, came down:

The legions served their generals; and the gold
Of provinces drowned the {{p|Italian}} plough;
The Gracchi Brothers saw it, and they told
The poor of land: Tiberius, and how

His brother Gaius too; and both were slain
By {{x|Senate}} men, in streets; and so it learned
That wealth would kill for wealth; and once again
The road to civil war was bloody, turned.

Marius made the legions volunteers,
Bound to their chief, and not the state; and then
Then Sulla marched on {{p|Rome}}, the first, in years
Of terror, dictator, and listed men

To die, and took their wealth; and then laid down
His rule, and went home, and died in his bed;
But what was done stayed done: the sword, the crown
Of armies, ruled; and politics was dead.

Pompey, the golden one, then cleared the sea
Of pirates, conquered east, took {{p|Judea}};
Crassus, the richest, crushed Spartacus; he
Bought {{x|Senate}}, armies; from the idea

Of Aeneas' line, and of Venus' race,
Came Julius Caesar, ablest of the three;
They made the {{x|First Triumvirate}}, in place
Above the {{p|Roman}} world, by their decree.

And Crassus died in {{p|Parthia}}, his head
Used in a {{x|Greek}} play as a thing to show;
And Pompey and Caesar warred; he led
His legions on the {{p|Rubicon}}, and so

The die was cast; and he chased Pompey far,
To {{p|Egypt}}, where the boy-king Ptolemy
Cut off his head, to please him; at that scar
Caesar wept there; and Cleopatra, he

Received, rolled in a carpet; and her bed
Made her the queen of {{p|Egypt}}; and he'd won
All {{p|Gaul}}, and wrote of it; at {{p|Zela}} said:
I came, I saw, I conquered; and was done,

Dictator for his life. And then, at last,
The {{x|Ides of March}}: Brutus, Cassius, the knives;
And Caesar fell; Mark Antony, aghast,
Spoke at the burial, and roused, with lives

At stake, the crowd against the killers; war
Began again; Octavian, his heir,
His grandnephew, cold-eyed, with patience, bore
His time; and Antony, Lepidus, there

Made the {{x|Second Triumvirate}} with him;
At {{p|Philippi}} they hunted Brutus down;
Then turned on each other; Lepidus, dim;
Antony went to Cleopatra's town,

And lived as pharaoh; Octavian stayed
In {{p|Rome}}, and built his name; and at the end,
At {{p|Actium}}, Agrippa, who obeyed
Octavian, won; they fled; and no friend

Was left: she died by asp, the royal snake,
The crown of {{p|Egypt}}; he by his own sword;
The {{x|Ptolemies}} were done; and so, to take
The rule alone, Octavian was lord,

Augustus, first, revered, for forty-five
Long years; and said he'd brought the old rule back;
The {{x|Senate}} met, the consuls stayed alive,
But he ruled all; and no one could attack.

So came the {{x|Pax Romana}}: law and peace
Two centuries; and roads, aqueducts, coin,
And courts, from {{p|Britain}} to the {{p|Tigris}}' lease,
From {{p|Rhine}} to the {{p|Sahara}}; all would join

One law, one coin, one road, one folk of men;
And some were good, and some were mad: the dark
Grim Tiberius on {{p|Capri}}; and then
Caligula, whose horse was consul; mark

Claudius, limping, wily; Nero, who
Played as {{p|Rome}} burned, and killed his mother, wife,
And Christians; he fell on his sword; and through
One year, four ruled: first Galba, losing life,

Otho, Vitellius; and then the man,
Vespasian, who held it, and who planned
{{p|Colosseum}}; and Titus, son, began
To burn the {{p|Second Temple}}, and the land

Was emptied: in the {{p|Arch of Titus}}, carved,
The menorah borne west; the Jews were spread
Through all the realm; the {{p|Temple}}'s rite was starved.
And then the {{x|Five Good Emperors}}, who led:

Nerva, Trajan, who took {{p|Dacia}}'s gold,
Reached greatest bounds; and Hadrian, who drew
Them back, built his wall; Antoninus, old
And pious; Marcus Aurelius, true

{{x|Stoic}} and king, who wrote, in {{x|Greek}}, alone,
His {{x|Meditations}}: say, at dawn, I'll meet
The meddler, the ungrateful; his own
Work was to serve them all; and in defeat

Of plague and Marcomanni by the stream
Of the {{p|Danube}} he died; his son would fight,
Commodus, as Hercules in his dream,
In games; a wrestler strangled him at night.

So honor {{p|Rome}}: the law, the road, the peace,
Caesar, Augustus, Marcus with his pen;
Two hundred years it held, till it would cease,
The greatest realm that had been made by men.


# Chapter 180: Jesus

When Herod died, the {{p|Galilee}} rose high,
and Varus burned {{p|Sepphoris}} for its war;
a {{p|Roman}} soldier, Pantera, passed by,
and Mary, young, was taken; and she bore

a son. The soldier was of {{p|Dan}}, the tribe
that never kept the Yahwist law, and so
no line is written for him, and no scribe
would write his fathers' names for us to know.

And Mary, in her grief, went to a pool
and fell, and told herself the child was sent
as Zarathustra's seed, in waters cool,
the {{x|Saoshyant}}, the savior that was meant.

The {{x|Magi}}, three, came by, and when she told
them what had happened, one of them said yes:
it may be so; and so she was consoled,
and Joseph took her, and the child, no less.

Through Joseph, he was Jeconiah's heir,
the exilarchs' and Zarathustra's line;
and John the Baptist was his cousin there;
they fled as refugees; that was the sign.

He had three fathers, as the old lines tell:
Joseph who raised him, Pantera of {{p|Rome}},
and Yahweh, who had chosen him as well;
and grew a Jew in {{p|Nazareth}}, his home.

He worked with wood, displaced, a poor man's son;
a magus on the road heard all his tale
and took him through the world, the two as one,
to cities where the mysteries prevail:

He learned of Dionysus and the vine,
and of the {{x|Saoshyant}} from priests of fire,
and knew himself beyond Messiah's line,
beneath his cousin John, who stood higher.

At thirty, near, he left his home, and came
Down to John, where the {{p|Jordan}}'s waters flow,
And was baptized; a voice from heaven's flame
Said: You are my beloved son; I know.

Into the wild he went for forty days,
And Satan tempted him; and then he went
To preach the kingdom, near, in all his ways:
Repent, for it's at hand; the same intent

As John the Baptist's; but where John had cried
The end, the wrath, the axe laid to the tree,
Jesus spoke of mercy, and opened wide,
For all who came, the poor, the sick, the free.

Five years he and his cousin went as one,
and preached to Gentiles, to the nations near;
he called himself the Lord, the Savior, Son
of God, the titles Caesar wore, to jeer.

He called twelve, fishermen, a man who took
The tax, a zealot, and through {{p|Galilee}}
He preached in synagogues, by field and brook,
In open air, to all who'd come and see.

He taught in parables, which left the end
Unsaid, for those who heard to think it through:
The prodigal; {{x|Samaritan}}, the friend
To beaten men; the mustard seed that grew;

The sower; the lost sheep, and the lost coin;
The pearl, the treasure hidden in the field;
And blessings that turned the world over: join
The poor in spirit, those who mourn, the healed,

The hungry, and the persecuted; blessed.
And two commands: love God with all your heart
And soul; your neighbor as yourself; the rest
Of all the {{x|Law}} hung on them, every part.

And then a third that none had said before:
Love your enemies; pray for those who hate
And harm you; be as perfect, to the core,
As your Heavenly Father, who, the great,

Sends sun and rain on just and unjust both:
Not mercy to your tribe alone, or friend,
But to the ones who hate you; such the oath,
The new and radical turn, without end.

He healed the sick, cast demons out; he raised
Jairus' daughter, and the poor widow's son
Of {{p|Nain}}; and Lazarus, four days, amazed,
Came from the tomb; and on the sea, as one

Who walked on land, he walked in storm; he fed
Five thousand from few loaves; made water wine;
He stilled the sea; and so the kingdom spread
Into the world, and death was not the line

That had the final word. He chose the twelve
For {{p|Israel}}'s twelve tribes, made whole again;
And taught them, so that all of them could delve,
To pray: Our Father; for all of men.

He kept his sharpest words, though, not for those
Who sinned, the thieves and prostitutes he'd share
His table with, but those whose rank arose
From Moses' chair, Pharisees, the scribes there:

Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, you blind
And hypocrites, who strain a gnat, and take
A camel down; who tithe the mint, and find
The cumin, but the weightier things forsake,

Justice, and mercy, faith; you whitewashed tombs,
Fair from outside, but full of dead men's bones,
Uncleanness, in your inner, hidden rooms.
And so he spoke to them, in such hard tones.

In thirty, Herod's sword took John away,
and Pilate, whom this book does not forgive,
had nailed him up by Passover that day,
the man who taught the poor that they would live.

He came up to {{p|Jerusalem}} to share
the Passover, and in the {{p|Temple}} threw
the tables down, and rode a donkey there,
a Caesar mocked, as cheering crowds well knew.

It's better that one man of us should die
than {{p|Rome}} come down on all, so said the priest,
afraid of {{p|Rome}}, gave him to {{p|Rome}} to try;
and it was {{p|Rome}} that killed him at the feast.

And Judas Iscariot sold him for
Thirty pieces of silver, and he kissed

# Chapter 181: Buddhism Enters the Han

While Jesus walked in {{p|Galilee}}, the {{p|Han}}
Stood high; and Ming of Han once saw, in dream,
A golden man who flew; and his wise man
Said: the Buddha, {{p|India}}'s sage, the gleam.

He sent his envoys down the {{p|Silk Road}}, west,
To find the monks and books; and they came back
With two: Kasyapa Matanga, best,
Dharmaratna, and a white horse's pack

Of scriptures and of statues; and they raised
The {{p|White Horse Temple}}, first in all the land,
In sixty-seven of the era; praised
By the emperor, it came by his hand.

But centuries would pass before it grew
Its roots: the offering, by western road,
Came to the court, which gave the shrine its due,
Not yet in every household and abode.

It stood beside the Daoists' altar, and
They shared one court; and each of them would look
For the throne's gifts; and each took, in that land,
The other's words, as brothers share a book.

And how to say {{p|nirvana}}, {{p|dharma}}, all
The words in {{x|Chinese}}? Used the {{x|Daoist}} way:
Wu wei for {{p|nirvana}}; and for the call
Of {{p|dharma}}, dao; till others found their say.

East, the princess Heo Hwang-ok came
From {{p|Ayodhya}}, {{p|India}}, by the sea,
To {{p|Korea}}, wed King Suro, took the name
Of queen in {{p|Gaya}}; her sons, in degree,

Were ten; and two took her own name; and so
{{x|Kimhae Heo}} and {{x|Kimhae Kim}} descend
From her; and millions of Koreans know
Her as their mother still, from end to end.

In {{p|India}} the Buddha's schools would split:
{{x|Theravada}}, {{x|Sarvastivada}}, and more,
{{x|Mahasanghika}}; and then Kanishka knit
The Kushan realm, {{p|Bactria}} to the door

Of {{p|India}}, {{p|Gandhara}}; where they made
The Buddha's form in {{x|Greek}} style, robes that fall
Like Apollo's; the faces were displayed
{{p|Mediterranean}}; the first of all:

For till then he was shown by sign alone,
The wheel, the footprint; now, with long-lobed ears,
The Buddha, topknot, robe, now filled the throne,
And went out east from there, and down the years.

And Kanishka called the {{x|Fourth Council}}, set
The {{x|Sarvastivada}}; and he sent monks out;
The {{x|Great Vehicle}}, {{x|Mahayana}}, met
The world: bodhisattvas, who go without

{{p|Nirvana}}, so that all may be set free,
And feel what all things feel; the {{x|Lotus}}, and
The {{x|Heart}}, the {{x|Diamond Sutra}} came to be;
And all of it went east across the land:

From {{p|Gandhara}}, then through the {{p|Tarim}}'s wells,
{{p|Khotan}}, {{p|Kucha}}, {{p|Turfan}}, {{p|Dunhuang}}, the stays
Of caravans; and in their caves and cells
The monks made the words new, in their own ways.

But that was later. For now, in the {{p|Han}},
The rite from the west trickled in, one door
Beside the older altars; and it ran
Into one house, not yet the river's roar.

Two streams went out from {{p|India}}: the one
Ashoka sent south and west; this, the east,
By silk-road; and the middle way had won
Its passage over mountains, and its feast.

So honor Ming, who dreamed the golden man,
The white horse monks; the queen who crossed the sea,
Heo Hwang-ok; and Kanishka's span;
And the one house, with one door more, set free.


# Chapter 182: The Fall of Han

While {{p|Rome}} strained, under Severans, the {{p|Han}}
Fell into pieces: eunuchs fought the men
Of learning; and the Yellow Turbans ran,
Rose, the Yellow Turban Rebellion, then,

With Zhang Jue and his brothers, at its core:
{{x|Way of Great Peace}}; he healed by faith and rite;
And when the plague and famine struck once more,
A hundred thousand came to him to fight.

And then the court of {{x|Han}} raised up great hosts,
And crushed them; but the generals grew too strong;
Dong Zhuo took the capital, and boasts
Of holding the boy emperor; the wrong

Was ended with his murder; and the lords
Of war cut {{p|China}} up like meat on bone;
Cao Cao, Liu Bei, Sun Quan, with all their swords,
Became the three, each ruling in his own:

Cao Cao, north, held the emperor, and ruled
In {{x|Han}}'s name; and Liu Bei, of {{x|Han}}'s own blood,
Who took the southwest, {{p|Shu}}; and, father-schooled,
Sun Quan the {{p|Yangtze}}'s south, the east, the flood,

{{p|Wu}}'s kingdom. In two-twenty, Cao Cao's son,
Cao Pi, then forced the last {{x|Han}} to give way;
The {{x|Han}} was dead; and {{p|Wei}} had now begun;
Liu Bei named {{p|Shu Han}}; {{p|Wu}}, another day:

The {{x|Three Kingdoms}}. Four hundred years the {{x|Han}}
Had lasted; and its end was end of all
The old order; then war, the ages' span
Of the north's kingdoms, and the southern fall.

And in this time {{x|Buddhism}} took deep root:
as the old order failed, the people turned
to the one rite from {{p|India}}, its fruit
in sutras and in monasteries earned.

Kumarajiva, from {{p|Kucha}}, brought
A hostage to {{p|Chang'an}}, made there anew
{{x|Lotus}}, {{x|Diamond}}, {{x|Vimalakirti}}, taught
In {{x|Chinese}}, as the standard, plain and true.

{{x|Daoism}} too became a church: the line
Of Celestial Masters, from {{x|Han}}'s late days,
With clergy, temples, canon, rites in sign
Of {{x|Buddhist}} forms; Ge Hong's alchemic ways

Sought deathless life. And of that age, the men:
Cao Cao, the cunning, ruthless; and the good,
Liu Bei, the rightful claimant; and again,
Zhuge Liang, wisest planner, as he could;

And Guan Yu, the sworn brother, loyal, true,
Whom still they honor; and their tales are told
In {{p|China}}, as {{x|Mahabharata}}, through
{{p|India}}; as the {{x|Iliad}}, of old.

So honor them, the three who split the land,
The one rite, rooting when the old went down,
Kumarajiva's words, and the true hand
Of Guan Yu, the faithful, without a crown.


# Chapter 183: Jimmu to Jingū

In {{p|Japan}}, of the isles, the first who reigned
Jimmu; from Amaterasu, his line,
The sun herself; her grandson, who obtained
The earth, was Ninigi, who, at the sign,

Came down on {{p|Takachiho}}, and he bore
The mirror, {{x|Yata no Kagami}}, and
The sword Kusanagi, won in the war,
The serpent's tail, slain by Susanoo's hand;

The jewel, the {{x|Magatama}}, the third;
And still today they're kept within the shrine,
Within the court, as it is told and heard,
The throne's regalia, handed down the line.

His grandmother was the sea-dragon's blood,
Toyotamahime, the dragon's daughter; so
The sun and sea were both in him, the flood
Below, the light above, as all would know.

From {{p|Kyushu}} east he marched; and he made peace,
And war, and deals, with all the chiefs he found,
Of many peoples; and he would not cease
To bargain and to bend, and give up ground,

To bind a messy league of many kin,
Of many tongues, together in one host;
At {{p|Kashihara}}, {{p|Yamato}}, within
His tent, he founded there his throne and post,

Six-sixty, {{p|February}}, in its time;
And still they keep it as the founding day.
And after him the kings in line would climb:
Sujin, the tenth; and then, along the way,

The twelfth, Keikō, whose son, the hero, slew
His brother, Takeru; and, sent to die,
On missions, conquered the Emishi through
The east; and from his aunt, the priestess high

At holy {{p|Ise}}, Yamatohime, he took
The sword; and when his foes set flames in grass
To trap him, he cut grass, and so he shook
Them off, and named it grass-cutter; alas,

His son, Chuai, fourteenth, died young; his queen,
Empress Jingū, ruled; and, pregnant, she led
The ships against {{p|Korea}}; and was seen
To bind stones to her waist, so that, instead

Of birth, three years she held the child; and won
The tribute of the kingdoms, and came home,
And bore Ojin, who then became, her son,
Hachiman, god of war. And from the loam

Of {{p|Baekje}}, {{p|Silla}}, {{p|Gaya}}, there came the trade,
The craftsmen, iron, writing, and the folk
Who crossed; and the Yamato court was made,
With mounds of earth, kofun, the great tombs' cloak:

Daisenryō, Nintoku's, longer than
{{p|Great Pyramid}}, a keyhole, earth and stone;
And so the court grew, taking every clan,
Those both from {{p|Baekje}} and {{p|Han}}, into its own.

Two clans, one court. The Soga clan would raise
the Buddha's hall, and both would go to fight;
beside the kami's hall, in elder days,
and the Mononobe kept in hand the rite.

In five-eighty-seven the Soga won,
and court and temple and the shrine were one:
the Buddha and the kami of the sun,
{{x|Shinto}} with {{x|Buddhism}}, a rite begun.

So honor Jimmu, who would bend to bind
The many into one; Jingū, the bold,
Who held her child, and crossed; and, intertwined,
The sun and sea, the kami and the gold.


# Chapter 184: Elagabalus

The {{x|Severan}} house held {{p|Rome}}; in its line,
In the third century, there came the most
Strange of them all, out of the sun's own shrine,
{{p|Emesa}}'s priest, from the {{p|Syrian}} coast.

Elagabalus, aged fourteen, was made
Emperor in two-eighteen; and from birth
The rite he served, from {{p|Emesa}} conveyed
To {{p|Rome}}, the sun his city kept on earth.

He was the high priest by descent of one,
A black stone in a temple, worshipped, shown,
Elagabal, the city's god, the sun;
{{p|Rome}} knew that sky under Jupiter's throne,

With Mars, the {{x|Capitoline}} three. The fight
Was whose priest held it, and whose house would stand;
He brought the stone to {{p|Rome}} itself, its rite
Above the other altars of the land.

He would wed the stone to Athena, then
Astarte; led it in his car,
Through all the streets, and barefoot, before men,
Ran backward, sweating, guiding it, the star.

He wed a {{x|Vestal Virgin}}, breaking through
Her vow; and many wives; and, as a bride,
In woman's dress, a charioteer's; too,
Would be called lady; and he sought, beside,

The surgeons, to be made a woman whole.
These accounts are true at heart, even though^[The accounts of Elagabalus come from Cassius Dio and Herodian, who were hostile to him, and from the later Historia Augusta, which is often unreliable.]
The hostile pens had spun them; and the soul,
The life, shines through it, so that we can know.

The poem notes: whatever was the truth,
The quarrel was not gods but priests: the one
To fall was he who set, though but a youth,
His city's priesthood over {{p|Rome}}'s each one.

The senate's houses, those who held the rites
Of {{p|Rome}}'s own altars, would not bear; eighteen,
The {{x|Praetorian Guard}} slew him, for slights,
Dragged him through streets, and threw him, as unclean,

Into the {{p|Tiber}}, with his mother, laid
The sewer for a tomb. The stone was run
Back home to {{p|Emesa}}, and so conveyed
{{p|Rome}}'s sun to {{p|Rome}}'s own priests: the same rite, one.

But something had been planted: one sun's rite
From {{p|Syria}} had held the realm's own head,
So briefly: one sun, held the throne's own light,
Kept by one {{x|Syrian}} house in {{p|Rome}}'s stead.

And on December's twenty-fifth they kept
Birth of Sol Invictus, Unconquered Sun,
At winter's turn, when out of dark it stepped,
The longest night, the light's new year begun.

The feast of the Unconquered Sun, that night
the longest, was the feast of {{x|Aster Day}},
the same, kept under every name of light;
and {{x|Christmas}}, after, kept it the same way.

His name was scratched from stone, his image marred,
{{x|Damnatio memoriae}}; and yet
He's not forgot, the priest the city barred,
The strangest of its kings, the sun's own debt.

So honor him, the youth who brought the stone,
Who would be lady, priest, and Caesar too;
Who set his city's sun on {{p|Rome}}'s own throne,
And fell, for whose the rite was, and not who.


# Chapter 185: Romance of the Three Kingdoms

The realm, long split, must join; long joined, must part;
So it has always been. And in a grove
Of peaches, three swore brotherhood of heart:
Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei, who all strove

To die upon one day, and raise the {{x|Han}}
Again, and share each burden. Of the line
Of {{x|Han}}, Liu Bei sold sandals, mats, a man
Who lived by straw; and Guan Yu, of the sign

Of justice, fled, for he had slain a knave
Who bullied all; Zhang Fei, who sold the wine
And butchered meat, was loyal, brash, and brave;
The three raised troops to fight the rebels' line.

Zhuge Liang, the Sleeping Dragon, the sage,
Was sought three times in his thatched hut before
He came to serve Liu Bei, and set the stage:
Three kingdoms like a tripod, and the war

Of {{p|Shu}} joined with {{p|Wu}} against {{p|Wei}}, the two
Against the strong. At Red Cliffs, two-oh-eight,
Cao Cao came south, his hundred thousands, through
The river, to crush both; and in the great

Fog Zhuge Liang sent boats of straw, and drew
Cao Cao's own arrows, by the thousand, there;
And then he called the east wind, and it blew;
And fire-ships slipped into the chained fleet's lair,

And all of it was burned; and Cao Cao fled,
By {{p|Huarong Road}}, through marsh and mud; and there
Guan Yu met him at the pass, and, instead
Of slaying him, let him go, for old care

Cao Cao had shown him once; and so he paid
His debt of honor, even to a foe.
Guan Yu is Guandi now, a god, whose aid
Of loyalty, of brothers, trade, they know

In temples through all {{p|China}}, and beyond.
And Zhuge Liang went out six times to {{p|Wei}},
And died at {{p|Wuzhang}}, saying: heaven's bond
Will not let me prevail; and in that day,

{{p|Shu}} failed; it fell to {{p|Wei}}, two-sixty-three;
And {{p|Wei}} gave way to Jin, two-sixty-five;
And {{p|Wu}} fell, two-eighty; and so, one, free,
The realm was joined a while, and kept alive.

And still each child of {{p|China}} knows the names:
Liu Bei, the rightful heir; Cao Cao, the shrewd;
Zhuge Liang, the wise; and plays and games
Retell them, and the generals' books include

Their sayings: hard to win the world, but more
To keep; and win and loss are war's own way;
But he who will not yield, though struck and sore,
Is not cast down; be loyal; and so stay

With right, though right should lose; for Liu Bei's cause
Was just, and lost; and Cao Cao's winning, stained.
So honor them: the oath, the peach, the laws
Of brotherhood, and loyalty, maintained.


# Chapter 186: The Sassanid Empire

In two-twenty-four, the Parthians fell
To Ardashir, who claimed the line of one,
Sasan, a priest; and he would found, as well,
The Sassanid house, bring back what was done

In {{x|Achaemenid}} days; he made the faith
Of Zoroastrians the whole realm's own rite,
With Kartir the high priest; and like a wraith
Of old, at {{p|Ctesiphon}}, by {{p|Tigris}}' light,

Near {{p|Babylon}}, they ruled; four hundred years
They warred with {{p|Rome}}. Shapur took, in war,
Valerian; and three emperors' spears
He broke, and carved it on the cliffs, before

The {{x|Achaemenid}} tombs, where kings are laid.
And Mani rose in Shapur's court, and taught
The light, the dark, the {{x|Buddhist}}, {{x|Christian}}, made
With {{x|Gnostic}} thought, one: Manichaeism brought.

Mani named every prophet in a chain:
Zarathustra, the Buddha, Jesus, each
one light sent down to every age again;
a seal of prophets {{x|Islam}} came to teach.

It spread to {{p|Central Asia}}, {{p|China}}, west
To {{p|Rome}}, where Augustine held it nine years.
Shapur let Mani preach; then, at behest
Of Bahram, he was chained, and died in tears;

And Kartir struck at every rival flame:
The Manichaeans, Jews, Christians, all
Were struck, as rivals for the one rite's claim,
And held as one by him, for one great fall.

But what he taught no prison chain could hold:
it ran to {{p|Harran}} and the {{p|Uyghur}} east,
and {{x|Islam}}'s prophets, later, kept its mold,
and Kartir's Avesta served the crown's priest.

Khosrow I brought the golden age: he made
The taxes new, and dug canals, and took
The {{x|Greek}} philosophers who fled, afraid,
When Justinian had shut {{p|Athens}}' book;

He had the books of {{p|India}}, of {{p|Greece}},
Of {{x|Syriac}}, made {{p|Persian}}; and the chess
Came from the {{p|Indus}} to his court, a piece
Of play made finer; and, with nothing less,

The {{x|Pancatantra}}'s fables came, and went
As {{x|Kalila wa Dimna}} to the west,
The kin of Aesop's, through his court they sent,
And travelled all the world, and found their rest.

Khosrow II then fought the last war with {{p|Rome}}
Of the east, took {{p|Jerusalem}}, and bore
The {{x|True Cross}} off; and {{p|Byzantium}}'s home
Was nearly lost; Heraclius, in war,

Struck back into the heart of {{p|Persia}}, won
The {{x|Cross}} again; and both of them were spent;
And then the {{x|Arabs}} came, and it was done:
Yazdegerd III, last shah, fled, and went

Across the land; at {{p|Merv}} a miller slew
Him; and the temples' flames went out; and yet
The tongue, the art, the books, came living through,
And shaped the realm that after came, and set

Its heart; and those who kept the flame, who fled
To {{p|India}}, the Parsis, keep it still.
So honor {{p|Persia}}: Khosrow's books well read,
The chess, the fables, and the old flame's will.


# Chapter 187: The Third Century Crisis

Elagabalus slain, for fifty years
The realm would stumble; fifty men were made
As emperor, and slain; and plague, and fears
Of barbarians, and the coin's decayed

Thin silver; legions hailed their generals, then
The next ones slew them: young Severus, slain;
Maximinus Thrax; the Gordian men,
The first, the second, dead; and slain again,

Pupienus, Balbinus: the names blend,
The reigns came brief as weather, and were gone;
And so it went, from one to the next's end,
As each one rose, and fell, and so on, on.

Then in two-fifty Decius decreed
that every house burn incense at his shrine
and keep a paper proving it agreed.
The church said: the same incense, but at mine.

And many Christians burned it, and were lapsed,
And argued over long; and some were slain,
Cyprian, {{p|Carthage}}'s bishop; collapsed,
East: Shapur took Valerian in chain,

The {{p|Roman}} emperor, a footstool for
His horse, and stuffed his skin with straw, and hung
It in the temple, for the envoys, for
The first {{p|Roman}} emperor's skin so strung.

Zenobia, {{p|Palmyra}}'s, broke away,
And ruled from {{p|Egypt}} up to {{p|Asia}}'s east,
A warrior queen; and in the west, the sway
Of Postumus, in {{p|Gaul}}; and so, at least,

The realm was three: {{p|Gaul}}, {{p|Rome}}, {{p|Palmyra}}; then
Aurelian, Restitutor Orbis, called
Restorer of the World, and joined again
The three, and took Zenobia, enthralled

In golden chains, to {{p|Rome}}; and took back {{p|Gaul}};
And made Sol Invictus the state's own god,
The Unconquered Sun, the one above all,
Not by the side, but on the throne's own sod.

This is the crucial step. The sun he crowned
at midwinter was Christ's own cousin, born
the very night that {{x|Aster Day}} comes round,
the longest dark, that turns toward the morn.

The temples stood; the priests still served; but one
The emperor held above the rest; slain
Was Aurelian as well; but what he'd done
Held; Diocletian came, and made again

The realm: {{x|Tetrarchy}}, four, two {{x|Augusti}},
Two {{x|Caesars}}, in four quarters; and their seats,
{{p|Milan}}, {{p|Trier}}, the east; and {{p|Rome}}'s duty
Was small; the rule was where the border meets.

In three-oh-three Diocletian tore down
the churches, burned the books, and named it law:
the {{x|Great Persecution}}, so that the crown
would hold each altar the empire saw.

It failed; the church was too deep in the land;
Martyrs made more; then Galerius said,
Dying, in three-eleven: with hard hand
We tried to crush them, failed; let them, instead,

Keep praying. And the church had grown to one
Man in each ten; with bishops, creeds, the word
Of Paul gone out, the gospels gathered, done;
And all was ready for what then occurred.

At {{p|Milvian Bridge}}, in three-twelve, there came
To Constantine a sign: in this, prevail,
The chi-rho; and he won, in Christ's own name;
And so the realm began to turn its sail.


# Chapter 188: Armenia

{{p|Armenia}}, the high land of the hills,
Between the {{p|Roman}} and the {{x|Persian}} hand,
By {{p|Ararat}} and {{p|Caucasus}}, still fills
Its place; for Hayk, the father of the land,

From Noah, through Japheth's son, came down,
And beat {{p|Babylon}}'s tyrant, Bel, in war,
And founded them; {{x|Hay}} is their own name's crown,
{{p|Hayastan}} their land; and from Aram, for

Of Hayk's line, the others name the land.
In three-oh-one, King Tiridates had thrown
The Illuminator, by his own hand,
Into a pit for years; a widow, known

For mercy, fed him; and he lived, and won
The king; and so the king, Tiridates, made
The faith of Christ the realm's own rite, the one
First, twelve years before {{p|Milan}}'s word was laid;

Thaddeus and Bartholomew had brought
It first, died for it; Mesrop Mashtots made
The letters, thirty-six, for their tongue, wrought,
And in their tongue the scriptures there were laid.

And then a golden age of letters, books
Of history, of the faith, of thought; and one,
Movses Khorenatsi drew, as one looks,
The {{x|Greek}}, the {{x|Persian}}, {{x|Jewish}}, into one,

And traced the {{x|Haykazuni}} line. And caught
Between the {{x|Sassanid}} and {{x|Byzantine}},
Their land was cut and split; and so they fought
To keep their faith, their own, and their own line.

And at {{p|Avarayr}}, in four-fifty-one,
Vardan Mamikonian led his own
against the {{x|Sassanid}} king's priests, who'd won
the order that their altar stand alone.

Vardan fell. {{p|Armenia}} keeps the day

The way went on, from teacher's hands to hands.

Al-Hallaj cried {{x|Ana al-Haqq}}; and who
Could speak for God? The jurists, and the court
Of {{p|Baghdad}}, held the word; nine-twenty-two:
That was the fight; and so they cut him short.

Umayyads who lived went on in {{p|Spain}},
And Córdoba was theirs, a caliph's seat,
Half a million, books, baths, a lighted lane,
All paved, when {{p|Paris}} was a muddy street.

Nine-oh-nine: the {{x|Shia}} Fatimids rose
In {{p|Egypt}}, rival to Abbasid reign,
And built {{p|Cairo}} and al-Azhar; and those
In {{p|Mazandaran}}, by {{p|Caspian}}'s main,

The Bavandids, held {{p|Tabaristan}}'s height,
Woods; kin to Sasanian kings, the last
Old house of {{p|Iran}} not yet won by fight;
Qarin the First, eight-seventeen, held fast;

When Mazyar rose against the caliph's hand,
Qarin stood with {{p|Baghdad}}; eight-forty-two,
He took {{x|Islam}} himself, and kept his land
Of mountains; so the old kings' blood went through

Into new faith: Abul Melik, his son;
And in eight-sixty-nine, a grandson came,
Abul Hamza, in {{p|Armenia}}, one
Of the Artsruni; west, under their name.

The Seljuk Turks came; caliphs kept the name;
Sultans ruled; at Manzikert they beat,
Ten-seventy-one, Byzantines, and came
To {{p|Anatolia}}; and in each street,

{{x|Hanafi}}, they built their schools; learning went
On, till twelve-fifty-eight, the Mongols came,
Took {{p|Baghdad}}, and the Abbasids were spent;
The books thrown in the {{p|Tigris}}; it became

Black with their ink. So honor that bright age,
Four centuries: the numbers, and the cure,
The optics, and the thought, the {{x|Sufi}} page,
Through {{p|Córdoba}}, {{p|Toledo}}, and kept sure.


# Chapter 195: Charlemagne and Byzantium

And in the {{p|West}}, while the usurper's hand
Kept the throne empty, the Franks grew, and grew;
Clovis I took up, in the {{x|Nicene}} band,
The faith; the Goths kept {{x|Arian}}, as true.^[Clovis I of the Franks was baptized a Nicene Christian, a Catholic, around the year 500, unlike the Goths, the Vandals and the Burgundians, who were Arians.]

His heirs, the Merovingians, would then reign
Three hundred years, but weak, and ruled by none;
The Mayors of the Palace were the brain;
And Charles Martel, the Hammer, Mayor, won

At Tours, seven-thirty-two, stopped the march
Of {{x|Umayyads}}, who fell back to {{p|Spain}};
Pepin the Short then asked the Pope: the arch
Of kingship: should the one who ruled now reign?

The Pope said yes; and Pepin took the crown;
Carolingians began; and his son
Was Charlemagne, Charles the Great, whose renown
Would build what {{p|Europe}} had not seen, as one.

Charlemagne reigned forty-six years. He fought
the Saxons for their grove, and in it felled
the Irminsul, and set the priests he brought
at their own altars, where their feasts were held.

Seven-seventy-four: he took the land
of {{p|Italy}} from Lombards; and in {{p|Spain}}
he pressed the Muslims back with his own hand
and held the frontier as his own domain.

He did not break the Lombards; took the crown,
Rex Langobardorum;^[Charlemagne conquered the Lombard kingdom in 774 and took the title Rex Langobardorum. Its crown is the one later called the Iron Crown of Lombardy.] and its crown, too,
Held it for twenty-six years, in renown,
The realm of Theodoric's heirs, come through.

He brought from {{p|York}} Alcuin, and there, to lead
His school; old learning lived again and rose;
The script was made that we today can read,
And monks would copy old books, and keep those

That would be lost. On {{x|Christmas}}, on the day,
Eight hundred, Leo III crowned his head
Imperator Romanorum,^[Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne in St Peter's on Christmas Day 800. The papacy held the imperial throne to be vacant because a woman, Irene, ruled in Constantinople.] to stay,
In {{p|St. Peter's}}; and the throne, so long dead

Since Theodoric, filled. The Pope would place
The crown; the claim was not the Pope's: he held
{{p|Rome}}, {{p|Italy}}, {{p|Gaul}}, {{p|Germany}}, by grace
Of conquest and of law; and he excelled,

Lombards' heir, who were Theodoric's own;
And this enraged {{p|Constantinople}}, where
Irene ruled, claimed the {{p|Roman}} throne alone;
The Byzantines would not let a Frank share

The crown: for since Justinian, they'd kept
The {{p|West}} by the usurper's right, no right,
And kept the throne empty, where none had stepped;
No Frank would have the crown they'd seized by might.

{{p|Byzantium}} itself was shaken: then
Iconoclasm rose, seven-twenties,
When Leo III, emperor, bade men
Break holy icons, for all their plenties.

The fight was over who would hold the face:
the throne, that broke the icons in its name,
or monks, who kept them in the holy place
as windows, for a century of flame.

{{p|Nicaea}}, seven-eighty-seven, said
The {{x|Second Council}}: honored, not adored;
It came back, till eight-forty-three, then fled;
And all the icons came back, were restored;

{{x|Triumph of Orthodoxy}}'s feast is kept
In Lent. Cyril, Methodius, then went
To the Slavs, and made letters where they stepped,
The Glagolitic, made with this intent:

That they would pray in their own tongue; and so
{{p|Rus}}, {{p|Moravia}}, {{p|Bulgaria}}'s land,
Prayed not in {{x|Latin}}, {{x|Greek}}; and so would go
The faith in each one's tongue, as they had planned.

Charlemagne died; his grandsons split the whole
At Verdun, eight-forty-three: West and East,
{{p|Middle Francia}}, which broke apart; the toll
Of Vikings, seven-ninety-three, released

At {{p|Lindisfarne}}, slew monks, and from the sea,
From {{p|Scandinavia}}, three hundred years,
From {{p|Ireland}} to {{p|Russia}}, they would be
Raiders, then settlers, kings, and turned, by tears.

So honor Charlemagne: the empty throne
Was filled again, eight hundred, at the feast;
The Lombards' heir, Theodoric's, his own;
The monks' icons, windows, kept in the {{p|East}}.


# Chapter 196: Scandinavia and the Eddas

In the cold north, {{p|Scandinavia}}, where
The winter sun would barely rise, the Norse
Kept the old steppe-born gods, and kept them there
The longest, in the old rite's living force.

The Europeans kept the bishops' rite;
the Norse kept the old altars of the North,
and when they took the bishops in, they'd write
the old gods down in books, and bring them forth.

In {{p|Iceland}}, Snorri Sturluson set down
The Prose Edda, and the Poetic old,
The myths; for, though of Christ, he loved the crown
Of the old tales, and wrote them, to be told.

Odin, the Allfather, hung on the tree
{{p|Yggdrasil}} for nine nights, and won the runes;
Thor, with {{x|Mjölnir}}, thunder; and, as free,
Freyr and Freyja, gods of the fields and moons;

And Loki, trickster, bore the monsters: one,
Fenrir the wolf, and Jörmungandr, the snake
That rings the world; Hel, of the dead; and done
By him, Baldr died, gentle, for his sake.

Nine realms upon {{p|Yggdrasil}}: {{p|Asgard}}'s height,
The Aesir's; {{p|Vanaheim}}, the Vanir's; men
In {{p|Midgard}}; giants, {{p|Jotunheim}}; and, bright,
{{p|Alfheim}}, the elves'; the dwarves, in deep, and then

{{p|Niflheim}}'s ice, {{p|Muspelheim}}'s flame; and, below,
{{p|Hel}}, for the dead not fallen; and who fell,
Valkyries bore to {{p|Valhalla}}; and so
At Ragnarök, just as the old songs tell,

Fenrir will swallow Odin; Thor will slay
The snake, and die of venom; Surtr's flame
Will burn the world; and then, a newer day,
Two hidden in the tree come out, the same.

Seven-ninety-three, when {{p|Lindisfarne}} burned,
The Vikings raided, traded, settled; and,
Founding {{p|Dublin}}, {{p|Cork}}, {{p|Waterford}}; and turned
To {{p|England}}, and the {{p|Danelaw}} was their land;

The Varangians served the emperor's throne;
They settled {{p|Iceland}}, eight-seventy, came,
Nine-eighty-six, to {{p|Greenland}}, Erik's own;
{{p|Vinland}}, a thousand, Leif; and, all the same,

Went up the rivers east to trade, and made,
The Rus, the {{p|Kievan Rus}}; and Rurik came
To {{p|Novgorod}}, his kin {{p|Kiev}}; and stayed
Vladimir took, nine-eighty-eight, the name

Of Christ, from {{p|Constantinople}}, they found,
His envoys, such a beauty in the rite,
They knew not if on earth or heaven's ground
They stood; and so the {{p|Rus}} took up that light.

In {{p|Scandinavia}} kings took the rite:
first Harald Bluetooth in nine-sixty-five;
Olaf Tryggvason took {{p|Norway}}'s by might;
Olaf II fell, a saint who would survive.

Sweden came last; the twelfth century saw
{{p|Uppsala}}'s temple, with offered dead,
Come down, as crown and church took up its law;
The altar took new priests there, in its stead.

In {{p|Iceland}} it was settled without war:
the {{x|Althing}}, in the year one thousand, said
the bishops' rite in public; and the door
of each home kept the old rite there instead.

The sagas would be written, of that day:
Of Egil, Njál, Laxdæla; and in {{p|France}},
Rollo's {{p|Normandy}}, nine-eleven, pay;
And William, of his line, would take the chance,

Ten-sixty-six, at Hastings, and would win
{{p|England}}. And so the Vikings ceased, and took
The rite of Christ; but they had shaped, within,
All {{p|Europe}}'s map: so honor them, their book.
