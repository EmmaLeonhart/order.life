# Chapter 141: P at Baikal — Paikal

In the deep {{p|Siberian}} winter came
Haplogroup P, unto {{p|Lake Baikal}}'s shore,
The deepest lake on earth, and of the same
Old crack the earth had opened long before.

Twenty-five million years the rift grew wide,
A mile and more in depth, and filled with rain;
A fifth of all earth's water fresh inside
That single crack, beneath the taiga's reign.

There swim the omul and the nerpa seal,
The only seal that lives where waters freeze;
The {{p|Evenki}} and the {{p|Buryat}} kneel
And beat the shaman's drum beside its seas.

And here came P, the father of the two,
Of Q and R, called Paikal by the lake,
Whose fathers, since the strait, had wandered through
{{p|Eurasia}}'s length; and here they paused, to take

Their breath beside the ice, and ask: which way?
Two answers came. The first was east, to walk
The dry land of {{p|Beringia}}, which lay
Dry then, where now the strait's cold waters talk.

And those who walked it east were Q, and they
Crossed to the {{p|Americas}}, and then spread
From the {{p|Aleutians}} all the long, long way
Down to {{p|Tierra del Fuego}}, far ahead.

The {{p|Inuit}}, and the nations of the south,
The peoples of that whole new world, are Q's,
And carry him in blood, and in the mouth
Of all their elders telling the old news.

The second answer was the west: to turn
And run back on the road the fathers came,
Through {{p|Central Asia}}, to the grass, to learn
{{p|Pontic-Caspian}} steppe; and R his name.

R is the boomerang, the line that went
As far as it could east, and then came back;
And all of {{p|Europe}}, {{p|India}}, are sent
Of R, his sons, who walk along his track.

And near {{p|Irkutsk}}, at {{p|Mal'ta}}, lay a child,
Four years of age, of R's house, near its root,
Still at {{p|Baikal}}; and in his blood were filed
Both roads at once, the seed and the far fruit:

The {{p|Navajo}} and {{p|Cherokee}} are kin
To him, the {{p|Inca}} too, and in the west
{{p|Lithuanian}} and the {{p|Swede}}: within
That buried child both roads already rest.

The lake is there today; the taiga lays
Its snow upon the shore; the drums are played
Beside the water; through the ice, always,
The nerpa rise to breathe in ice's shade.

So honor P, who stood upon the ice
And watched his sons go into dusk, the two:
Q to the east, R west; and both suffice
To people half the world, as now they do.

Paikal, the deepest water, and the stone
Of the crossroads, where one line went both ways;
The stars, the ice, the silence, and alone
The father, in {{p|Siberia}}'s long days.


# Chapter 142: Manu and Yemo — R and the Yamnaya

And R came back. From {{p|Lake Baikal}}'s cold shore
He turned and walked the long road to the west,
Across the taiga, and the {{p|Urals}}, for
The sea of grass, where he would come to rest.

From the {{p|Carpathians}} to the {{p|Altai}}, wide,
The {{p|Pontic-Caspian steppe}}; there he stayed,
And there his sons were born, and at his side
His two sons grew, the house that R had made.

His sons were Manu and Yemo, the twins,
The houses R1a and R1b;
And here the story of the world begins,
When Manu offered Yemo up; and he

Was slain, the first of all; and then the whole
Of the world's order rose from Yemo's frame:
His head became the sky, the sun's bright bowl
His eyes, and moon; his bones, the mountains came;

His blood, the rivers; and from him the kind
Of every man: the priest came from his head,
The fighter from his chest, and, so designed,
The farmer from his loins, the land to tread,

The servant from his feet; the cattle, too,
Came from his flesh; and so the world was made
From that first sacrifice, and it holds true:
The order of the world from Yemo laid.

The north calls Yemo Ymir, and it calls
Manu Mannus; {{p|India}}'s Yama died
The first of men, and rules the dead's dark halls,
And Manu there outlived the flood's great tide.

And on the steppe they tamed the horse; and then
A man on horseback crossed in one bright day
What walking took a week; the herds, the men,
The wars, now moved with speed none could gainsay.

And so the {{p|Yamnaya}} rose, riders, those
Who herded cattle on the grass, and raised
The {{p|kurgan}} mounds above their dead in rows
On the {{p|Pontic-Caspian}} lands they grazed.

And out they rode, in every way they went:
West to {{p|Europe}}, R1b to the west,
R1a to the east, as they were sent,
Brought {{p|Corded Ware}} and {{p|Beaker}}, and the rest.

And so the men of {{p|Europe}} stand in three:
The house of I below, of G between,
Of R above; {{p|Sardinia}}, by sea,
Keeps G, and in the north the I is seen.

South, through {{p|Central Asia}}, R1a rode
To {{p|India}} with chariot and song,
And with the house of H and L abode,
And they were one, the {{p|Vedas}}' holy throng.

There it became {{p|Sanskrit}}, and in {{p|Iran}}
{{p|Avestan}}; and {{p|Tocharians}}, far east,
In {{p|Tarim}}'s sands, the farthest of the clan,
Left red-haired dead, and kept the steppe's old feast.

And half of all who live, today, still speak
The tongue of R, from {{p|Ireland}} to {{p|Nepal}}:
The {{p|Romance}} tongues, {{p|Germanic}}, {{p|Slavic}}, {{p|Greek}},
{{p|Armenian}} and {{p|Celtic}}, Persian, all.

To {{p|Cucuteni}}'s painted towns there came
The riders; and the farmers' daughters wed
The sons of R, and in one house, one name,
The steppe and the old {{p|Europe}} both were bred.

So honor R, the boomerang, who came
Back from the east, and tamed the horse, and rode;
And honor Manu, Yemo, and their name,
The twins, the sacrifice, the world's first code.


# Chapter 143: E — Returns to Africa

And E came back. Haplogroup E had crossed
The {{p|Bab al-Mandab}} once with Noah's band,
And wandered {{p|Eurasia}}, but was not lost:
He turned, and walked back to his fathers' land.

His brothers, D and C and F, went on,
To {{p|Japan}}, to {{p|Tibet}}, the far-off lands,
To {{p|Australia}}, the steppe; but he was gone
Back south, alone of all the outbound bands.

North from {{p|Yemen}}, to the {{p|Levant}} he came,
Then south again through {{p|Sinai}}, past the sea
At {{p|Suez}}, into {{p|Egypt}}, and the same
The green {{p|Nile Valley}}, where his sons would be.

And then across the whole {{p|Sahara}}, green
In those days, lakes and rivers, grass and game,
Where sand lies now; and all the lands between
He filled, from west to east, and gave his name.

{{p|North Africa}}, the west, the east, he filled,
And took the river valleys and the plain,
The grass, and all the lands, as he had willed;
And there his house grew great, and there would reign.

He did not find the land an empty place:
The houses that had never left were there,
The A and B, the San and Mbuti race,
In {{p|Kalahari}} sands and forest air.

They kept the desert and the forest's deep,
And E the grass; and of the men today
In {{p|Africa}}, the most are his, to keep
His house, from north to south, in every way.

His {{p|Bantu}} sons^[The house E-V38, also called E1b1a.] rose where the two lands meet,
{{p|Cameroon}} and {{p|Nigeria}}, and spread
Their tongue to east and south, on many feet,
Till half the continent spoke what they said.

{{p|Swahili}}, {{p|Zulu}}, {{p|Kikuyu}}, the speech
{{p|Kinyarwanda}}: all are of one tongue,
First {{x|Proto-Bantu}}, spoken in the reach
Of that small homeland when the house was young.

And in the north his other houses dwell:^[E-M81 among the Berbers; E-M78 in Egypt and the Horn.]
The {{p|Berbers}} of {{p|Morocco}} and the sand
Of {{p|Libya}}, {{p|Algeria}} as well,
{{p|Tunisia}}; the {{p|Egyptians}} of the land

By the {{p|Nile}}; the {{p|Ethiopians}}, too,
{{p|Eritreans}}, {{p|Somalis}} of the {{p|Horn}};
And E and J, the houses old and true,
Share {{p|Afroasiatic}}, sisters born.

His sons were the {{p|Egyptians}}, who of old
Raised up the pyramids, and to the sun
Gave disks, and gods with heads of beasts, and told
The soul's long journey when its life was done.

And far across the sea, in lands today
Of the {{p|Americas}}, his sons are found,
And there, too, E has made his house and way,
And stands upon that far and foreign ground.

So honor E, who walked away and came
Back home, the one whose road went out and in:
The door swung both ways; and the house's name
Is {{p|Africa}}'s, where all of us begin.


# Chapter 144: Malta — The Oldest Temples

And now the builders of the temples rise,
The first of them, a people without name;
They kept no writing, but beneath their skies
They raised their gods' first houses all the same.

On a small dry isle in the middle sea,
{{p|Malta}}, some few thousand farmers at most
Raised temples out of limestone, standing free,
The oldest on the earth, before the host

Of {{p|Egypt}} piled its pyramids, before
The ziggurats of {{p|Sumer}}, and ahead
Of {{p|Stonehenge}}: they began, in days of yore,
Thirty-six hundred B.C.E., and spread

For {{p|Ġgantija}} and {{p|Ħaġar Qim}}, and for
{{p|Mnajdra}} and {{p|Tarxien}}, out of bedrock's hold
They cut out blocks of fifty tons, and bore
Them with no wheel or metal, strong and bold.

They left no name for themselves, or for one
Of any gods they served; and what they keep
Is stone and sherd and bone, and, one by one,
The little figures, seated or asleep:

The {{p|Fat Lady}}, broad of hip and of thigh,
In clay and limestone, singular in art
In {{p|Europe}}'s {{p|Neolithic}}, the reply,
{{p|Malta}}'s idiom, to an older heart:

The mother Marija Gimbutas found
Beneath Old {{p|Europe}}, ere the horsemen came;
{{p|Venus of Willendorf}} on other ground;
{{p|Cucuteni-Trypillia}}'s clay: the same

Great mother, and the same unbroken line.
But nowhere else did that devotion raise
Such stone. Look at the temples' own design,
The shape they kept through all their building days:

Paired lobes along a central passageway,
A kidney or a clover in the plan,
The entrance on the long axis; they lay
Before each room the uprights lintels span:

The trilithon, a thousand years ahead
Of {{p|Stonehenge}}. The carved spirals on the walls,
The plant, the beast, the pillar-pairs that lead
As gateways of the world; and in the halls,

Figurines, ash of the ritual fire,
The bones of offered beasts beneath the floor.
A thousand years the isle fed the choir
Of builders. Then the builders were no more.

Then, near twenty-five hundred B.C.E.,
The temples stood, and none would tend them more;
No war, no burning, and no enemy;
So ended temple-building, and its lore.

The {{x|Bronze Age}} settlers who came later on
Kept no remembrance of the builders' name;
The isle, ringed by the sea, was spent and gone:
Its soil worn thin, its forests felled for flame

And moving stone, until the land that fed
Some thousands of the builders could not feed
Them more; not with a bang, they starved or fled,
Or thinned away, in slow decline and need.

The temples stood upon the windy rise
Four thousand years, empty, without a stir,
And stand there still, beneath the island skies,
And are, today, the very thing they were.

So honor them, the first ones in the line
After {{p|Göbekli Tepe}}, where hunters came
To gather at the stones; here, at the shrine,
The settled farmer laid his harvest's claim.

Foraging gave way to the field; the feast
Gave way to the god's standing house of stone;
Here, for the first time known, a people ceased
To keep back anything; their surplus, grown

Each year, went to the temples, till the land
Gave out. No palace and no city wall,
No fortress: only temples, by each hand.
They built for their gods, and that was the all.

The temple, then, is elder than the town,
As at {{p|Göbekli Tepe}} it had been shown,
Greater for thousands of years; none laid down
A purer bond of faith to work in stone.

In one narrow sense, they were most devout
Of any people in the human line;
And so the warning: if the work runs out
Ahead of field and harvest, then the shrine

Devours the hands that raise it. The wrong
Is not the temple; building can outrun
The ground beneath it; and the isle, ere long,
Was stripped, its trees and fields and harvest done.

Small island, fervent faith, the building spree,
And then the crash, and then the island's fall:
{{p|Malta}} was first, a warning, as we see,
That few who came thereafter heard at all.

Was this a tragedy, or was it great?
Both, without laughter and without a blame;
And each of them, the two, has equal weight;
And so we tell them both, and both the same:

The temples splendid, and the builders dead;
Their gods remembered now by none who live;
The sacred turned to ruin; in its stead
The ruin turned memorial. They give

Us all we have: the stones upon the hill,
The spirals and the lobes, the wind that blows
Along the empty corridors; and still,
In {{p|Valletta}}, glassed, the {{p|Fat Lady}}'s pose.

Honor them: builders who built past their store;
The priests who led the work; the women's hands
That shaped the mother out of clay; the corps
Of farmers whose surplus fed stone, till lands

Ran out: the whole people that set the shrine
Above itself, and perished by the choice.
{{p|Malta}}: first temple people in the line
Since {{p|Göbekli Tepe}}; the first warning voice.

The first loss: in the human age, the first
Civilization whose remembered name
Is gone, its people scattered and dispersed,
With none of all who live its stones to claim.


# Chapter 145: The Old Kingdom — Pyramids at Giza

While {{p|Malta}} spent itself upon its isle,
A greater people on a greater stream
Began to build, along the {{p|Nile}}'s long mile,
At such a scale as none had dared to dream.

The sons of E had dwelt beside the {{p|Nile}}
Long ages: {{p|Badari}}, {{p|Naqada}}'s clay,
Their pots, their copper, and the signs whose style
Grew into hieroglyphs; and on their way

They joined the {{p|Two Lands}} under one, the thin
Long valley, and the {{p|Delta}}'s spreading fan,
Thirty-one hundred B.C.E.; and in
That union, Menes' kingship then began.

And Menes is Narmer, shown on the stone
Of his great palette, first of them to reign;
And then the scribes, the god-king on his throne,
The tax, the grain, the order of the plain.

Then Djoser at {{p|Saqqara}} built the first
Great house of stone, the {{p|Step Pyramid}}, made
By Imhotep, the builder, priest, well-versed
In healing, who was honored and obeyed.

A tomb laid flat, then raised in stages high,
Two hundred feet, and fifteen acres wide,
The first great work of stone in years gone by,
The sign of all to come, the builders' pride.

And then Sneferu raised three: at {{p|Meidum}} one
That fell in part; {{p|Bent Pyramid}}, whose slope
Was changed midway; the {{p|Red Pyramid}}, done
Smooth-sided, true, the first fulfilled great hope.

And then his son, Khufu, at {{p|Giza}} raised
{{p|Great Pyramid}}, four hundred eighty feet,
Two million stones and more, his name thus praised,
The tallest work of human hands complete.

Its faces true to north and east and west,
The King's and Queen's rooms, the Gallery's height,
The shafts that point to stars; and, at the crest,
The soul of the dead king would take its flight

Up through the stone, to join the northern stars
That never set, a star himself on high;
The pyramid his road, and nothing bars
The king, once dead, from climbing to the sky.

His son, Khafre, built the second, and set
On higher ground, so both seem equal-tall,
And carved the {{p|Sphinx}}, the lion-man, that yet
Looks to the rising sun, and guards them all.

Menkaure built the third and smallest; three
Khufu, Khafre, Menkaure, there they stand,
The most known works of all humanity,
Still standing, after all, upon the sand.

The pharaoh is a god, Horus in flesh;
And, dying, he becomes Osiris, lord
Of the dead, judge of souls; his son, afresh,
Becomes Horus; and so the throne's restored.

And so the pyramid is not a tomb
Alone, but cosmic order's very frame:
Built right, the king will rise and stars will bloom;
Built wrong, the world itself would fall to shame.

For twenty years his thousands worked the stone,
Not slaves, but fed and paid and healed, and sent
From every village, each crew named and known,
And then went home again, their service spent.

"Khufu's Drunkards," "Khufu's Friends," so they wrote
Their names upon the blocks, with jest and pride;
The quarries, copper mines, the grain, the boat,
The tax, the scribes, the first state far and wide.

But after Menkaure the work grew less,
The later pyramids grew small and low;
The nomarchs rose; the king's strength, in distress,
Grew weak; and drought came, and the flood's great flow

Failed, as it never had; and famine then
Came; the {{p|Old Kingdom}} broke, and fell, and lay
In pieces, under rival kings and men;
And {{p|Akkad}}, in that drought, fell the same way.

The pyramids at {{p|Giza}} stand today,
Their faces weathered, all their casing gone;
And still at sunset, gold and orange, they
Glow as they glowed when Khufu's crews looked on.

So honor them: the god-kings and the stone,
The scribes, the crews who built and then went home;
The house of E, who made the {{p|Nile}} his own,
And raised the first great works beneath the dome.


# Chapter 146: The Akkadian Empire

While pyramids were rising at the {{p|Nile}},
Across the {{p|Sinai}} and the desert sand,
Between the rivers, in another style,
The first of empires, ruled by command,

Of Sargon of Akkad. Before his day
The cities stood apart, each with its wall:
{{p|Uruk}}, {{p|Ur}}, {{p|Lagash}}, {{p|Kish}}, and on their way
{{p|Umma}}, {{p|Nippur}}, and {{p|Eridu}}, and all,

Each with its king, its god, its watered field;
And they had made the writing pressed in clay,
The wheel, the bronze, the sixty that would yield
The hours and the angles we use today.

Their kings were lugal, ensi; and they fought
And traded, and one city ruled the rest
A while; and Gilgamesh at {{p|Uruk}} wrought,
And Lugalzagesi held {{p|Sumer}} best.

But none could hold for long. Then Sargon came:
His mother laid him in a basket made
Of reeds, upon the river; with no name,
And a gardener raised him in the shade.

He rose as cupbearer to {{p|Kish}}'s king,
And then was king himself; his name, the true,
The rightful king; and he began to bring
The cities under him, and {{p|Akkad}} too,

And {{p|Elam}} to the east; and to the north
{{p|Syria}}, {{p|Anatolia}}, the shore
Of the great {{p|Upper Sea}}, where he went forth
And washed his weapons, as none had before;

And in the {{p|Lower Sea}} he washed them too,
The {{p|Persian Gulf}}; and none had ruled so wide;
And so the first empire came and grew,
And {{p|Akkad}} rose upon the river's side.

He reigned for fifty-six years; and his tongue,
{{p|Akkadian}}, of J's house, then became
The common speech; {{p|Sumerian}} was sung
Less and less, though it kept its sacred name.

His daughter, Enheduanna, at {{p|Ur}}
Was Nanna's priestess; and she was the first
Whose name we know of all who wrote; and her
Great hymns to Inanna still are rehearsed.

The {{p|Exaltation of Inanna}} sings
The goddess of both love and war, and still
Is read today; the daughter of the kings
Was the first author, writing by her will.

His sons, Rimush, Manishtushu, reigned,
And both met risings, and were slain; and then
His grandson Naram-Sin the summit gained,
And named himself a god among all men,

The first who did it living; and, made known
On his great stele, he climbs up the height,
A god in war, his foes beneath him thrown,
The sun and stars above him, burning bright.

But Naram-Sin laid waste Enlil's own shrine
At {{p|Nippur}}; and the gods, in wrath, called down
{{p|Gutians}} from the {{p|Zagros}} on his line,
And drought besides, on every field and town.

His son Shar-Kali-Sharri fought in vain;
It fell; the {{p|Gutians}}, a mountain band,
Ruled for a hundred years with little gain,
Till {{p|Uruk}}, then {{p|Ur}}, drove them from the land.

Of Sargon came the form of rule: the host
Of standing soldiers; governors per land;
One tongue, one weight, one road from coast to coast,
One tax, the King of the Four Quarters' hand.

And Sargon is remembered still, the king
Who rose from nothing, foundling, to the throne;
His capital, great {{p|Akkad}}, is a thing
Whose place is hidden; but his name is known.

So honor Sargon, first to rule them all,
Enheduanna, first to sign her song,
And Naram-Sin, the god-king, and his fall,
And {{p|Akkad}}, first empire, lost, yet strong.


# Chapter 147: Stonehenge

Far on an isle at {{p|Europe}}'s western shore,
Before a word was written there, there rose
On {{p|Salisbury Plain}}, a stone work, and more:
{{p|Stonehenge}}, the ring of stones in ordered rows.

Three thousand years before the Common Age
It started as a ditch and bank, a round
Of three hundred and thirty feet, a stage
Of wooden posts upon the sacred ground.

The sons of I and G began it, those
Of hunters and of farmers, and they brought
The bluestones from {{p|Preseli}}, where they rose
In {{p|Wales}}, a hundred fifty miles, and wrought

A double circle of them at the heart,
Each two to four tons, eighty stones in all,
On sledge and log and raft, by strength and art,
Across the hills and coast, and raised them tall.

In twenty-five hundred B.C.E. came
The sarsens, twenty-five tons each, from north
Some twenty miles; and thirty, in one frame,
Held up a ring of lintels, joined henceforth;

And five great trilithons, a horseshoe, stood
Inside the ring; and tenon, mortise, cut
In stone, as carpenters would work in wood;
The lintels curved, the perfect circle shut.

And all of it was set to face the sun:
Midsummer's dawn above the {{p|Heel Stone}} rose,
Midwinter's sun set, when the year was done,
Between the greatest trilithon, to close.

And in that time the {{p|Beaker}} people came,
Of R1b, who brought the steppe's own speech;
They took the isle, and kept the work the same,
And added to it, and enlarged its reach.

The bluestones, set in oval, they arranged
In the horseshoe, two thousand B.C.E.;
The people of the isle were new and changed,
And yet the work went on, as all could see.

Then, fifteen hundred B.C.E., the last
Of building ceased, and it was left complete,
The ditches silting, and some great stones cast
Down by the years, beneath the wind and sleet.

It was the center of a sacred land
Of mounds and henges, of the dead, the sky,
The ancestors, the year; from every hand
Of all the isle and over sea they'd lie

In death beside the stones, their ashes laid
Within its ring; the place that all agreed,
Above all others, where the work be made,
The axis of their world, and of their creed.

The temple first: the sacred place made one
The scattered people round it; and the new,
The {{p|Beaker}} folk, kept on the work begun,
And knew it holy, and they kept it too.

It stands on {{p|Salisbury Plain}}; and the sun
Of winter sets between the uprights still;
At solstice, crowds still gather, everyone,
To watch the sun rise there above the hill.

So honor them: the ones who moved the stone
From {{p|Wales}}, who raised the sarsens, and the new
Who kept the work, and those whose ashes, sown,
Lie in the ring; and all the pilgrims, too.

{{p|Stonehenge}}: the ring of stones, the solstice sign,
The calendar that still tells time today,
The work that outlived builders, tongue, and line,
And stands upon the plain at close of day.


# Chapter 148: Minoan Crete

While {{p|Stonehenge}} weathered on its windy plain,
And {{p|Egypt}} rose again from its dark years,
Upon an isle amid the middle main
A palace people reached their height: their spheres

Were {{p|Crete}}, the largest island of the sea
{{p|Aegean}}, and the {{p|Minoans}} were their kind,
Who wrote in {{p|Linear A}}, their own, free
From any other tongue that men could find.

From nineteen hundred B.C.E. they grew,
And flourished till the fifteen-hundreds came;
{{p|Knossos}}, {{p|Phaistos}}, {{p|Malia}}, {{p|Zakros}} too,
The palaces that held their wealth and name.

They had no walls; the palaces arose
Straight from the land, unguarded, open, free;
Their fleet kept them, and in the sea, their foes
Could never reach them: masters of the sea.

And on their walls the frescoes moved with life:
The dolphins, octopus, and flying fish,
Wild goats and lilies, crocus, and the knife
Of saffron-gatherers, and every wish

Of bull-leapers, priestesses, and the lines
Of bearers, offerings; and how they cared
For sea and land, the beasts, the plants, the vines,
And light-wells lit the halls, and air was shared.

At {{p|Knossos}} water ran, both hot and cold,
And flushed away, four thousand years ago;
Fifteen hundred rooms, many-storied, old,
Around a central court, ran to and fro,

A labyrinth, where corridors would wind
With no one axis; and the bull was there
On every altar, horns of every kind,
And in the maze, the Minotaur's own lair.

The mother goddess with her snakes was theirs,
In open bodice, holding up the two;
And priestesses, not priests, led all the prayers,
And her young consort died, and rose anew.

This was the old {{p|Europe}}'s great mother, kept
Upon an island, safe within the sea,
Through all the years when other peoples slept,
The fairest work of all the {{x|Bronze Age}} free.

And far they traded: {{p|Egypt}}, where they bore,
The Keftiu, their gifts before the throne;
The {{p|Levant}}, {{p|Anatolia}}, the shore
Of {{p|Cyclades}} and {{p|Greece}}; and theirs alone

Was the {{p|Aegean}} for some hundred years,
A lake of {{p|Crete}}: the oil, the wine, the tin,
The copper, cloth, and pottery; their peers
Were none, so rich the palaces within.

Then, fifteen hundred B.C.E., there came
The fire of {{p|Thera}}, northward, far away,
The greatest burst of mountains, and its flame
Blew out the island's heart into the bay,

And left the crescent of {{p|Santorini}};
The ash fell over {{p|Crete}} and over all,

# Chapter 153: The Middle Kingdom

And {{p|Egypt}} emerged from the {{p|First Intermediate Period}}.

The collapse of the {{p|Old Kingdom}} around twenty-two hundred BCE
Had fragmented the unified state into rival regional dynasties.
Upper and {{p|Lower Egypt}} had split. {{p|Herakleopolis}} ruled the
North. {{p|Thebes}}—an obscure provincial town at the time—gradually

Rose in the south. For over a century, {{p|Egypt}} was divided, weakened,
And suffering from the climate shifts that had brought down so
Many civilizations simultaneously across the {{p|Old World}}. And then,
Around twenty-sixty BCE, Mentuhotep II, king of {{p|Thebes}}, defeated

The northern dynasties and reunified the country. {{p|Egypt}} was whole
Again. The {{p|Middle Kingdom}} had begun.

                                                   The {{p|Middle Kingdom}}
Is generally dated from approximately twenty-fifty-five to seventeen
Hundred BCE—three and a half centuries of restored centralized rule.

It is often called, in {{p|Egyptian}} historiography, the classical
Period of {{p|Egyptian}} civilization—not because it was the wealthiest
Or the most expansive, but because it was the period of greatest
Literary and artistic refinement. The pyramid-building megalomania

Of the {{p|Old Kingdom}} had passed. The empire-building militarism of
The later {{p|New Kingdom}} had not yet arrived. The {{p|Middle Kingdom}} sat
In between—an {{p|Egypt}} that was confident but not aggressive, wealthy
But not extravagant, pious but not monumental. And in this quieter

Mode, it produced some of the finest {{p|Egyptian}} literature ever
Written.

            The {{p|Eleventh Dynasty}}, under Mentuhotep II and his
Successors, ruled from {{p|Thebes}}. The {{p|Twelfth Dynasty}}, which
Succeeded them around nineteen ninety BCE, moved the capital north

To {{p|Itjtawy}} (near modern {{p|El-Lisht}}), strategically positioned at
The junction between {{p|Upper}} and {{p|Lower Egypt}}. The {{p|Twelfth Dynasty}}
Included some of the most capable pharaohs of the entire {{p|Egyptian}}
Sequence: Amenemhat I (founder of the dynasty), Senusret I,

Senusret III, and Amenemhat III. These kings consolidated royal
Authority, rebuilt infrastructure, launched ambitious irrigation
Projects (particularly the land-reclamation works in the {{p|Faiyum
Oasis}}), expanded {{p|Egyptian}} control south into {{p|Nubia}}, and

Patronized the arts and sciences on a scale that would make the
{{p|Middle Kingdom}} the reference-point for every subsequent
{{p|Egyptian}} renaissance.

                                 And the literature of the {{p|Middle
Kingdom}} is remarkable. This is the period of the great tales and

Wisdom texts that would be copied and studied in {{p|Egyptian}} schools
For the next fifteen hundred years. {{p|The Tale of Sinuhe}}—a narrative
Of an {{p|Egyptian}} noble who flees the court after a political
Disturbance, lives in exile among the {{p|Amorites}} in {{p|Canaan}}, marries

A {{p|Canaanite}} princess, rises to prominence in the foreign court,
And eventually returns home to {{p|Egypt}} to be buried among his
Ancestors—is one of the first great works of narrative prose
Literature in human history. It combines autobiography, adventure,

Reflection, and homecoming-longing in a form that prefigures the
Novel. {{p|Sinuhe}}'s yearning for {{p|Egypt}} during his exile—the famous
Passages in which he describes his desire to be buried in his
Homeland, to have an {{p|Egyptian}} funeral, to rejoin his ancestors

In the proper {{p|Egyptian}} way—is one of the most emotionally direct
Pieces of ancient literature that survives. Three thousand nine
Hundred years later, it still reads as recognizably human.

And {{p|The Shipwrecked Sailor}}—a tale of a sailor stranded on a
Magical island ruled by a giant talking serpent, who tells the

Sailor his own tragic story before sending him home with gifts.
The tale is a frame-narrative, a fantasy, a meditation on fate
And resilience. {{p|The Tale of the Eloquent Peasant}}—a small
Farmer appeals to the royal courts after being cheated by a

Corrupt official, delivering nine increasingly elaborate speeches
On the nature of justice. The king, secretly pleased by the
Eloquence, deliberately delays deciding the case so that more
Speeches will be produced. The tale is a meditation on rhetoric,

On justice, and on the relationship between the weak petitioner
And the powerful authorities. {{p|The Instruction of Amenemhat}}—a
Political-philosophical text in which the ghost of the murdered
Amenemhat I instructs his son on the treacheries of kingship

And the necessity of constant vigilance. {{p|The Instruction for
Merikare}}—a practical handbook for young princes on good
Governance. {{p|The Dialogue of a Man with His Ba}}—a suicidal man
Debates with his own soul about whether life is worth living, one

Of the earliest surviving philosophical texts on the meaning of
Existence. {{p|The Satire of the Trades}}—a humorous poem listing
The miseries of various manual occupations (the barber, the
Washerman, the field laborer, the carpenter) to encourage the

Reader's son to become a scribe instead.

                                                These are not the
Religious funerary texts of the {{p|Old Kingdom}} (the {{p|Pyramid Texts}})
Or the extended spell-books of the {{p|New Kingdom}} (the {{p|Book of the

Dead}}). These are literary works—short, focused, narratively or
Philosophically interesting, designed to be enjoyed as well as
Studied. They are {{p|Egypt}}'s classical literature. They were copied
Over and over by later scribes as school texts, which is why they

Survive in multiple manuscript traditions. And they reveal a
Civilization that, having settled into a more modest political
Scale after the grandiosity of the pyramid age, turned its energies
Toward the cultivation of mind and letter. The {{p|Middle Kingdom}}

Scribe was the intellectual ideal of subsequent {{p|Egyptian}}
Civilization. Every later period looked back at the {{p|Twelfth
Dynasty}} the way {{p|Renaissance Europe}} looked back at classical
{{p|Rome}}—as the golden age that later generations tried to imitate.

And religiously, the {{p|Middle Kingdom}} saw important developments.
Osiris—the god of the underworld, the mummified god whose story
Of death and resurrection had been the exclusive preserve of the
Royal afterlife in the {{p|Old Kingdom}}—was democratized. The

Possibility of eternal life through identification with Osiris
Was extended, in the {{p|Middle Kingdom}}, from the pharaoh alone to
The broader noble class, and eventually, in principle, to any
{{p|Egyptian}} who could afford the funerary rites. Every {{p|Egyptian}}

Could now become an Osiris at death. The {{p|Coffin Texts}}—spells
Inscribed on the sides of wooden coffins to guide the deceased
Through the underworld—were the {{p|Middle Kingdom}}'s expanded
Version of the {{p|Old Kingdom}}'s royal {{p|Pyramid Texts}}. The afterlife

Was being opened up. The cosmic judgment, in which the deceased's
Heart was weighed against the feather of Ma'at (truth/justice),
Applied to all {{p|Egyptians}}, not just the king. A moral-religious
Democratization that was, for the ancient world, striking.

And {{p|Middle Kingdom}} {{p|Egypt}} opened itself to its neighbors to a
Degree that the {{p|Old Kingdom}} had not. Trade flourished with the
{{p|Levant}}, with {{p|Nubia}}, with {{p|Punt}} (on the {{p|Somali}} or {{p|Yemeni}}
Coast of the {{p|Red Sea}}—the frankincense-and-myrrh source). Diplomatic

Relations were established with the {{p|Cretan}} {{p|Minoans}}, whose frescoes
Would later appear at {{p|Middle Kingdom}}-influenced sites across the
{{p|Aegean}}, and whose ceramics began arriving in {{p|Egyptian}} ports. The
Relationship with the {{p|Semitic}} peoples to the northeast intensified—

{{p|Canaanite}} populations began migrating into the {{p|Nile Delta}} in
Increasing numbers, settling in the eastern delta as pastoralists,
Farmers, and merchants. These were the {{p|Asiatics}}, as the {{p|Egyptians}}
Called them—the J-haplogroup {{p|Semitic}} speakers whose presence

Would gradually transform the demographic balance of {{p|Lower Egypt}}.
By the end of the {{p|Middle Kingdom}}, the eastern {{p|Nile Delta}} had a
Substantial {{p|Canaanite}} population coexisting with the native
{{p|Egyptian}} population. And this coexistence would, in a later

Generation, produce the {{p|Hyksos}}—the foreign dynasty that would
Eventually rule {{p|Lower Egypt}}. But the {{p|Middle Kingdom}} phase of
This process was peaceful. {{p|Canaanites}} were welcomed as useful
Subjects. {{p|Egyptian}} culture and {{p|Canaanite}} culture began the long

Process of mutual influence that would shape the ancient {{p|Near
Eastern}} world. And this is where the biblical Joseph story, the
{{x|Gaiad}} will argue in the following chapters, is historically situated—
During the later {{p|Middle Kingdom}} or the transition to the {{p|Second

Intermediate Period}}, when {{p|Canaanite}} families entering {{p|Egypt}}
Could rise to positions of prominence and influence.

The {{p|Middle Kingdom}} did not last. By the {{p|Thirteenth Dynasty}},
The central state began to weaken. Rapid succession of short-reigned

Pharaohs, climate pressures from the occasional low {{p|Nile}} flood,
And the gradual political autonomization of the {{p|Canaanite}} population
In the eastern delta led, around seventeen-forty to seventeen-thirty

{{p|Hyksos}}—the "rulers of foreign lands," as the {{p|Egyptians}} called
Them—took control of the eastern delta and established their own
Dynasty there, with its capital at {{p|Avaris}}. The {{p|Second Intermediate
Period}} began. A second {{p|Egyptian}} collapse, three centuries after

The first. This second collapse would be the context of the biblical

                      But the {{p|Middle Kingdom}} itself—as a phase, as
An achievement, as a cultural legacy—was {{p|Egypt}}'s classical age.

It produced the literature, the religious democratization, the
Foreign relations, and the domestic consolidation that subsequent
{{p|Egyptian}} civilization would look back at as its reference-point.
It is less famous, outside of {{p|Egyptological}} circles, than the

{{p|Old Kingdom}} with its pyramids or the {{p|New Kingdom}} with its

Culturally accomplished phase of {{p|Egyptian}} history. The pharaohs
Were less grandiose. The art was more refined. The literature was

More sophisticated. The religion was more democratic. And the
{{p|Egyptian}} sense of itself as a coherent civilization, after the
Near-death experience of the {{p|First Intermediate Period}}, was
Consolidated and strengthened. The civilization had survived its

First collapse and come back stronger in certain important ways.

# Chapter 154: Jacob, Joseph, and the Hyksos

And so the story shifts to a specific {{p|Canaanite}} family.

Jacob—grandson of Abraham, son of Isaac, father of twelve

And {{p|Islamic}} traditions, and the {{x|Gaiad}} will not rehearse them in
Detail. What the {{x|Gaiad}} does do, in this chapter, is place Jacob
And his family historically—in the late {{p|Middle Kingdom}} or the
Beginning of the {{p|Second Intermediate Period}}, around seventeen-

Hundred to sixteen-fifty BCE, in the context of the {{p|Hyksos}}
Arrival in {{p|Egypt}}. And the {{x|Gaiad}} makes a specific claim: Joseph's
Rise to prominence in {{p|Egypt}} is legible precisely because he is
A {{p|Canaanite}}—a member of the same J-haplogroup {{p|Semitic}} population

That is, at this moment, gaining political power in the eastern
{{p|Nile Delta}}.

                      The biblical account has Joseph sold by his
Brothers to a caravan of {{p|Ishmaelite}} traders, who carry him down
To {{p|Egypt}} and sell him to Potiphar, a high-ranking {{p|Egyptian}}

Official. Joseph rises in Potiphar's household. Is falsely
Accused by Potiphar's wife. Is imprisoned. Interprets the dreams
Of two fellow prisoners. Is eventually brought before the pharaoh
To interpret the pharaoh's own dreams—of seven fat cows and seven

Lean cows, of seven full ears of grain and seven withered ears.
Joseph predicts seven years of abundance followed by seven years
Of famine. The pharaoh appoints him vizier—second only to the king
Himself—to manage the stockpiling and distribution of grain. When

The famine comes, {{p|Egypt}} has enough. The surrounding regions do
Not. Jacob's remaining sons come down to {{p|Egypt}} to buy grain.
Joseph recognizes them. They do not recognize him. A dramatic
Recognition scene follows, after which Joseph brings his entire

Family down to {{p|Egypt}} and settles them in the region of {{p|Goshen}}—
The eastern {{p|Nile Delta}}, the pastoral lands best suited to their
Herding lifestyle.

                       And the {{x|Gaiad}}'s historical overlay on this
Narrative is the {{p|Hyksos}} framework. The {{p|Hyksos}}—whose name in

{{p|Egyptian}} is Heka-khasut, "rulers of foreign lands"—were a
{{p|Semitic}} population (primarily J2-haplogroup, with some admixture)
Who had been migrating into the eastern {{p|Nile Delta}} throughout the
Late {{p|Middle Kingdom}}. They spoke a {{p|West Semitic}} language related

To early {{p|Canaanite}} and {{p|Amorite}}. They worshipped {{p|Semitic}} gods
Like Baal and Anat, which they syncretized with {{p|Egyptian}}
Seth (the god of the desert, storms, and foreign regions). Their
Culture was a hybrid—retaining {{p|Canaanite}} dress, names, diet, and

Religious practices while adopting {{p|Egyptian}} administrative forms,
Hieroglyphic writing, and some {{p|Egyptian}} cultic practices. They
Brought with them technologies that the {{p|Egyptians}} had not yet
Developed: the horse-drawn chariot, the composite bow, advanced

Bronze metallurgy. These technologies would, when the {{p|Egyptians}}
Eventually expelled the {{p|Hyksos}}, be retained and weaponized by the
{{p|New Kingdom}} pharaohs against their own neighbors.

Around seventeen-thirty to seventeen-twenty BCE, the {{p|Hyksos}}
Seized political control of the eastern delta and established the

{{p|Fifteenth Dynasty}} at {{p|Avaris}} (the site of modern {{p|Tell el-Dab'a}}).
The native {{p|Egyptian}} {{p|Thirteenth}} and {{p|Fourteenth Dynasties}} were
Displaced from the delta and retreated south. The native {{p|Seventeenth
Dynasty}} eventually established itself at {{p|Thebes}} as the {{p|Hyksos}}-

Resistance regime. For about a century and a half, {{p|Egypt}} was
Divided—the {{p|Hyksos}} ruling the delta, the native {{p|Egyptians}} ruling
{{p|Upper Egypt}}, with {{p|Nubian}} kingdoms further south occasionally
Allied with either side depending on strategic calculations.

And this is the world in which, the {{x|Gaiad}} argues, the biblical
Jacob and Joseph narratives take place. Joseph's rise to
Vizier-level prominence is possible because he is a {{p|Canaanite}}—
And the pharaoh who appoints him is a {{p|Hyksos}} pharaoh. The

{{p|Hyksos}} rulers would naturally favor another {{p|Canaanite}} in their
Administration. Joseph's brothers' migration into {{p|Egypt}} is
Possible because {{p|Canaanites}} are at this moment welcome in the
Delta—this is the century of active {{p|Canaanite}} settlement in

{{p|Lower Egypt}}. Jacob's family settling in {{p|Goshen}} is the norm,
Not the exception; thousands of other {{p|Canaanite}} families were
Doing the same thing. The biblical narrative sits comfortably
Inside the {{p|Hyksos}} period.

And the {{x|Gaiad}} makes a further claim: the {{p|Hyksos}} themselves are
Not the villains that later {{p|Egyptian}} historiography made them out
To be. Later native {{p|Egyptian}} sources—particularly the {{p|New Kingdom}}
Accounts written after the {{p|Hyksos}} expulsion—would demonize them as

Brutal foreign invaders who destroyed temples, oppressed the native
Population, and plundered the wealth of {{p|Egypt}}. But archaeology
Tells a different story. The {{p|Hyksos}} at {{p|Avaris}} actively maintained
{{p|Egyptian}} administrative systems, built {{p|Egyptian}}-style temples

Alongside their own, patronized {{p|Egyptian}} scribal traditions,
Preserved {{p|Egyptian}} cultural practices in their territory. They
Were not destroyers—they were adapters. They governed {{p|Egypt}} as
Foreign rulers, yes, but competent and respectful foreign rulers.

Their rule was not a catastrophe; it was a transition. And the
Joseph story, in its biblical form, is consistent with this:
Joseph serves a pharaoh who welcomes {{p|Canaanites}}, who appoints
One as vizier, who settles an extended {{p|Canaanite}} family in the

Delta with royal favor. This is a {{p|Hyksos}}-era pharaoh, not a
Native {{p|Egyptian}} pharaoh. The later narrative demonization of the
{{p|Hyksos}} distorted the memory. The biblical narrative, which
Preserves the memory of {{p|Egyptian}}-{{p|Canaanite}} friendly relations,

Is closer to the archaeological reality than the later {{p|Egyptian}}
Propaganda is.

                    The {{p|Hyksos}} period lasted about a century and
A half. Six {{p|Hyksos}} pharaohs ruled the delta from {{p|Avaris}}.
Their names—preserved in Manetho's later king-list and in

Archaeological seals—are {{p|Semitic}}: Salitis, Apophis, Khyan,
Apepi. These are not native {{p|Egyptian}} names. They are {{p|Canaanite}}
Names transcribed into {{p|Egyptian}}. And they ruled the delta with
{{p|Egyptian}} administrative competence while maintaining {{p|Canaanite}}

Cultural identity. A model of dual-culture governance that the
Ancient {{p|Near East}} would see many times thereafter—in {{p|Mesopotamia}}
Under various {{p|Amorite}} and {{p|Kassite}} dynasties, in {{p|Mesopotamia}}
Under the {{p|Persians}}, in various later situations where a foreign

Dynasty adopted local administrative forms while retaining its
Own linguistic and religious identity.

                                             And the Jacob family—
The future Israelites—were, in the {{x|Gaiad}}'s reading, a sub-population
Of this larger {{p|Canaanite}} presence in the {{p|Hyksos}}-controlled delta.

They came during the {{p|Hyksos}} period. They flourished during it.
They were welcomed, even privileged, during it. Joseph's rise
Is legible against this background. And the specific tribal names

Economy and administration.

# Chapter 155: The Aryan Migration

And while the {{p|Hyksos}} were settling in the {{p|Nile Delta}}, another
Foreign people was settling—halfway across the world—in another
Great river valley. The parallel is structural. Foreign populations
Entering pre-existing civilizations on two continents at the same

Time. In the west, the {{p|Hyksos}} entering {{p|Egypt}}. In the east, the
{{p|Aryans}} entering {{p|India}}. Both arriving in the eighteenth, seventeenth,
And sixteenth centuries BCE. Both bringing their own languages,
Religions, and haplogroups into territories previously dominated

By other civilizations. Both transforming the cultures they entered,
But also being transformed by those cultures. The {{p|Hyksos}} became
Partly {{p|Egyptianized}}. The {{p|Aryans}} became partly {{p|Indianized}}. Both
Inserted themselves into existing civilizational matrices and came

Out, on the other side, as something neither purely themselves nor
Purely the people they had joined.

                                           The {{p|Aryan Migration}}.

                                                                          Here
The {{x|Gaiad}} must be careful. The word "{{p|Aryan}}" has been abused in

Modern history—used by nineteenth and twentieth century ideologies
To construct racial hierarchies, to justify conquest and genocide,
To fabricate a pure-blood master-race that never existed. The
{{x|Gaiad}} rejects all of that. The "{{p|Aryan}}" people of the ancient world

Were not a "race"; they were a linguistic and cultural group—
Speakers of the {{p|Indo-Iranian}} branch of {{p|Indo-European}}, probably
Mixed in ancestry, probably a distinct elite lineage within a broader
Population, but not the "master race" of {{p|Nazi}} mythology. They were

A migrating population with a language and a religious tradition,
Nothing more supernatural than that. The word "{{p|Aryan}}" in its
Original context meant, simply, "noble" in the {{p|Indo-Iranian}}
Languages—a self-designation of the elite warrior class, not a

Racial category. The {{x|Gaiad}} uses the word in its original linguistic
And cultural sense, with no racial implications.

                                                        So: around
Fifteen-hundred BCE, give or take a century or two, a migration of
R1a-haplogroup {{p|Indo-Iranian}}-speaking peoples began moving

South from their homeland in the {{p|Central Asian}} steppes (the
{{p|Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex}}, or {{p|BMAC}}, was probably
A key staging ground). Some of them went southwest into what is
Now {{p|Iran}}, where they became the {{p|Iranian}} peoples—the {{p|Medes}},

The {{p|Persians}}, and the ancestors of the peoples who would produce
{{p|Zoroastrianism}}. Some of them went southeast, across the {{p|Hindu
Kush}} mountains and through the {{p|Khyber Pass}}, down into what is
Now {{p|Pakistan}} and northwestern {{p|India}}. This southeastern branch

Became the {{p|Indo-Aryan}} peoples of {{p|India}}. And they arrived in a
{{p|Punjab}} and upper {{p|Ganges}} region that was in the process of
Demographic transition—the {{p|Indus Valley}} civilization, the great
Pre-Aryan urban civilization of the subcontinent, was in the late

Stages of its collapse. The cities of {{p|Harappa}} and {{p|Mohenjo-daro}}
Had already been abandoned or were being abandoned. The population
Was dispersing eastward and southward. The {{p|Indus Valley}} was no
Longer the organized urban civilization it had been a thousand

Years earlier.

                    The {{p|Aryan}} arrival is not, in the {{x|Gaiad}}'s
Reading, the cause of the {{p|Indus Valley}} collapse. The collapse
Was already underway before they arrived—probably driven by climate

Change (the gradual drying of the {{p|Sarasvati}} river system, the
Shifting of monsoons) and by internal political and economic
Pressures. The {{p|Aryans}} arrived into an ongoing collapse, not a
Stable civilization. They filled a partial vacuum. And—crucially—

The {{x|Gaiad}} reads the relationship between the incoming {{p|Aryans}}
And the pre-existing {{p|Dravidian}} and other non-{{p|Aryan}} populations
As substantially cooperative rather than violently adversarial.

This is an important departure from some earlier narratives. The

Older "{{p|Aryan invasion theory}}," developed in nineteenth-century
{{p|European}} scholarship, posited a violent conquest—chariot-driving
{{p|Aryan}} warriors destroying the peaceful {{p|Dravidian}} cities, imposing
Their language and religion by force, pushing the pre-existing

Populations south into the {{p|Deccan}} and {{p|Tamil}} regions. More recent
Scholarship has significantly revised this picture. The collapse
Of the {{p|Indus Valley}} cities preceded the {{p|Aryan}} arrival. The
Incoming {{p|Indo-Aryans}} were not a massive invasion force but a

Gradual trickle of migrating populations over several centuries.
Genetic studies show substantial admixture, not replacement—the
{{p|Indian}} population today is a blend of pre-{{p|Aryan}} ancestry (primarily
H, L, R2a, and other haplogroups associated with the {{p|Indus

Valley}} populations) and {{p|Aryan}} ancestry (R1a-M417, with its
Characteristic steppe-pastoralist signature). The mixing happened.
It was not an extermination. It was not even really a conquest in
The traditional military sense. It was a long, complex, mutually

Transformative encounter.

                                  And the cultural results of that
Encounter produced what became classical {{p|Hindu}} civilization. The
{{p|Vedic}} hymns—the {{p|Rig Veda}} in particular, composed between

Approximately fifteen-hundred and twelve-hundred BCE in the
{{p|Punjab}} and upper {{p|Ganges}} region—are the oldest surviving
{{x|Indo-European}} religious texts. They preserve the {{p|Aryan}} religious
Imagination in its most archaic form: sky-father Dyaus, storm-god

Indra, fire-god Agni, soma-intoxication ritual, horse sacrifice,
Elaborate priestly hymns addressed to an array of celestial and
Atmospheric deities. The {{p|Vedic}} religion is recognizably {{p|Indo-
European}}—the same pantheon structure as {{p|Greek}}, {{p|Roman}}, and

{{p|Germanic}} religion, just with different names for the gods. Dyaus
Is the same as Zeus and Jupiter and Tyr ({{p|German}})—all
Descended from {{x|Proto-Indo-European}} {{p|Dyeus Phter}} ("sky father").
Indra is cognate with the {{x|Greek}} Heracles in his storm-god

Aspects. The {{p|Vedas}} are {{p|Aryan}} religion in its pure form.

But even within the {{p|Rig Veda}}, the encounter with pre-{{p|Aryan}}
Populations is visible. The hymns mention "Dasyus" and "Dasas"—
The indigenous peoples the {{p|Aryans}} encountered—sometimes as

Enemies but often as subjects, servants, and fellow participants
In {{p|Indian}} society. Some {{p|Aryan}} deities show syncretic features
That suggest influence from pre-{{p|Aryan}} religious traditions. Rudra
—the wild and dangerous storm-and-mountain god who would eventually

Become Shiva—has features that don't quite fit the standard
{{p|Indo-European}} pantheon. Rudra/Shiva is probably, in part, a
Pre-{{p|Aryan}} deity absorbed into the {{p|Vedic}} pantheon. The
Shiva lingam—the iconic phallic emblem of Shiva-worship—may

Descend from pre-{{p|Aryan}} {{p|Indus Valley}} cultic objects. (A number of
Conical and cylindrical stone objects found at {{p|Mohenjo-daro}} and
Other {{p|Indus Valley}} sites have been interpreted as possible early
{{p|lingas}}, though the interpretation is not certain.) The worship

Of Shiva is, in one reading, a pre-{{p|Aryan}} {{p|Indus Valley}} tradition
That survived the civilizational collapse, was absorbed into the
Incoming {{p|Aryan}} religion as Rudra, and gradually grew in importance
Until, by the classical period, Shiva was one of the three great

Gods of {{p|Hinduism}} alongside Vishnu and Brahma. If so, then
{{p|Hindu}} civilization is not a pure {{p|Aryan}} religious civilization—
It is a synthesis, with {{p|Indus Valley}} elements preserved within
{{p|Aryan}} frameworks. The continuity is real. The civilization

Did not simply end and restart; it flowed through, absorbed, and
Continued to carry pre-existing religious content under new names
And in new frameworks.

                            And the caste system ({{p|varna}}) emerges
In this encounter. The four-{{p|varna}} scheme of the {{p|Rig Veda}}—

{{p|Brahmins}} (priests), {{p|Kshatriyas}} (warriors), {{p|Vaishyas}} (merchants
And farmers), and {{p|Shudras}} (servants)—is the {{p|Indo-European}}
Tripartite-plus-one social ideology familiar from other {{p|Indo-
European}} societies, extended with a fourth group for the non-

{{p|Aryan}} subject population. The {{p|Shudras}} are originally the
Pre-{{p|Aryan}} populations, accepted into the social system at its
Lowest rank. Over subsequent centuries, this four-fold scheme
Would be elaborated into the thousands of sub-castes ({{p|jatis}})

That make up the classical {{p|Indian}} caste structure. The caste
System is, in its origins, the social codification of the {{p|Aryan}}-
Non-{{p|Aryan}} encounter—a rigid hierarchy that preserved the
Distinction between the incoming population and the pre-existing

One. The caste system's later history—its ossification, its
Religious legitimation through the doctrine of {{p|karma}} and
Rebirth, its role in keeping {{p|Indian}} civilization structurally
Stagnant for much of its later history—is covered in the "{{p|India

Stagnation}}" chapter much later. Here it is just noted as one of
The structural inheritances of the {{p|Aryan Migration}} period.

                                                                         And
Language. The {{p|Aryans}} brought {{p|Sanskrit}}—or the early version of
It that linguists call {{p|Old Indo-Aryan}} or {{p|Vedic Sanskrit}}. This

Language, over subsequent centuries, became the sacred and classical
Language of {{p|Indian}} civilization. From {{p|Sanskrit}} descended the
Modern {{p|Indo-Aryan}} languages: {{p|Hindi}}, {{p|Urdu}}, {{p|Bengali}}, {{p|Punjabi}},
{{p|Marathi}}, {{p|Gujarati}}, and many others. More than a billion people

Today speak an {{p|Indo-Aryan}} language as their native tongue. That
Linguistic heritage is the {{p|Aryan Migration}}'s most durable legacy.
The {{p|Dravidian}} languages of the south—{{p|Tamil}}, {{p|Telugu}}, {{p|Kannada}},
{{p|Malayalam}}—represent the survival of the pre-{{p|Aryan}} linguistic

Substrate, much as {{p|Basque}} preserves the pre-{{p|Indo-European}}
Substrate of {{p|Europe}}. The line between {{p|Aryan}} and {{p|Dravidian}}
{{p|India}} is roughly the boundary between the two great language
Families, though bilingualism, literary exchange, and cultural

Mixing have blurred it extensively over the centuries.

The {{p|Aryan Migration}} is not, in the {{x|Gaiad}}'s reading, a catastrophe.
It is a transition—one that produced the civilization that would
Compose the {{p|Vedas}}, the {{p|Upanishads}}, the {{p|Bhagavad Gita}}, the

{{p|Mahabharata}}, the {{p|Ramayana}}, and the entire corpus of classical
{{p|Indian}} literature and philosophy. It brought {{p|Indo-European}}
Language and religion into {{p|South Asia}}. It merged with the pre-
Existing civilizations of the subcontinent to produce something

New—neither purely {{p|Aryan}} nor purely pre-{{p|Aryan}}, but a synthesis
That became, over the following centuries, one of the most
Spiritually and intellectually rich civilizations in human history.

And the parallel with the {{p|Hyksos}} holds. Both migrations happened
In roughly the same period. Both were R-haplogroup (the {{p|Hyksos}}
Were J2, not R; I correct myself—the parallel is structural,
Not genetic. The {{p|Hyksos}} were J2, the {{p|Aryans}} were R1a. But

Both were foreign populations entering an established civilization
At roughly the same time.) Both adopted substantial elements of
The civilizations they entered. Both were, eventually, partially
Expelled or absorbed. The {{p|Hyksos}} would be expelled from {{p|Egypt}}

By Ahmose I around fifteen-fifty BCE. The {{p|Aryans}} would not
Be expelled from {{p|India}}—they would merge so thoroughly with the
Pre-existing populations that no expulsion was possible. The
Demographics of {{p|India}} were permanently transformed. But both

Migrations showed the same pattern: foreign arrival, partial
Absorption, cultural transformation in both directions, and
Lasting legacy.

                   The {{p|Aryan Migration}}. The R1a-haplogroup
Chariot-riders entering {{p|South Asia}}. The {{p|Vedic}} hymns. The

Language tree that includes a billion modern speakers.

Shiva rising from pre-{{p|Aryan}} {{p|Indus}} cultic objects into

# Chapter 156: The New Kingdom

And the {{p|Hyksos}} were expelled.

Around fifteen-fifty BCE, Ahmose I, the {{p|Theban}} pharaoh of the
{{p|Eighteenth Dynasty}}, completed the campaign his elder brother
Kamose had begun. The {{p|Hyksos}} capital at {{p|Avaris}} was captured.
The {{p|Hyksos}} rulers and a portion of their population were expelled

Back into the {{p|Levant}}. Their fortress-city of {{p|Sharuhen}} in southern
{{p|Canaan}} was besieged for three years and finally taken. {{p|Egypt}} was
Reunified under native {{p|Egyptian}} rule. The {{p|New Kingdom}} had begun.

But the {{p|Egypt}} that emerged from the {{p|Hyksos}} expulsion was
Transformed. It had learned, during the century and a half of
{{p|Hyksos}} rule, the chariot, the composite bow, bronze-age military
Technology that the native {{p|Egyptian}} dynasties had not previously

Possessed. The humiliation of foreign rule had bred a determination
Never to let such a thing happen again. The {{p|New Kingdom}} {{p|Egypt}}
Was militarized, outward-looking, imperial. For the first time in
Its history, {{p|Egypt}} projected power far beyond its traditional

{{p|Nile Valley}} borders. {{p|Egyptian}} armies campaigned in {{p|Canaan}}, in
{{p|Syria}}, as far north as the {{p|Euphrates}} under Thutmose I. They
Campaigned south into {{p|Nubia}}, where they established direct
{{p|Egyptian}} administration over what had previously been nominally

Tributary regions. The {{p|Egyptian}} Empire—the first real {{p|Egyptian}}
Empire in the full imperial sense—was born.

The {{p|Eighteenth Dynasty}} was the great age of this expansion.
Ahmose I, Amenhotep I, Thutmose I, Thutmose II—then

Hatshepsut. One of the most remarkable figures in {{p|Egyptian}}
History. Daughter of Thutmose I, wife and half-sister of
Thutmose II, stepmother of the young Thutmose III. When
Thutmose II died, Hatshepsut initially served as regent for her

Young stepson. But within a few years, she took the title of
Pharaoh herself—a female pharaoh, ruling in her own right, in a
Culture that had almost no precedent for female kingship. She
Reigned for approximately twenty-two years. She was astonishingly

Successful. She prioritized trade over warfare—the famous expedition
To {{p|Punt}} (on the {{p|Red Sea}} coast), which brought back myrrh-trees,
Incense, exotic animals, and {{p|African}} wealth, was Hatshepsut's
Crowning diplomatic achievement. She built one of the most beautiful

Mortuary temples in {{p|Egypt}}—{{p|Deir el-Bahari}}, on the western bank
Of the {{p|Nile}} opposite {{p|Thebes}}, a terraced colonnaded structure
Cut into the cliff-face. Her sculptures often depicted her with a
Ceremonial false beard (the standard iconography of pharaonic

Kingship), but her feminine features were also retained. She
Navigated the gender-contradictions of her role with a sophisticated
Adaptive strategy. And when she died, Thutmose III—now an adult—
Took the throne. Years later, Thutmose III or his successors

Attempted to erase Hatshepsut's memory: her images were chiseled
Off public monuments, her cartouches defaced. This was not entirely
Successful. She was rediscovered by modern {{p|Egyptologists}}, and her
Full stature as one of the greatest pharaohs of the {{p|New Kingdom}}

Has been restored.

                         Thutmose III himself was the military
Genius of the dynasty. He conducted seventeen campaigns into the
{{p|Levant}} and {{p|Syria}}, consolidating {{p|Egyptian}} control over the entire

Region. His greatest victory, at {{p|Megiddo}} (the site whose name
Would become, in {{p|Greek}}, "{{p|Armageddon}}" in the {{p|Book of Revelation}}),
Defeated a coalition of {{p|Canaanite}} kings and established {{p|Egyptian}}
Supremacy over the {{p|Levant}} for the next century. Thutmose III is

Sometimes called "{{p|The Napoleon of Egypt}}" by modern historians—a
Tactically brilliant field commander who consolidated an empire.

But the {{p|New Kingdom}}'s expansion and militarization created a
Problem at home. The {{p|Amun}} priesthood—centered at {{p|Thebes}}, the

Dynastic capital—grew enormously wealthy on the spoils of empire.
Tribute from conquered territories poured into the temple of
Amun-Ra at {{p|Karnak}}. The {{p|Amun}} priesthood's power approached
Rivalry with the royal court. And it is in this context—the tension

Between the royal authority and the entrenched {{p|Amun}} priesthood—
That Akhenaten rose to the throne.

In his own time, Tutankhamun was a minor pharaoh who reigned

# Chapter 157: The Hittites

In the north, beyond the {{p|Sinai}}, beyond {{p|Canaan}}, beyond {{p|Syria}},
In the high plateau country of what is now central {{p|Turkey}},
A kingdom was emerging that would become {{p|Egypt}}'s great rival.

The {{p|Hittites}}.

                       An {{p|Indo-European}} people whose R1b haplogroup
Ancestors had arrived in {{p|Anatolia}} quite early in the {{p|Yamnaya}}
Expansion—probably in the third millennium BCE, through the

{{p|Caucasus}} or across the {{p|Aegean}}—and who had established themselves
As the dominant power in the central {{p|Anatolian}} plateau. Their
Language, {{p|Hittite}}, is the earliest attested {{p|Indo-European}}
Language in the historical record. It was first written in the

Adapted {{p|Akkadian}} cuneiform system, which the {{p|Hittites}} had
Borrowed from the {{p|Mesopotamian}} scribal tradition. The texts they
Left behind—some thirty thousand clay tablets recovered from their
Capital at {{p|Hattusa}}—are the oldest {{p|Indo-European}} written

Documents. They preserve {{p|Hittite}} royal correspondence, legal
Codes, religious texts, diplomatic treaties, historical annals,
Ritual handbooks, and mythological narratives. The {{p|Hittites}} were
A literate, bureaucratic, well-documented civilization. And when

The archives at {{p|Hattusa}} were rediscovered in the late nineteenth
And early twentieth centuries, they revolutionized our understanding
Of the {{x|Bronze Age}}.

                           The {{p|Hittite}} kingdom—or {{p|Hatti}}, as it
Called itself—was founded around seventeen-fifty BCE by Anitta,
A king who had conquered the city of {{p|Hattusa}} and several other

{{p|Anatolian}} centers. The {{p|Old Hittite Kingdom}} was consolidated
Under Hattusili I around sixteen-fifty BCE, with {{p|Hattusa}} as
Its capital. The kingdom expanded rapidly. Under Mursili I, the
{{p|Hittites}} conducted a remarkable military expedition: they marched

South across the {{p|Taurus Mountains}}, through {{p|Syria}}, and all the
Way down to {{p|Babylon}}. In fifteen-ninety-five BCE, Mursili I
Sacked {{p|Babylon}}—this was the raid that ended the {{p|Old Babylonian}}
Empire of Hammurabi's dynasty. The {{p|Hittites}} did not stay to

Occupy {{p|Babylon}}; they withdrew back to {{p|Anatolia}} with their
Plunder. But the sack was devastating, and it marks the end of
The {{p|Old Babylonian}} period and the transition to the {{p|Kassite}}
Dynasty in {{p|Mesopotamia}}. The {{p|Hittites}}, having projected power

Across a thousand miles, retreated to their highland capital and
Consolidated.

                   The {{p|Hittite}} empire reached its peak in the
Fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE under a series of capable
Rulers: Suppiluliuma I, Mursili II, Muwatalli II, Hattusili III,

Tudhaliya IV. The empire controlled central and eastern {{p|Anatolia}},
Northern {{p|Syria}}, and much of the {{p|Levant}} (in competition and
Occasional cooperation with {{p|Egypt}}). The {{p|Hittites}} were the
Northern pole of a bipolar {{x|Bronze Age}} political system in which

{{p|Egypt}} was the southern pole. The two empires met, diplomatically
Or militarily, at their mutual border zone in {{p|Syria}} and the
Upper {{p|Levant}}. And the great flashpoint was the {{p|Battle of Kadesh}}
In twelve-seventy-four BCE.

                                  {{p|Kadesh}}. A small city on the
{{p|Orontes River}} in central {{p|Syria}}. Strategically located on the
Border zone between {{p|Hittite}} and {{p|Egyptian}} spheres of influence.
And in twelve-seventy-four BCE, Ramesses II of {{p|Egypt}} and

Muwatalli II of {{p|Hatti}} confronted each other here with the
Largest chariot forces ever fielded in human military history up
To that point. The {{p|Egyptian}} army consisted of perhaps twenty
Thousand infantry and two thousand chariots. The {{p|Hittite}} army

Fielded a similar infantry strength plus three thousand chariots.
The battle was massive, confused, and fought partly around a
{{p|Hittite}} ambush that caught Ramesses unprepared—the {{p|Egyptian}}
Pharaoh had advanced too far ahead of his main force, and his

Camp was overrun by {{p|Hittite}} chariotry. The battle was recovered—
Ramesses rallied his remaining forces and held the field by
Nightfall—but the outcome was indecisive. Both sides claimed
Victory. Both accounts are partially preserved: Ramesses's in

{{p|Egyptian}} inscriptions at {{p|Karnak}} and the {{p|Ramesseum}}, Muwatalli's
(Indirectly, through later {{p|Hittite}} documents) in the archives
At {{p|Hattusa}}. The historical consensus is that the battle was
Effectively a draw. Neither side had the ability to dislodge the

Other. And after years of continued low-level conflict, the two
Powers eventually negotiated a peace.

                                              The {{p|Egyptian-Hittite}}
Peace Treaty was signed around twelve-fifty-nine BCE, between
Ramesses II and Hattusili III (who had succeeded Muwatalli II

After a brief civil war). The treaty established a permanent peace
Between the two empires, a mutual defense pact against external
Aggression, and provisions for the extradition of political
Refugees. It was sealed with a dynastic marriage: Hattusili III's

Daughter married Ramesses II and became one of his principal
Queens. The treaty was written in cuneiform on silver tablets, with
Copies kept in both capitals. It is the oldest known international
Peace treaty in human history. A copy of the {{p|Hittite}} version on

Silver is displayed at the {{p|United Nations}} building in {{p|New York}}
As a symbolic precedent for modern international diplomacy. The
Treaty held for the rest of both empires' existences—the {{p|Hittite}}
Empire would collapse in the {{x|Bronze Age Collapse}} around twelve

Hundred BCE, and {{p|Egypt}} would never again face a northern rival
On the scale of {{p|Hatti}} until the rise of {{p|Assyria}} centuries
Later. But for the duration of both empires, the treaty held. The
First international peace treaty was also the first long-lasting

One.

     The {{p|Hittites}} were culturally distinctive. Their religion was
{{p|Indo-European}} in its deep structure (sky-father, storm-god, the
Usual pantheon) but heavily syncretized with {{p|Anatolian}} and

{{p|Hurrian}} elements absorbed from their neighbors and subject
Populations. The {{p|Hittites}} famously called their kingdom the
"{{x|Land of the Thousand Gods}}"—they incorporated every deity of every
Conquered territory into their expanding pantheon, rather than

Suppressing local cults. This made {{p|Hittite}} religion tolerant and
Syncretic, willing to add rather than replace. The storm-god
Teshub (borrowed from the {{p|Hurrians}}) was their chief deity. The
Sun-goddess of {{p|Arinna}} was his consort. Mountain-gods, river-gods,

Ancestral kings elevated to divine status, agricultural deities—all
Were honored. This religious inclusivism was a political strategy
As well: conquered populations were more likely to accept {{p|Hittite}}
Rule if their local gods were incorporated rather than abolished.

And the {{p|Hittites}} had a distinctive law code. It was generally
Less brutal than Hammurabi's code. Capital punishment was rare.
Most offenses carried restitution-based penalties—payment to the
Victim or the victim's family, rather than physical mutilation or

Execution. The {{p|Hittite}} law code is one of the less vengeful
Ancient {{p|Near Eastern}} codes. Scholars have speculated that this
Reflected {{p|Indo-European}} cultural differences from the {{p|Semitic}}
World. Whether that is true or whether it reflected specific

{{p|Anatolian}} circumstances is debated. But the {{p|Hittite}} legal
Tradition was noticeably less retributive than its southern
{{p|Mesopotamian}} counterpart.

And the {{p|Hittites}} in the biblical record—this is interesting. The
{{p|Hebrew Bible}} mentions "Hittites" repeatedly as one of the
Peoples of {{p|Canaan}} encountered by Abraham and later {{p|Israelite}}
Generations. Uriah the Hittite is a {{p|Hittite}} character in the

Court of King David. The biblical Hittites appear to be a
Subset of the actual {{p|Hittite}} population—specifically, {{p|Neo-Hittite}}
Remnant populations in northern {{p|Syria}} and southern {{p|Turkey}} who
Survived the fall of the {{p|Hittite}} Empire and continued to maintain

A distinct {{p|Hittite}} cultural identity into the first millennium BCE.
These {{p|Neo-Hittite}} city-states—{{p|Carchemish}}, {{p|Aleppo}}, {{p|Hamath}},
{{p|Sam'al}}—are where the biblical Hittites come from. The great
{{p|Hittite}} Empire was already gone by the time the biblical narrative

Takes shape, but its cultural and ethnic remnants persisted for
Several more centuries.

                              And the fall of the {{p|Hittite}} Empire
Is, the {{x|Gaiad}} has already indicated, part of the {{x|Bronze Age Collapse}}.
Around twelve-hundred BCE, the {{p|Hittite}} capital {{p|Hattusa}} was

Sacked and burned. The {{p|Hittite}} central administration collapsed.
The royal archives were hurriedly hidden in temples, which is why
They survived. The population scattered. The specific cause is
Debated—probably a combination of the {{p|Sea Peoples}} raids, internal

Rebellions (possibly by the {{p|Kaska}} tribes of the {{p|Black Sea}} coast),
Climate-induced famine, and the general systemic failure of the
{{x|Bronze Age}} international system. Whatever the cause, the {{p|Hittite}}
Empire ended suddenly and catastrophically, more so than any other

Major {{x|Bronze Age}} civilization. {{p|Egypt}} survived, diminished. {{p|Assyria}}
Survived, briefly interrupted. {{p|Babylon}} survived, under {{p|Kassite}}
Rule. But the {{p|Hittites}} as an imperial civilization were gone
Within a generation. Their capital was abandoned and forgotten.

Their language was lost. By the first century BCE, the word
"{{p|Hittite}}" had become, in the biblical and later traditions, a
Vague name for unnamed eastern peoples. The actual {{p|Hittite}} Empire
Was unknown to historians until Hugo Winckler and Theodor Makridi

Began excavating {{p|Hattusa}} in nineteen-oh-six and discovered the
Lost archive. Three thousand years of historical amnesia, ended by
A German-Turkish archaeological team. The rediscovery of the
{{p|Hittites}} is one of the great stories of twentieth-century archaeology.

And now we know them. We can read their tablets. We can name their
Kings. We can trace their wars and treaties. We can read the terms
Of the {{p|Kadesh}} peace treaty in the original {{p|Hittite}} cuneiform.
The empire that disappeared so completely that even the {{p|Bible}} lost

Its proper memory has been, in the last century, substantially
Reconstructed.

The sack of {{p|Babylon}}. The rivalry with {{p|Egypt}}. The {{p|Kadesh}} peace
Treaty. The "{{x|Land of the Thousand Gods}}." The law code less brutal

The collapse in the {{x|Bronze Age Collapse}}. The three thousand years

# Chapter 158: The Mycenaeans

And in the west, on the southern tip of the {{p|Greek}} mainland and
Across the {{p|Aegean}} islands, a third great {{x|Bronze Age}} civilization
Was reaching its peak. {{p|Mycenaean Greece}}.

                                                   Named for {{p|Mycenae}},
The citadel on a hill in the {{p|Argolid}} whose massive "{{p|Cyclopean}}"
Walls of unmortared limestone blocks, some weighing over twenty tons,
Dominated the landscape. {{p|Mycenae}} was the political and cultural

Center of the {{x|Bronze Age}} {{p|Greek}} world. Other major centers were
At {{p|Tiryns}}, {{p|Pylos}}, {{p|Athens}} (already a settled site, though
Not yet the city it would become), {{p|Thebes}}, {{p|Orchomenos}}, and
Several others. The {{p|Mycenaean}} civilization emerged around sixteen

Hundred BCE—roughly contemporary with the {{p|New Kingdom}} beginning
In {{p|Egypt}} and the {{p|Hittite}} kingdom consolidating in {{p|Anatolia}}—
And flourished until the {{x|Bronze Age Collapse}} around twelve hundred BCE.

The {{p|Mycenaeans}} were R1b-haplogroup {{p|Indo-European}}-speakers—
Descendants of the {{p|Yamnaya}} expansion that had reached {{p|Greece}} in
The early {{p|Bronze Age}}. Their language was an archaic form of {{p|Greek}},
Written in a syllabic script called {{p|Linear B}}—adapted from the

{{p|Minoan}} {{p|Linear A}} script that preceded it. Unlike {{p|Linear A}},
{{p|Linear B}} has been successfully deciphered. In nineteen-fifty-two,
Michael Ventris, a young {{p|English}} architect with a passion for
Ancient scripts, demonstrated that {{p|Linear B}} was used to write

Early {{p|Greek}}. The achievement is one of the most celebrated in
The history of decipherment. It opened up an entire {{x|Bronze Age}}
Civilization to modern understanding. We can read the {{p|Linear B}}
Tablets recovered from {{p|Pylos}}, {{p|Knossos}}, {{p|Mycenae}}, and other

{{p|Mycenaean}} sites, and they reveal a palace-centered economic and
Administrative system managing vast agricultural, craft, and
Commercial operations. {{p|Linear B}} tablets are administrative
Records—inventories of livestock, lists of workers, tax collections,

Distributions of rations, inventories of military equipment. They
Are not literature. But they provide a detailed picture of the
Bureaucratic machinery of a {{p|Mycenaean}} palace, managing the economic
Life of its region down to the number of bronze arrowheads in the

Armory and the ration of wheat allocated to each category of worker.

And above the bureaucratic record, the {{p|Mycenaean}} world was also
A warrior society. The {{p|Mycenaean}} elite warriors—depicted in
Frescoes, buried with bronze swords and boar's-tusk helmets and

Gold-masked faces—were an {{x|Indo-European}} warrior aristocracy of the
Type that the {{p|Yamnaya}} expansion had produced across the
{{x|Indo-European}} world. The shaft-graves at {{p|Mycenae}}, excavated by
Heinrich Schliemann in eighteen-seventy-six, contained gold funerary

Masks (including the famous "{{p|Mask of Agamemnon}}," though the
Mask actually predates any possible historical Agamemnon by
Several centuries), bronze weapons, elaborate jewelry, and other
Signs of an extraordinarily wealthy warrior elite. The {{p|Mycenaeans}}

Were, in their cultural self-conception, the heroes who would later
Be memorialized in Homer's poetry. They valued combat, loot,
Honor, and the gift-exchange diplomacy of aristocratic warrior
Bands. They were the original {{p|Greek}} aristocracy whose memory

Fueled the later {{p|Iron Age}} reconstruction of the "heroic age."

And their relationship with the {{p|Minoan}} civilization is complex.
The {{p|Mycenaeans}} had originally been the junior partners of the
{{p|Minoans}}—culturally influenced by the island civilization, less

Wealthy, less sophisticated. But around fourteen-fifty BCE, after
The {{p|Thera}} eruption had weakened the {{p|Minoan}} civilization, the
{{p|Mycenaeans}} invaded {{p|Crete}}, occupied the major palace centers,
And took over the {{p|Minoan}} administrative and cultural system. The

{{p|Linear B}} tablets found at {{p|Knossos}} date to this {{p|Mycenaean}}
Occupation period. The {{p|Mycenaeans}} adopted {{p|Minoan}} artistic
Styles, religious iconography, and palace architecture—but adapted
Them to their own {{p|Indo-European}} warrior ethos. {{p|Mycenaean}} frescoes,

Unlike the peaceful nature scenes of {{p|Minoan}} art, often depict
Warriors, hunts, and battle scenes. The serenity of {{p|Minoan}}
Civilization was replaced by the martial vigor of the {{p|Mycenaean}}
Overlay. But many {{p|Minoan}} elements survived: goddess-worship, the

Labyrinthine palace-plan, the bull-symbolism. The {{p|Mycenaean}}
Civilization was a hybrid of {{p|Indo-European}} warrior aristocracy
And {{p|Minoan}} palace sophistication. The combination was potent.

And it was wealthy. {{p|Mycenaean}} trade networks extended across the
{{p|Eastern Mediterranean}}. {{p|Mycenaean}} pottery has been found in {{p|Egypt}},
The {{p|Levant}}, {{p|Anatolia}}, {{p|Cyprus}}, and as far west as {{p|Sardinia}} and
{{p|Sicily}}. The {{p|Mycenaeans}} imported copper from {{p|Cyprus}}, tin from

Distant sources (possibly {{p|Cornwall}} or {{p|Afghanistan}}), gold from
{{p|Egypt}}, ivory from {{p|Syria}}, cedar from {{p|Lebanon}}. They exported
Olive oil, wine, textiles, and their distinctive pottery. The
{{p|Aegean}} was a trading lake in which {{p|Mycenaean}} ships sailed in

Every direction, moving goods that the palaces redistributed and
Taxed. The palatial economy was in the middle of the trade network,
And the wealth that flowed through it made {{p|Mycenae}} and its
Sister-cities genuinely rich by {{x|Bronze Age}} standards.

And one episode from this period—probably around the end of the
Thirteenth century BCE, shortly before the {{x|Bronze Age Collapse}}—
Would become the greatest memorial narrative in {{p|Western}} literature.
The {{p|Trojan War}}.

                         The historicity of the {{p|Trojan War}} is
Debated but, the {{x|Gaiad}} accepts, broadly real. There was a city
At {{p|Troy}} (the site called {{p|Hisarlik}} in northwestern {{p|Turkey}}),
And it was destroyed by violence, and the destruction can be dated

To roughly the right period. The site shows evidence of siege and
Burning in its {{p|Troy VIIa}} level, which is usually identified
With the {{p|Homeric}} {{p|Troy}}. Whether the war was fought by a single
Coalition of {{p|Mycenaean}} kings led by Agamemnon, whether the

Participants had the names Homer would give them six centuries
Later, whether Achilles and Hector and Odysseus and Helen and
Paris were historical individuals—these are uncertain. But a war
Between {{p|Mycenaean}} {{p|Greeks}} and the kingdom of {{p|Wilusa}} ({{p|Troy}}'s

{{p|Hittite}} name, which is cognate with "{{p|Ilios}}," {{p|Homer}}'s term) is
Attested in {{p|Hittite}} diplomatic correspondence. There was a real
Conflict over this real city between real {{p|Mycenaean}} forces and
Real {{p|Anatolian}} defenders. Homer's later epic dressed the event

In the heroic costume of the oral tradition, compressing and
Elaborating and fictionalizing, but the historical kernel was there.

                                                                            And
The {{p|Mycenaean}} civilization is, the {{x|Gaiad}} argues, the {{p|Greek}}
Contribution to the {{p|Bronze Age Collapse}} chapter. Within a

Generation or two after the {{p|Trojan War}}, the {{p|Mycenaean}} palaces
Themselves were destroyed. {{p|Mycenae}} was burned. {{p|Tiryns}} was
Sacked. {{p|Pylos}} was burned (which is why its {{p|Linear B}} tablets
Survive—they were baked hard by the fire instead of being allowed

To dissolve in the normal way). The entire palatial civilization
Collapsed in a generation around twelve-hundred BCE. The cause was
The general {{x|Bronze Age Collapse}}—{{p|Sea Peoples}} raids, possibly
Internal rebellions by the servile population, climate pressures,

And the systemic failure of the {{x|Bronze Age}} international trade
Network. The {{p|Mycenaean}} palaces went down in a coordinated wave
Of destructions that archaeologists can date, across many sites,
To the same twenty-year window. The civilization did not recover.

After the collapse, {{p|Greece}} entered a "{{p|Dark Age}}" that lasted
Approximately three centuries. {{p|Linear B}} writing was forgotten.
The palace economies vanished. Population collapsed. Monumental
Architecture ceased. Trade networks shrank to local exchange.

The {{p|Greeks}} of the {{p|Dark Age}} lived in small villages, farmed at
Subsistence levels, and preserved the memory of the {{p|Mycenaean}}
Heroic age only in oral tradition. When writing finally returned—
Via the {{p|Phoenician}} alphabet, around the eighth century BCE—the

{{p|Greeks}} were a completely different civilization from what the
{{p|Mycenaeans}} had been. They had forgotten {{p|Linear B}} entirely. They
Had forgotten the palace economies. They had forgotten, in most
Cases, the specific historical geography of their {{x|Bronze Age}}

Predecessors. What they remembered was the heroic poetry—the songs
About Achilles and Odysseus and Agamemnon that had been
Transmitted orally through three or four centuries of illiterate
{{p|Dark Age}}. When Homer (whoever he was, whenever exactly he

Lived—probably the eighth century BCE, probably a single poet but
Possibly a tradition) composed the {{p|Iliad}} and the {{p|Odyssey}}, he
Was working from this oral tradition. The poems preserve fragments
Of {{p|Mycenaean}} material culture (the boar's-tusk helmet, the

Catalog of ships, certain personal names, certain geographical
References) that must have been transmitted from the {{x|Bronze Age}}
Without being archaeologically rediscovered until the nineteenth
Century. Homer knew things about the {{p|Mycenaean}} world that he

Could only have known through oral tradition stretching back
Centuries. The poetry is a time-capsule. The {{p|Mycenaean}} civilization
Lives, in attenuated and elaborated form, in the {{p|Homeric}} epics.

And this chapter, before it concludes, gestures toward one other
Thing. The {{p|Dionysiaca}}—the mythological cycle about the god
Dionysus's campaigns into {{p|India}}. In later {{p|Greek}} mythology,
Dionysus (god of wine, ecstasy, and the irrational) is said to

Have invaded {{p|India}} at the head of an army and established his
Worship there before returning to {{p|Greece}}. This myth is preserved
In Nonnus of Panopolis's late antique epic {{p|Dionysiaca}}—a
Forty-eight-book epic that is the longest surviving poem of

Classical antiquity. The story is historically fantastical. But
The {{x|Gaiad}} takes it as an eastward gesture, a pointing toward the
Connection between the {{p|Mediterranean}} and {{p|Indian}} worlds that will
Become increasingly important in later chapters. Dionysus into

{{p|India}} prefigures the {{p|Ramayana}}'s expansion westward toward
{{p|Troy}} and {{p|Egypt}}. The mythological cycles of the two civilizations,
Separated by thousands of miles, include cross-pointing gestures
That suggest deeper awareness of each other than the strict

Archaeological record demonstrates. The {{p|Mycenaean}} world, at its
Height, was in communication with {{p|Anatolia}}, {{p|Syria}}, {{p|Egypt}}, and
Probably indirectly with {{p|Mesopotamia}} and beyond. The cultural
Connections of the late {{x|Bronze Age}} were real and extensive. And

The myths preserve traces of those connections, even after the
Archaeological and documentary evidence has fragmented.

# Chapter 159: The Ramayana

And now the great swerve of the {{x|Gaiad}}. The moment where the
Narrative crosses all the rational chronologies and follows the
Thread of mythic resonance instead. The {{p|Ramayana}}.

                                                   Consider what
The {{p|Ramayana}} tells us. Rama, prince of {{p|Ayodhya}} in northern
{{p|India}}, exiled from his kingdom by the machinations of his
Stepmother, wanders in the forest with his wife Sita and his

Brother Lakshmana. Sita is abducted by Ravana, the ten-headed
Demon-king of {{p|Lanka}}. Rama assembles an army—an army of monkeys,
Led by the monkey-god Hanuman—and marches south. They build a
Bridge across the ocean. They invade {{p|Lanka}}. They defeat Ravana.

They recover Sita. They return to {{p|Ayodhya}} and Rama is restored
To his throne. The epic is one of the foundational {{p|Sanskrit}}
Texts, composed probably between the seventh and fourth centuries
BCE, though its oral roots go much deeper—perhaps to the {{x|Vedic}}

Period, perhaps to the {{x|Aryan}} migration itself. And the text is
Usually read as purely {{p|Indian}}: a story of northern {{p|India}}
Conquering southern {{p|India}}, or of {{p|Indo-Aryan}} peoples subduing
{{p|Dravidian}} peoples, or of cosmic dharma triumphing over demonic

{{x|Adharma}}. All of these readings are valid. But the {{x|Gaiad}} reads the
{{p|Ramayana}} differently. The {{x|Gaiad}} reads the {{p|Ramayana}} as evidence
Of something extraordinary: that the {{p|Indo-European}} diaspora,
Which by the second millennium BCE had spread from {{p|Ireland}} to

{{p|India}}, retained a shared narrative imagination. That the story
Of Rama and Sita and the recovery of a stolen queen from a
Fortified city across the sea—this story is not only {{p|Indian}}.
It is also, in structural terms, the story of the {{p|Trojan War}}.

Consider the parallels. A virtuous prince's wife is abducted.
The prince raises an army of allies. The army crosses the sea
To a fortified island or peninsula where the abductor rules.
The siege lasts years. Eventually the fortress falls, the wife

Is recovered, the villain is slain. In the {{x|Greek}} case: Menelaus,
Helen, Paris, {{p|Troy}}, the {{p|Achaean}} coalition. In the {{p|Indian}} case:
Rama, Sita, Ravana, {{p|Lanka}}, the monkey army. The parallels are
Not superficial. They are structural. They extend to specific

Motifs: the test of the bow (only the true husband can string it),
The divine intervention (gods meddling in mortal wars), the
Question of the wife's chastity after her return (was she violated
By the abductor?). Sita's trial by fire and Helen's ambiguous

Reception in {{p|Sparta}}—these are not random coincidences. These are
Two reflexes of a single {{p|Proto-Indo-European}} narrative, preserved
At the extreme western and eastern ends of the diaspora, recognized
For what they are only when scholars began comparing myths across

Traditions in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries CE. And the
{{x|Gaiad}} goes further. The {{x|Gaiad}} proposes that Rama is not only
A structural parallel of the {{p|Trojan War}}. Rama's expedition
Also resonates with {{p|Egyptian}} and {{p|Ethiopian}} narratives of

Righteous kingship. Rama's exile and return mirrors Osiris's
Death and resurrection. Rama's southern campaign mirrors the
{{p|Egyptian}} Kebra Nagast traditions of kingship moving southward
Into {{p|Ethiopia}}. Rama's blue skin—the iconic depiction of Rama

As dark-blue or dark-skinned—links him visually to both the
{{p|African}} and the {{p|Tamil}} traditions. In some esoteric readings,
Rama visits {{p|Ethiopia}} on his travels. In some esoteric readings,
Rama and Ravana are two aspects of a single cosmic principle.

In some esoteric readings, the {{p|Ramayana}} is not a story about
{{p|India}} at all but about the whole human civilization of the
{{x|Bronze Age}}, refracted through the {{p|Indian}} tradition into a local
And specific form. The {{x|Gaiad}} entertains these readings. The {{x|Gaiad}}

Entertains them not as claims of literal historical fact but as
Recognitions of deep mythic unity. The {{p|Ramayana}} is the {{p|Indian}}
Reflex of a narrative complex that humanity as a whole was
Generating in the late {{x|Bronze Age}}—a complex that also produced

The {{p|Iliad}}, the {{p|Egyptian}} stories of the war against {{p|Hyksos}}
And {{p|Sea Peoples}}, the {{p|Hebrew}} narrative of Moses leading his
People out of bondage, the {{p|Hittite}} accounts of the battle of
{{p|Kadesh}}. Different cultures. Different protagonists. Different

Specific contents. But a common structural feature: the
Confrontation with a powerful adversary, the long siege or long
Journey, the eventual restoration of righteous order. This is
The shared grammar of {{x|Bronze Age}} civilizational narrative.

                                                           And
Rama's dharma—the concept of righteous conduct, duty, the
Alignment of the individual with cosmic order—this dharma is
Not only the central ethical principle of {{p|Hindu}} civilization.

It is also a specific {{p|Indian}} articulation of a concept that
Appears in cognate forms across {{p|Indo-European}} traditions.
The {{p|Latin}} "religio" (binding oneself to the sacred). The
{{p|Persian}} "asha" ({{p|Zoroastrian}} cosmic truth). The {{p|Greek}} "themis"

(Divinely sanctioned moral order). The {{p|Norse}} "örlög" (fate,
Cosmic law). These are not the same concepts. But they are
Cognate concepts. They trace back to a {{p|Proto-Indo-European}}
Conception of cosmic order that each successor civilization

Elaborated in its own way. Rama is the {{p|Indian}} embodiment of
Dharma—the perfect king, the perfect husband, the perfect warrior,
Whose every action aligns with cosmic order. And as such, Rama
Is also, structurally, the {{p|Indian}} cognate of Osiris, of

Yudhishthira, of Arthur, of every righteous sovereign whose
Task is to restore the cosmos when it has been disturbed.

                                                           Let us
Slow down and narrate Rama's story proper. Rama is born to
King Dasharatha of {{p|Ayodhya}}, one of the great cities of ancient

Northern {{p|India}}, traditionally located in what is now {{p|Uttar Pradesh}}.
Dasharatha has four sons by three wives: Rama, Bharata,
Lakshmana, Shatrughna. Rama is the eldest and the rightful heir.
As a young prince, Rama is trained in warfare and statecraft.

He travels with the sage Vishwamitra to protect sacrifices from
Demons. He visits the kingdom of {{p|Mithila}} and there he meets
Sita, the daughter of King Janaka. Sita is not born of ordinary
Means; she was found in a furrow during a ritual ploughing, a

Daughter of the earth itself. King Janaka has declared that whoever
Can string the bow of Shiva may marry Sita. Rama strings the bow.
In fact, he breaks it. He and Sita are married. They return to
{{p|Ayodhya}} together. Dasharatha prepares to abdicate in favor of

Rama, but Dasharatha's second queen Kaikeyi, manipulated by her
Servant Manthara, invokes two old boons from Dasharatha: that
Her son Bharata be crowned instead, and that Rama be exiled to
The forest for fourteen years. Dasharatha is honor-bound to grant

These boons. Rama, ever dharmic, accepts his exile without protest.
Sita insists on accompanying him. Lakshmana joins them voluntarily.
The three wander the forests of central {{p|India}} for years. And then,
While Rama is distracted by a magical deer, Ravana abducts Sita

And carries her to {{p|Lanka}}. The demon-king Ravana rules from
{{p|Lanka}}—in traditional {{p|Indian}} geography identified with {{p|Sri Lanka}},
Though some scholars have proposed other locations. Ravana has ten
Heads, twenty arms, enormous magical power, and a fortified island

Capital protected by demon armies. Rama and Lakshmana search for
Sita desperately. They meet Hanuman, the monkey-general, who
Becomes Rama's most devoted ally. Hanuman is himself an avatar of
The wind-god, possessed of extraordinary strength and the ability

To change his size at will. Hanuman leaps across the ocean to
{{p|Lanka}}, finds Sita in captivity, confirms her faithfulness,
And returns to report to Rama. Now Rama raises his army: the
{{p|Vanara}} army of monkeys led by Sugriva (a monkey king whose

Throne Rama has helped restore), with Hanuman as his chief
Lieutenant. They march to the southern coast of {{p|India}}. They
Build a stone bridge across the sea to {{p|Lanka}}—the bridge of
{{p|Ram Setu}} or {{p|Adam's Bridge}}, still visible today as a chain of

Shoals in the {{p|Palk Strait}} between {{p|India}} and {{p|Sri Lanka}}. Over
This bridge the army crosses. The siege of {{p|Lanka}} begins. Great
Battles are fought. Ravana's demon generals fall one by one.
Lakshmana kills Indrajit, Ravana's warrior son. Finally Rama

Confronts Ravana in single combat. Ravana is terrible; his ten
Heads regenerate; his twenty arms wield divine weapons. But Rama
Uses the divine arrow given to him by the sage Agastya—an arrow
Enchanted with {{p|Brahma}}'s power—and pierces Ravana's heart. The

Demon-king falls. {{p|Lanka}} is conquered. Sita is recovered. The
Righteous order is restored.

                              But there is a shadow in the story.
Rama, having recovered Sita, questions her chastity. She has lived
In Ravana's palace for months. Has she been violated? She insists

She has not. Rama demands proof. Sita walks into a fire—the
{{x|Agnipariksha}}, the trial by fire. The fire-god Agni refuses to
Burn her. She emerges unharmed. Her chastity is vindicated. They
Return to {{p|Ayodhya}} and Rama is crowned king. His reign is the

{{p|Rama Rajya}}, the reign of Rama—remembered in {{p|Indian}} tradition as
The golden age of perfect just rule. But even this is not the full
Story. In later additions to the {{p|Ramayana}}, Rama, pressured by
Rumors questioning Sita's purity even after the fire, exiles her

Again. She takes refuge in the forest with the sage Valmiki
(Traditionally the author of the {{p|Ramayana}} itself), where she
Gives birth to twin sons, Kusha and Lava. Years later Rama
Encounters them and recognizes them. Sita, having demonstrated

Her purity one final time, is swallowed by the earth—returning to
The mother who bore her. Rama continues to rule until his time
Ends and he enters the {{p|Sarayu River}} and returns to his divine
Source. This is the narrative shape of the {{p|Ramayana}}. A story of

Righteous exile and restoration. A story of divine incarnation—for
Rama is identified as the seventh avatar of Vishnu, come to
Earth to destroy Ravana, who cannot be defeated by any god due
To a boon. A story with profound ambiguities around gender, purity,

Royal obligation, the tension between personal love and public
Duty. A story that, more than any other single text, has shaped
The {{p|Hindu}} moral imagination for two and a half millennia.

                                                                The
{{p|Ramayana}}'s reach is not only {{p|Indian}}. It spread across Southeast
{{p|Asia}} with the expansion of {{p|Hindu}} and {{p|Buddhist}} civilization. In

{{p|Thailand}} the epic becomes the {{p|Ramakien}}, the foundational
Narrative of {{p|Thai}} royal ideology; {{p|Thai}} kings still take the
Name "Rama" as their royal title, with the current {{p|Thai}} monarch
Being Rama X. In {{p|Indonesia}} the {{p|Ramayana}} is performed in the

Shadow puppet theater of {{p|Wayang}}. In {{p|Cambodia}} it is carved into
The walls of {{p|Angkor Wat}}. In {{p|Laos}}, {{p|Vietnam}}, {{p|Myanmar}}, {{p|Malaysia}}—
The {{p|Ramayana}} is a shared civilizational text of Southeast
{{p|Asian}} {{p|Indic}} culture. And its influence extends westward too.

In {{p|Tibet}} the {{p|Ramayana}} appears in {{p|Buddhist}} recension. In {{p|Mongolia}}.
In {{p|Central Asia}}. In {{p|Ethiopia}}—and this is where the {{x|Gaiad}}'s deepest
Claim becomes interesting—there are traditions that connect Rama
To {{p|African}} figures. Some {{p|Ethiopian}} traditions associate Rama with

Ramses (the name is cognate), and through Ramses with the lineage
Of divine pharaohs. Some traditions propose that Rama and Ravana
Are structurally related to Horus and Seth, the {{p|Egyptian}} brothers
Whose conflict structures so much of {{p|Egyptian}} theology. The

{{x|Gaiad}} does not insist on these connections. They are speculative.
They operate at the level of poetic rather than historical truth.
But they are part of the living imagination of how civilizations
Remember themselves and each other. The {{p|Ramayana}}, in this reading,

Is a window onto a moment in {{x|Bronze Age}} history when the whole
{{p|Mediterranean}} and {{p|Indian Ocean}} world was engaged in a single
Great conversation about the nature of kingship, the recovery of
Stolen queens, and the restoration of cosmic order. The {{p|Ramayana}}

Does not literally describe a campaign by Rama into {{p|Ethiopia}}
Or {{p|Egypt}} or {{p|Troy}}. But the {{p|Ramayana}} is a node in a network of
Narratives that did span that whole world, and that remembered
That whole world as sharing a common civilizational project.

                                                               And
This is the meaning of the great swerve of the {{x|Gaiad}}. The meaning
Of pausing the {{x|Bronze Age}} collapse chronology long enough to tell
The {{p|Ramayana}}. It is a reminder that humanity, even in the age

Of warring empires and isolating geographies, was already one
Humanity. The {{p|Hittites}} in {{p|Anatolia}} and the {{p|Mycenaeans}} in {{p|Greece}}
And the {{p|Aryans}} in {{p|India}} and the {{p|Egyptians}} in the {{p|Nile}} Valley—
These were not isolated civilizations. They were nodes in a

Network. Their myths resonated. Their heroes were cognate. Their
Gods were translatable. Their kings corresponded with each other
In {{p|Akkadian}} cuneiform across thousands of miles. And when the
{{x|Bronze Age}} system collapsed around twelve-hundred BCE, it was

Not the collapse of isolated local civilizations. It was the
Collapse of an integrated world. The {{p|Ramayana}} preserves, in
Literary form, the memory of that integrated world. The memory
Of a time when Rama could march an army across the sea to recover

A stolen queen, and this was the shape of heroic action that
Made sense to {{p|Indian}} and {{p|Greek}} and {{p|Hittite}} and {{p|Egyptian}} minds
Alike. One humanity. One narrative imagination. Differentiated
Into specific cultural forms, but grounded in a common grammar.

The {{p|Ramayana}} is the eastern reflection of that common grammar.
The {{p|Iliad}} is the western reflection. The {{p|Exodus}} is the southern
Reflection. The sack of {{p|Hattusa}} is the northern reflection. Four
Great narratives of {{x|Bronze Age}} crisis and transformation. Four

Local instantiations of a shared civilizational moment. The {{x|Gaiad}}
Honors this by telling them together. Not as isolated stories of
Isolated peoples, but as resonances of a single crisis in the
Integrated {{x|Bronze Age}} world.

# Chapter 161: Fall of Shang

While the {{p|Mediterranean}} collapses, in the east, another civilization
Is also transforming. The {{p|Shang Dynasty}} of {{p|China}}, which has ruled
The {{p|Yellow River}} valley for nearly six centuries, falls to a rising
Power from the west: the {{p|Zhou}}.

                                     The {{p|Shang}}, as the {{x|Gaiad}} has
Already noted, were the first historically documented {{p|Chinese}}
Dynasty. Their capital shifted several times, but the last and
Greatest was {{p|Yin}} or {{p|Anyang}}, in what is now {{p|Henan Province}}.

There the {{p|Shang}} kings ruled, divined, sacrificed, and buried their
Dead with astonishing quantities of grave goods: bronze ritual
Vessels, jade ornaments, silk, lacquerware, and—in the royal tombs—
Human sacrifices numbering in the hundreds. {{p|Shang}} society was

Stratified, ceremonial, and oriented toward an elaborate ancestor
Cult. The king's main duty was to communicate with the royal
Ancestors, who in turn communicated with the supreme deity Shang Di
(the "Lord on High"). The divination method used pyro-osteomancy:

Questions were inscribed on ox scapulae or turtle plastrons, which
Were then heated until they cracked; the cracks were read as
Responses from the ancestors. These inscriptions—the so-called
{{p|Oracle Bone Script}}—constitute the earliest written {{p|Chinese}}

Language, and they survive in the tens of thousands. They record
Royal hunts, military campaigns, ritual sacrifices, weather
Forecasts, dream interpretations. They give us intimate access to
The {{p|Shang}} mind. The {{p|Shang}} king was not quite a god but a privileged

Intermediary. The {{p|Shang}} state was organized around his ritual
Calendar. And the {{p|Shang}} military was a formidable force, operating
Chariots (introduced from the western steppe, probably via the
{{p|Indo-European}} or {{p|Tocharian}} transmission that the {{x|Gaiad}} will

Return to later), bronze weapons, and professional warriors.

                                                               By
The twelfth century BCE, however, the {{p|Shang}} had grown decadent.
The last {{p|Shang}} king, Di Xin (also known as Zhou Xin, though this

Creates confusion with the {{p|Zhou Dynasty}} name—the characters are
Different), was remembered by later tradition as a tyrant of
Almost Neronian proportions. He built a "lake of wine and forest
Of meat" for his debauches. He invented cruel punishments for his

Enemies, including the "cannon of roasting" in which victims were
Forced to walk across a greased bronze pole suspended over a pit
Of burning coals; when they inevitably fell, the tyrant and his
Favorite concubine Daji would laugh. Daji was said to be a

Fox spirit possessing a human body, and her influence on Di Xin
Was held responsible for the moral collapse of the dynasty. The
Details are almost certainly exaggerated, written by later {{p|Zhou}}
Historians to justify their conquest, but some kernel of truth

Likely survives. The {{p|Shang}} state had become oppressive. Its
Taxation and labor demands were ruinous. Its military campaigns
Had become defensive and ineffective. Its ritual legitimacy was
Crumbling. And on the western frontier, a subordinate people, the

{{p|Zhou}}, were growing in strength.

                                        The {{p|Zhou}} were originally a
Client state of the {{p|Shang}}, ruling the {{p|Wei River}} valley in what
Is now {{p|Shaanxi Province}}. They were probably ethnically distinct

From the {{p|Shang}}—some scholars have proposed that they had {{p|Qiang}}
Or {{p|Tibeto-Burman}} ancestry, or at least significant cultural ties
To western populations. But by the twelfth century BCE they had
Adopted {{p|Shang}} writing, {{p|Shang}} ritual forms, and {{p|Shang}} political

Structures, while retaining their own distinctive identity. The
{{p|Zhou}} traced their lineage to Hou Ji, the "Lord of Millet," an
Agricultural culture hero. Their leadership in the eleventh
Century was exercised first by King Wen (the "Accomplished King"),

Who spent his life building coalitions against the {{p|Shang}} without
Openly rebelling; and then by his son King Wu (the "Martial King"),
Who led the final military campaign. And they were advised by a
Figure of enduring legendary importance: Jiang Ziya, also known

As Taigong Wang or Jiang Taigong, a fisherman-sage whom King Wen
Discovered by a river and recognized as the greatest strategist
Of the age. Jiang Ziya's military treatise, the {{p|Six Secret Teachings}},
Became one of the foundational {{p|Chinese}} military classics. The

{{p|Zhou}} prepared carefully. They built alliances with disaffected
{{p|Shang}} vassals. They cultivated a reputation for virtue and
Righteousness that contrasted with Di Xin's cruelty. They waited
For the right moment.

                         That moment came around ten-forty-six BCE—
The date established by the {{p|Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project}}
Completed in two-thousand CE, using a combination of astronomical
Calculations, radiocarbon dating, and textual analysis. King Wu led

A {{p|Zhou}} coalition army to the {{p|Battle of Muye}}, just outside the
{{p|Shang}} capital at {{p|Yin}}. Here the {{p|Zhou}} forces decisively defeated
The {{p|Shang}} army—though tradition holds that many {{p|Shang}} soldiers
Defected on the battlefield, reversing their weapons and joining

The {{p|Zhou}}. Di Xin fled back to his palace, set it on fire with
Himself inside, and perished. The {{p|Shang Dynasty}} ended. King Wu
Entered {{p|Yin}}, ritually acknowledged the fallen dynasty, and
Established the {{p|Zhou Dynasty}}—which would become the longest-lasting

Dynasty in {{p|Chinese}} history, enduring in various forms until
Two-twenty-one BCE, a span of over eight centuries.

                                                     And here the
{{p|Zhou}} innovation: the {{p|Mandate of Heaven}}. The {{p|Zhou}} could not simply

Claim that they had conquered the {{p|Shang}} by military force. They
Needed a legitimating ideology that would explain why the transfer
Of power was right and just. They developed—probably articulated
Most clearly by King Wu's brother Duke of Zhou, who served as

Regent for King Wu's young son after King Wu's early death—the
Concept of {{p|Tianming}}, Heaven's charge. The high
God, Tian, was Shang Di of the {{p|Shang}}'s own sky,
One Lord, two names; the fight was for the rite:
Which house would make the offering, by right,
To Heaven. And the {{p|Shang}}'s own dead were fed:
{{p|Zhou}} gave {{p|Song}} to {{p|Shang}}'s heirs, to set the bread
Before their fathers. Heaven gives the strong
House leave to rule, but only for so long

As that house governs virtuously. If the ruling house becomes
Oppressive, tyrannical, or negligent, Heaven withdraws its mandate
And transfers it to a new, worthier house. This is what had
Happened to the {{p|Shang}}: Di Xin's corruption had forfeited the

Mandate, and Heaven had granted it to King Wen and King Wu. The
{{p|Zhou}} conquest was thus not usurpation but divinely sanctioned
Succession. The {{p|Mandate of Heaven}} became the foundational
Political theology of {{p|Chinese}} civilization. For the next three

Thousand years—through every subsequent dynasty, every rebellion,
Every civil war—{{p|Chinese}} political legitimacy was framed in terms
Of the mandate. A successful rebel had received the mandate from
A corrupt dynasty. A fallen dynasty had lost the mandate through

Misgovernance. A long dynastic reign was evidence that the mandate
Was still held. Natural disasters, military defeats, or peasant
Rebellions were evidence that it was slipping. The {{p|Mandate of Heaven}}
Is one of the most durable political concepts in human history,

Shaping {{p|East Asian}} political thought as profoundly as divine
Right of kings shaped {{p|European}} thought. And it was born in the
{{p|Zhou}} conquest of the {{p|Shang}}. A product of eleventh-century BCE
{{p|Chinese}} political theology, responding to the need to justify

Dynastic transition.

                    The {{p|Zhou}}, once established, proceeded to
Reorganize {{p|Chinese}} civilization. They developed the feudal system
Of {{p|Fengjian}}: the {{p|Zhou}} king (now styled Wang or "king," a title

Previously reserved for {{p|Shang}} monarchs) granted territories to
Royal kinsmen and allied lords, who ruled as semi-autonomous
Vassals. Each vassal owed tribute, military service, and ritual
Compliance to the {{p|Zhou}} king. Over time this system would

Fragment, producing the {{p|Spring and Autumn}} and {{p|Warring States}}
Periods—but for the first several centuries of the {{p|Zhou}}, it
Worked reasonably well. The {{p|Zhou}} also elaborated the ritual
Culture that would become the foundation of {{p|Chinese}} classical

Civilization: the {{p|Book of Rites}} ({{p|Liji}}), the {{p|Book of Songs}}
({{p|Shijing}}), the {{p|Book of Changes}} ({{p|Yijing}} or {{p|I Ching}})—all these
Canonical texts either originated in or were heavily developed
During the {{p|Zhou}} period. The {{p|Zhou}} developed the calendar. They

Standardized the writing system. They institutionalized the ritual
Calendar of sacrifices, harvest festivals, and seasonal ceremonies
That would define {{p|Chinese}} culture for millennia.

                                                       And the
{{p|Zhou}} established something that would prove even more influential:

The concept of {{p|Zhongguo}}, the "{{p|Middle Kingdom}}" or "Central
States." The {{p|Zhou}} thought of their territory as the civilizational
Center of the world, with progressively less civilized peoples
Radiating outward—the {{p|Rong}}, {{p|Di}}, {{p|Yi}}, {{p|Man}}, the so-called "four

Barbarians" of the four directions. This ethnocentric cosmology
Would shape {{p|Chinese}} self-understanding ever after. {{p|China}} conceived
Of itself not as one nation among many but as the civilizational
Center from which civilization radiated. Other peoples could

Adopt {{p|Chinese}} culture and become civilized, or reject it and
Remain barbarian, but {{p|China}} itself was the reference point. This
Is a very different ideology from the {{p|Mediterranean}} or {{p|Indian}}
Traditions, and it has shaped {{p|East Asian}} geopolitics for three

Thousand years. The {{p|Zhou}} articulated it first. Their conquest
Of the {{p|Shang}} was not just a dynastic change. It was the creation
Of a political and cultural framework that would define {{p|China}}.

And consider the timing. The {{p|Shang}} fell around ten-forty-six BCE.

The {{p|Trojan War}} ended around twelve-hundred BCE. The {{p|Bronze Age Collapse}}
Devastated the {{p|Mediterranean}} between twelve-twenty-five and
Eleven-seventy-five BCE. The {{p|Exodus}} (in its thirteenth-century BCE
Dating) occurred during Ramesses II's or Merneptah's reign. These

Events are not simultaneous, but they are broadly contemporary.
Across {{p|Eurasia}}—from the {{p|Aegean}} to the {{p|Yellow River}}—the late
{{x|Bronze Age}} was ending, and new political and religious forms were
Emerging in its wake. The {{p|Zhou Dynasty}} is the {{p|East Asian}} equivalent

Of the post-collapse reorganizations happening in the {{p|Mediterranean}}.
Its political theology—the {{p|Mandate of Heaven}}—is the {{p|East Asian}}
Equivalent of the new theologies being worked out in {{p|Israel}},
{{p|Greece}}, and the {{p|Near East}}. The same underlying shift: from

Archaic god-king theocracies toward systems grounded in virtue,
Cosmic order, and the contingent legitimacy of rulers.

                                                         The {{p|Zhou}}
Dynasty's early centuries—the {{p|Western Zhou}} period, from ten-forty-six

BCE to seven-seventy-one BCE—were the classical age of {{p|Chinese}}
Culture. The Duke of Zhou was remembered as a paragon of
Statesmanship; Confucius, writing five centuries later, would
Take the Duke of Zhou as his personal hero, the model of the

Virtuous official who serves his lord without seeking power for
Himself. The {{p|Western Zhou}} ended when the capital was sacked by
Western barbarians (the {{p|Quanrong}}) in seven-seventy-one BCE,
Forcing a relocation eastward to {{p|Luoyang}}. This begins the

{{p|Eastern Zhou}} period, which divides into the {{p|Spring and Autumn}}
Period (ending around four-eighty BCE) and the {{p|Warring States}}
Period (four-seventy-five to two-twenty-one BCE). During these
Later {{p|Zhou}} centuries the central authority weakened and the

Feudal vassals became de facto independent kingdoms. But the {{p|Zhou}}
Ritual order, the classical texts, the {{p|Mandate of Heaven}} theology,
And the sense of {{p|China}} as a civilizational unity—all these
Persisted and would provide the foundation for the eventual

Reunification under {{p|Qin}} and {{p|Han}}.

                                                 But here, at the
Fall of the {{p|Shang}}, we are at the beginning of all that. Di Xin
In flames in his palace. King Wu entering {{p|Yin}}. The Duke of Zhou

Drafting the political theology. The eight-hundred-year dynasty
Beginning. The great {{p|Chinese}} classical age being born in the
Moment when the {{x|Bronze Age}} died.

                                  {{p|Shang}}. {{p|Zhou}}. {{p|Muye}}. {{p|Mandate of Heaven}}.
Di Xin. Daji. King Wen. King Wu. Duke of Zhou. Jiang Ziya. The
Fox spirit in the palace and the sage fisherman by the river.
The tyrant's cruelty and the virtuous rebel's restraint. The

Lost mandate and the new dynasty. The {{p|Middle Kingdom}} defining
Itself as center of the world. The eight-hundred-year reign.

Fall of {{p|Shang}}. Rise of {{p|Zhou}}. {{p|China}}'s {{x|Bronze Age}} ending and its

# Chapter 162: The Bantu Expansion

And now, while the {{p|Mediterranean}} shatters and {{p|China}} is reborn,
We must turn south. Because something vast is happening on the
{{p|African}} continent. A population expansion that, in sheer scope
And demographic impact, will equal any of the movements the {{x|Gaiad}}

Has described—including the {{p|Yamnaya}} expansion, the {{p|Austronesian}}
Migrations, the {{p|Aryan}} movements into {{p|India}}. But unlike those,
The {{p|Bantu}} expansion takes place almost entirely within a single
Continent, and reshapes that continent so thoroughly that the

Continent's demographic map becomes the map of {{p|Bantu}} speakers.

                                                                  The
{{p|Bantu}} peoples originated in what is now the border region between
{{p|Cameroon}} and {{p|Nigeria}}, in {{p|West Africa}}. They spoke a language

Belonging to the {{p|Niger-Congo}} family, specifically the {{p|Bantu}}
Branch. They carried primarily the E1b1a (now called E-V38)
{{p|Y-chromosome}} haplogroup—a sub-clade of the E haplogroup that
The {{x|Gaiad}} has already described as the {{p|African}} branch that

Stayed on the continent while its sister clades migrated out. And
Around three thousand BCE—earlier than the event the {{x|Gaiad}} is
About to narrate but laying the necessary foundation—the early
{{p|Bantu}} speakers developed agriculture: {{p|West African}} yam cultivation

Together with oil palm and other local crops. They developed
Pottery. And they gradually acquired, through trade and diffusion
From the north, some of the technologies of {{p|Sahelian}} and {{p|North African}}
Civilization, including eventually iron smelting. Iron smelting

Reached the {{p|Bantu}} region between approximately fifteen-hundred
And one-thousand BCE—a transformation roughly contemporary with
The iron-age transition that was happening in the {{p|Mediterranean}}
And the {{p|Near East}} after the {{p|Bronze Age Collapse}}. With iron tools,

{{p|Bantu}} farmers could clear forest more effectively than before.
With iron weapons, they could defend themselves against rivals
And raid for resources. And with iron, combined with their
Established agricultural package, they had the technological

Preconditions for rapid expansion.

                                     And they expanded. Around
Fifteen-hundred BCE, or possibly earlier, some {{p|Bantu}} groups
Began moving eastward and southward out of their homeland. The

Eastward migration took them along the southern edge of the
{{p|Congo}} rainforest, through what is now the {{p|Central African Republic}}
And {{p|South Sudan}}, into the {{p|Great Lakes}} region of {{p|East Africa}}—
{{p|Lake Victoria}}, {{p|Lake Tanganyika}}, {{p|Lake Malawi}}. There they

Encountered {{p|Afro-Asiatic}}-speaking peoples, {{p|Nilo-Saharan}}-speaking
Peoples, and {{p|Khoisan}}-speaking peoples. They interacted with all
These groups: sometimes peacefully, through trade, intermarriage,
And cultural exchange; sometimes violently, through warfare and

Displacement; and most importantly, through gradual demographic
Replacement, as their agricultural productivity supported much
Larger populations than the hunter-gatherer or pastoralist
Economies they encountered. The {{p|Bantu}} did not necessarily outfight

Other peoples. They outgrew them. Their farming villages produced
Many more children than the {{p|Khoisan}} foraging bands that had
Previously occupied the land. Over generations, the {{p|Bantu}}
Population density rose, while the prior populations either

Assimilated into {{p|Bantu}} farming communities, withdrew to marginal
Lands (forests, deserts, mountains) where farming was less
Practical, or disappeared. The demographic transition was gradual
But inexorable. And it continued for over two thousand years.

The southward migration took the {{p|Bantu}} through the {{p|Congo}} basin
(Either skirting it through the savanna or gradually penetrating
Its river valleys) and into southern {{p|Africa}}—what is now {{p|Angola}},
{{p|Namibia}}, {{p|Zambia}}, {{p|Zimbabwe}}, {{p|Botswana}}, {{p|Mozambique}}, and the

Coastal regions of what is now {{p|South Africa}}. By the beginning of
The common era, {{p|Bantu}}-speaking peoples had reached the {{p|Limpopo}}
River. By three-hundred to five-hundred CE, they had crossed the
{{p|Limpopo}} and were settling in what is now northeastern {{p|South Africa}}.

By one-thousand CE, they had reached the {{p|Great Fish River}} in
Eastern {{p|Cape}}. The total migration, from its origin in the
{{p|Nigerian-Cameroonian}} border region to its southernmost extent,
Covered over three thousand miles and took approximately two

And a half millennia. It is one of the longest sustained population
Movements in human history.

                               The demographic outcome is
Staggering. Today there are approximately four hundred million

{{p|Bantu}}-speaking people, comprising several hundred distinct
Languages including {{p|Swahili}}, {{p|Zulu}}, {{p|Xhosa}}, {{p|Shona}}, {{p|Lingala}},
{{p|Kikuyu}}, {{p|Ganda}}, {{p|Rwanda}}, {{p|Kirundi}}, {{p|Luba}}, {{p|Kikongo}}, {{p|Setswana}},
{{p|Sesotho}}, {{p|Chichewa}}, and hundreds more. They occupy approximately

The southern half of the {{p|African}} continent—everything south of
A line running roughly from {{p|Cameroon}} through {{p|Kenya}}—with the
Exception of a few pockets where pre-{{p|Bantu}} languages survive:
The {{p|Khoisan}} languages of the {{p|Kalahari}} and surrounding areas;

The {{p|Cushitic}} and {{p|Nilotic}} languages of the eastern {{p|Rift Valley}};
The {{p|Pygmy}} populations of the {{p|Congo}} rainforest (who themselves
Have largely adopted {{p|Bantu}} languages while retaining distinct
Ancestry and some cultural elements). The demographic and

Linguistic unity of sub-Saharan {{p|Africa}} from {{p|Kenya}} to {{p|Cape Town}}
Is, essentially, the unity of the {{p|Bantu}} expansion. It is one
Of the defining features of the modern {{p|African}} continent. And
It was created by this long, slow, multi-millennial demographic

Movement, driven not by conquering armies but by farming villages
Gradually spreading into lands that could be farmed.

                                                       Genetically,
The {{p|Bantu}} expansion is visible. Modern sub-Saharan {{p|Africans}}

South of the equator carry predominantly E1b1a-V38 or related
E sub-clades on their {{p|Y-chromosome}}. Their maternal lineages
Show some regional diversity—the original {{p|Bantu}} homeland's
Haplogroups are mixed with local haplogroups from wherever the

{{p|Bantu}} settled—but the paternal lineages show striking uniformity
Consistent with the pattern of {{p|Bantu}} men taking local wives as
They expanded. This is a common feature of agricultural expansions:
The men move in force, displace or absorb the men of the prior

Populations, and take local women as wives. The resulting
Population retains the invading male lineages but has mixed
Female lineages. The same pattern is visible in the {{p|Yamnaya}}
Expansion into {{p|Europe}} and the {{p|Aryan}} expansion into {{p|India}}. It

Is the demographic signature of agricultural male-dominant
Expansion, and it characterizes the {{p|Bantu}} spread.

                                                       But what
Makes the {{p|Bantu}} expansion distinctive is that it did not destroy

The prior populations so completely. The {{p|Khoisan}} peoples still
Exist. The {{p|Pygmy}} peoples still exist. The {{p|Cushitic}} and {{p|Nilotic}}
Peoples still exist. {{p|Africa}} did not become a monoculture. It
Became a {{p|Bantu}} majority with significant and culturally vibrant

Minorities. The {{p|Khoisan}} of the {{p|Kalahari}} preserve {{p|Africa}}'s
Oldest linguistic lineages, including the click consonants that
Some scholars believe may descend from the very first human
Languages. The {{p|Pygmies}} of the {{p|Ituri}} and {{p|Congo}} forests preserve

The foraging traditions of {{p|Central African}} hunter-gatherers that
Predate the {{p|Bantu}} arrival by tens of thousands of years. The
{{p|Nilotic}} peoples of the {{p|Sudan}} and {{p|East African}} {{p|Rift Valley}}
Preserve pastoralist traditions linked to the cattle complex of

The eastern savanna. And among the {{p|Bantu}} themselves, there are
Profound cultural and historical differences between, say, the
{{p|Kikuyu}} of {{p|Kenya}} and the {{p|Zulu}} of {{p|South Africa}}, or between the
{{p|Kongo}} peoples of the {{p|Congo}} basin and the {{p|Tswana}} of the

{{p|Kalahari}} fringe. The {{p|Bantu}} expansion unified sub-Saharan {{p|Africa}}
Linguistically but did not homogenize it culturally. Each regional
{{p|Bantu}} population developed its own particular traditions, its
Own political systems, its own religious cosmologies, its own

Economic specializations. By the turn of the common era, the
{{p|Bantu}} world was a patchwork of kingdoms, chiefdoms, and village
Communities ranging from dense agricultural states in the {{p|Great Lakes}}
Region to semi-nomadic cattle-herders in the savanna to forest

Villages in the {{p|Congo}}. The largest and most complex of these
{{p|Bantu}} polities would emerge in later centuries: the {{p|Kingdom of Kongo}},
The {{p|Luba}} and {{p|Lunda}} empires of {{p|Central Africa}}, {{p|Great Zimbabwe}},
The {{p|Mutapa Empire}}, the {{p|Buganda Kingdom}}, the {{p|Zulu Kingdom}}. Each

Would be a product of local {{p|Bantu}} development working with
Particular ecological and political circumstances. But all would
Be rooted in the linguistic and cultural framework established
By the long {{p|Bantu}} expansion.

                                   Consider also the economic and
Technological dimensions. The {{p|Bantu}} carried with them a specific
Agricultural package: yams, oil palm, pearl millet, sorghum, some
Varieties of rice and beans. As they encountered new environments,

They adopted new crops: cattle and small livestock in the {{p|East African}}
Highlands; plantains and bananas (which arrived in {{p|Africa}} from
{{p|Southeast Asia}} via {{p|Madagascar}} and the {{p|Indian Ocean}} trade and
Were transmitted westward into the {{p|Great Lakes}} region by around

One-thousand BCE, just in time to supercharge the {{p|Bantu}} expansion);
Sweet potatoes and other {{p|New World}} crops after the {{p|Columbian}}
Exchange. The {{p|Bantu}} agricultural system was adaptive and
Incorporative. Each new crop that became available was integrated

Into the system, supporting still larger populations. Bananas in
Particular were revolutionary: they could grow in the humid
{{p|Great Lakes}} region where grains struggled, and they produced
Enormous caloric yields per unit of labor. By the time the

{{p|Bantu}} expansion was completing its southward spread, banana
Cultivation was driving a second wave of population growth in
The {{p|Great Lakes}} region and {{p|East African}} highlands—producing
Some of the densest rural populations anywhere in {{p|pre-colonial Africa}}.

Iron technology was also central. The {{p|Bantu}} developed independent
Iron-smelting traditions that produced large quantities of iron
Tools and weapons. {{p|African}} iron production in the first millennium
CE was sophisticated and large-scale, using natural-draft furnaces

That achieved high temperatures with minimal fuel—a design that
In some respects was more efficient than contemporary {{p|European}}
Iron-smelting technology. {{p|Bantu}} smiths were often regarded as
Ritual specialists as well as artisans, and in many societies

They formed hereditary castes with specialized religious functions.
The smith's transformation of ore into metal was understood as
A powerful, quasi-magical act, demanding ritual preparation and
Giving the smith special spiritual authority. This is a theme that

Appears across many {{p|African}} societies and that connects to
Deeper religious conceptions of transformation and creation.

                                                               And
Consider the religious and cultural patrimony the {{p|Bantu}} carried.

{{p|Bantu}} religious traditions share certain common features across
Their vast geographic spread: a high creator god who is often
Remote from daily human affairs; ancestor veneration, with the
Recently deceased remaining present as spiritual forces influencing

The living; nature spirits inhabiting significant features of the
Landscape—particular trees, hills, rivers, caves; specialists in
Communication with the spiritual world (diviners, healers, rain-
Makers); an ethic of community, hospitality, and social harmony

Summed up in the {{p|Zulu}} and {{p|Xhosa}} concept of {{p|ubuntu}} ("I am because
We are"). These shared features do not mean {{p|Bantu}} religions are
Uniform—they are diverse and locally elaborated—but they share
Deep structural affinities inherited from the original {{p|Bantu}}

Homeland and carried southward and eastward during the expansion.
In this they parallel the shared structural features of other
Great language-family expansions: {{p|Indo-European}}, {{p|Austronesian}},
{{p|Semitic}}. A shared linguistic origin tends to preserve a shared

Religious and cultural substratum, even as specific traditions
Develop in locally distinctive ways.

                                        The {{p|Bantu}} expansion is
One of the great movements of human history. It is the story of

How sub-Saharan {{p|Africa}} below the equator became linguistically
And demographically unified. It is the story of how agriculture
And iron transformed a continent. And it is the story of how a
Particular people—originating in a small region of {{p|West Africa}}—

Became, through slow demographic growth and gradual geographic
Spread, one of the world's largest ethnolinguistic families. The
{{x|Gaiad}} has tended, in its narration of this vast process, to
Emphasize demographic and linguistic continuity. But we must also

Note that the {{p|Bantu}} expansion was not peaceful. It involved
Displacement, warfare, and occasional atrocity. The {{p|Khoisan}} and
Other pre-{{p|Bantu}} peoples who were pushed to the margins of their
Former territories did not go willingly. Some were killed; some

Were enslaved; some were assimilated under duress. The {{p|Bantu}}
Expansion was gentler than many conquest migrations in world
History (the {{p|Mongol}} expansion, the {{p|Indo-European}} expansion
Into {{p|Europe}}, the {{p|European}} colonization of the {{p|Americas}}), but it

Was not bloodless. It was a real historical movement with real
Human costs. The {{p|Khoisan}} of the {{p|Kalahari}}, the {{p|Pygmies}} of the
{{p|Ituri}}, the remnant {{p|Cushitic}} populations of {{p|East Africa}}—these
Peoples live in the ecological margins where {{p|Bantu}} farming

Could not displace them. Their survival on those margins is itself
A testimony both to their resilience and to the completeness of
The {{p|Bantu}} occupation of everywhere more agriculturally desirable.

The {{x|Gaiad}} honors the {{p|Bantu}} expansion. The {{x|Gaiad}} honors also the

Pre-{{p|Bantu}} peoples who were displaced or assimilated in its course.
The {{x|Gaiad}} honors the hybrid populations that resulted. The {{x|Gaiad}}
Honors the {{p|Pygmies}} who retained their identity in the depths of
The {{p|Ituri}}. The {{x|Gaiad}} honors the {{p|Khoisan}} who retained theirs in

The depths of the {{p|Kalahari}}. The {{x|Gaiad}} honors the {{p|Nilotic}} and
{{p|Cushitic}} peoples who retained theirs along the {{p|Rift Valley}} and
The {{p|Horn of Africa}}. All of these peoples, together, constitute
The anthropological mosaic of {{p|Africa}}. The {{p|Bantu}} are the largest

Component but not the only component. Sub-Saharan {{p|Africa}} is
More diverse than its {{p|Bantu}} majority might suggest. And the
{{x|Gaiad}} reads the {{p|Bantu}} expansion as one of the most important
Processes of the last three thousand years—because it laid the

Demographic foundation of the modern {{p|African}} continent.

E1b1a. {{p|West Africa}}. {{p|Great Lakes}}. {{p|Congo}} basin. {{p|Kalahari}} and {{p|Cape}}.
{{p|Swahili}}, {{p|Zulu}}, {{p|Xhosa}}, {{p|Shona}}, {{p|Kikuyu}}, {{p|Ganda}}. Iron smelting

And banana cultivation. Three thousand miles and two thousand
Years. Four hundred million speakers today. The demographic

# Chapter 163: Zarathustra

On the {{p|Iranian}} plateau, between the {{p|Caspian Sea}} and the {{p|Hindu Kush}},
A prophet appeared who would reshape world religion.

                                                     Zarathustra.
Or, in {{p|Greek}} transliteration, Zoroaster. An {{p|Indo-European}} speaker

Of the {{p|Iranian}} branch. A man of the R1a haplogroup lineage that
Had carried {{p|Indo-Aryan}} peoples onto the {{p|Iranian}} plateau and into
The {{p|Indus Valley}}. A priest in the tradition of the {{p|Indo-Iranian}}
Fire-sacrifice, the same tradition that in {{p|India}} produced the

{{p|Vedic}} religion. And a figure so foundational to {{p|Iranian}} religion,
To {{p|Iranian}} philosophy, and to subsequent {{p|Western}} religious thought
That his actual historical location in time is difficult to pin
Down—estimates range from fifteenth century BCE (placing him near

The {{p|Indo-Aryan}} migration) to sixth century BCE (placing him near
The rise of the {{p|Achaemenid}} Empire). Most modern scholarship
Settles on some point between twelve-hundred and one-thousand BCE—
That is, roughly contemporary with the {{p|Bronze Age Collapse}} in the

{{p|Mediterranean}} and the {{p|Zhou}} conquest in {{p|China}}. The {{x|Gaiad}} adopts
This dating. Zarathustra is another node in the widespread
Religious transformation that followed the collapse of the Bronze
Age order. His reform of {{p|Indo-Iranian}} religion is part of the

Broader Axial shift that would also produce, in their own times
And places, the {{p|Hebrew}} prophets, the {{p|Greek}} philosophers, the
{{p|Upanishads}} of {{p|India}}, Confucius and Laozi in {{p|China}}, and
Buddha and Mahavira in the {{p|Gangetic}} plain. Zarathustra is the

Earliest named prophet of this transformation. He is the first to
Speak, in the surviving record, with the voice of a new religious
Vision.

            And now the {{x|Gaiad}} must say something carefully. A
Common misconception treats {{p|Zoroastrianism}} as the original

Monotheism—the religion that invented the idea of a single
Supreme god and from which {{p|Judaism}}, {{p|Christianity}}, and {{p|Islam}}
Ultimately derive their monotheism. This account is partially true
But oversimplified. The {{x|Gaiad}} has already advanced, in its earlier

Treatment of comparative religious history, a more nuanced view:
That original {{p|Zoroastrianism}} was not strictly monotheistic in
The modern sense. It was dualistic. Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord,
Was the supreme good deity, creator of truth and light and order.

But opposed to him was Angra Mainyu (later Ahriman), the
Destructive Spirit, who was a co-eternal principle of evil, lies,
Darkness, and disorder. The cosmos was not the creation of a
Single all-powerful god. It was the battleground between two

Cosmic principles. The good principle, Ahura Mazda, would
Eventually prevail, but only through the collaborative effort of
Humans aligning themselves with his side of the cosmic conflict.
This is dualism, not monotheism. And it is dualism of a specific

Type: ethical dualism, in which the fundamental cosmic opposition
Is between good and evil, truth and lie, order and chaos. The
Gods of prior traditions had been morally mixed—capable of great
Good and great evil, subject to passions and whims. Zarathustra's

Theological innovation was to purify the supreme god of moral
Ambiguity. Ahura Mazda is unambiguously good. The moral
Ambiguity that exists in the world does not come from him; it
Comes from the opposing principle. This ethical sharpening—this

Assignment of cosmic meaning to the moral structure of existence—
Was Zarathustra's great contribution. It would profoundly
Influence all subsequent {{p|Near Eastern}} and {{p|European}} religion,
Even when the dualism was later flattened into strict monotheism.

The {{x|Gaiad}} has already discussed how later {{p|Zoroastrianism}},
Particularly under the {{p|Sassanid}} empire, became more explicitly
Monotheistic, with Angra Mainyu increasingly described as a
Creature rather than a co-eternal principle. And the {{x|Gaiad}} has

Discussed how ancient {{p|Persian}} religious concepts shaped the
{{p|Jewish}} captivity experience in {{p|Babylon}} and the subsequent
Development of {{p|Second Temple}} {{p|Judaism}}. Here we focus on
Zarathustra himself—the reformer, the prophet, the voice speaking

From the {{p|Iranian}} plateau.

                                  What did Zarathustra teach?
The {{p|Gathas}}, the oldest {{p|Zoroastrian}} texts and the ones most
Likely to preserve his actual teachings, are seventeen hymns in

An archaic form of {{p|Avestan}}—a language closely related to early
{{p|Vedic}} {{p|Sanskrit}}. They are attributed directly to Zarathustra.
They are dense, difficult, ecstatic. And they articulate several
Fundamental ideas: That the cosmos is a moral battleground. That

Every human must choose, in every thought, word, and deed, between
Asha (truth, order, righteousness) and Druj (lie, disorder,
Wickedness). That the choice is real and consequential. That
Ahura Mazda is the supreme reality, worthy of worship, creator

Of all good things. That at the end of time, Ahura Mazda will
Triumph, evil will be eliminated, and a final judgment will
Distinguish the righteous from the wicked. That the righteous
Will be rewarded in a paradise; the wicked will suffer in a state

Of torment; and ultimately, after cosmic purification, all will
Be restored in a renewed creation. These ideas—final judgment,
Afterlife reward and punishment, cosmic restoration—were
Theologically novel in the {{p|Indo-Iranian}} world. They would

Migrate, centuries later, into {{p|Jewish}} apocalyptic literature,
Into {{p|Christian}} eschatology, into {{p|Islamic}} doctrine. The Last
Judgment, heaven and hell, the coming of a savior—these
Conceptions all have clear {{p|Zoroastrian}} antecedents. Whether

Zarathustra originated them wholly or synthesized them from
Earlier {{p|Iranian}} folk beliefs is debated. But he was the first
Known figure to articulate them in a systematic theology.

                                                                He
Also reformed ritual. The old {{p|Indo-Iranian}} religion, which the
{{x|Gaiad}} saw developing on the steppe and arriving in {{p|India}} with
The {{p|Aryans}}, centered on elaborate animal sacrifices conducted
By a hereditary priestly caste. Zarathustra reduced the emphasis

On animal sacrifice. He emphasized instead the sacred fire—
Maintained continuously in fire temples, symbolic of Ahura Mazda's
Presence and of the principle of Asha in the world. He emphasized
Good thoughts, good words, good deeds as the core of religious

Practice. The steppe's old gods kept their old form:
Mithra, Anahita, the sun and storm
Bowed to Ahura Mazda or became
The {{p|Amesha Spentas}}, holy, the same.
And Indra, whom the {{p|Vedic}} hymns put high,
Was called a daeva, servant of the lie.
So {{p|Sanskrit}} deva names a god of light;
In {{p|Avestan}}, daeva, a foe. The fight
Was not between two faiths. It was one rite,
One fire, one tongue, one pressed and holy drink,
Two priesthoods at one altar, and the brink
They fought on was whose lords would take the bowl.
One family split its fire, but not its soul:
The plateau's peoples, who took up his word,
Kept Zarathustra's fire; and those who heard
Refused, in the {{p|Indo-Aryan}} east,
Kept deva for their gods, and fed the feast
Of {{p|Vedic}} rite. Each kept the same old flame
And called the other's lords by a worse name.

And
Zarathustra was, by his own account and by subsequent tradition,
A controversial reformer. He was initially rejected by his own

Community. His teachings were unwelcome. He traveled, seeking a
Patron who would hear him. He found one in King Vishtaspa—a
King probably of eastern {{p|Iran}}, possibly in what is now {{p|Afghanistan}}
Or {{p|Turkmenistan}}. Vishtaspa accepted Zarathustra's message. His

Court converted. {{p|Zoroastrianism}} spread from Vishtaspa's
Kingdom outward across the {{p|Iranian}} plateau over the following
Centuries. By the time the {{p|Medes}} and {{p|Persians}} began building
Their great empires in the first millennium BCE, {{p|Zoroastrianism}}

Had become the dominant religion of the {{p|Iranian}} world. Cyrus
The Great, Darius I, Xerxes, Artaxerxes—the {{p|Achaemenid}} kings
Of {{p|Persia}}—were all {{p|Zoroastrian}}, or at least enacted their
Kingship in {{p|Zoroastrian}} terms. Their inscriptions invoke

Ahura Mazda and pledge to uphold Asha and defeat Druj.
When the {{p|Achaemenid}} empire became the largest the world had
Yet seen—stretching from the {{p|Indus}} to the {{p|Aegean}} to {{p|Egypt}}—
{{p|Zoroastrianism}} became the religion of a vast multicultural imperial

Structure. It was the first world religion in the political sense,
The first ideology that commanded an empire spanning multiple
Languages, cultures, and ecological zones.

                                             And the {{p|Persian}}
Empire's encounter with {{p|Judea}} would prove theologically decisive.

When Cyrus conquered {{p|Babylon}} in five-thirty-nine BCE and found
The {{p|Jewish}} exiles there, he issued his famous decree permitting
Them to return to {{p|Jerusalem}} and rebuild their temple. He did
This, in {{p|Persian}} understanding, because it was Ahura Mazda's

Will that subject peoples be restored to their proper homelands
And worship. Cyrus styled himself the liberator of the {{p|Jews}},
And the {{p|Jewish}} prophet Isaiah—or the anonymous author of
{{p|Deutero-Isaiah}}—went so far as to call Cyrus Yahweh's

"Anointed one" ({{p|mashiach}}), a {{p|messianic}} title usually reserved
For legitimate {{p|Israelite}} kings. This is an astonishing theological
Generalization: a foreign {{p|Zoroastrian}} king given a {{p|Hebrew}}
{{p|messianic}} title by a {{p|Jewish}} prophet. It reflects the degree to

Which {{p|Zoroastrian}} and early post-exilic {{p|Jewish}} thought had
Converged in their understanding of cosmic morality, divine
Sovereignty, and the role of human agents in historical
Redemption. The {{p|Jews}} who returned from {{p|Babylon}} brought back

With them elements of {{p|Persian}} religious vocabulary and
Conceptual structure. Angels (a {{p|Persian}} innovation, previously
Absent from {{p|Hebrew}} tradition) began to populate {{p|Jewish}} scripture.
The figure of Satan, who in earlier {{p|Hebrew}} texts was a legitimate

Member of Yahweh's divine council, became reinterpreted along
Angra Mainyu lines as a cosmic adversary. The concept of a
Final judgment, resurrection of the dead, and {{p|messianic}} age was
Absorbed into {{p|Jewish}} apocalyptic thought. By the time of the

{{p|Second Temple}} period and the emergence of {{p|Christianity}} and
{{p|Rabbinic Judaism}}, the {{p|Zoroastrian}} imprint on {{p|Jewish}} religious
Imagination was thorough. {{p|Christianity}} and {{p|Islam}}, inheriting
From {{p|Judaism}}, inherited also from {{p|Zoroastrianism}}. The {{p|Western}}

Monotheistic tradition as we know it is, in substantial measure,
A {{p|Zoroastrian}}-{{p|Jewish}} synthesis. Without Zarathustra, the
Religious history of the {{p|West}} would be unrecognizable.

                                                             And
Yet {{p|Zoroastrianism}} itself would, over time, retreat. The
{{p|Achaemenid}} empire fell to Alexander in the fourth century BCE.
The {{p|Seleucid}} and then {{p|Parthian}} successors continued {{p|Zoroastrianism}}
But in attenuated forms. The {{p|Sassanid}} empire restored {{p|Zoroastrianism}}

As the state religion and systematized its doctrines and texts
In the third and fourth centuries CE, codifying the {{p|Avesta}}. But
The {{p|Sassanid}} empire itself fell to the {{p|Arab}} {{p|Muslim}} conquest
In the seventh century CE. {{p|Iran}} was gradually {{p|Islamized}}.

{{p|Zoroastrians}} became a minority; many emigrated to {{p|India}}, where
Their descendants, the {{p|Parsis}}, still maintain the religion.
Others remained in {{p|Iran}} as a small, tolerated but marginalized
Community. Today there are perhaps two hundred thousand {{p|Zoroastrians}}

Worldwide—a tiny remnant of what had once been the state religion
Of the greatest empire of antiquity. But {{p|Zoroastrianism}}'s
Theological influence vastly exceeds its demographic footprint.
Every {{p|Christian}}, {{p|Muslim}}, {{p|Jewish}}, or secular {{p|Western}} thinker

Who believes in the ultimate triumph of good over evil, in the
Moral structure of the cosmos, in the final judgment of souls, in
Heaven and hell—every such thinker is, whether they know it or
Not, a theological descendant of Zarathustra. The prophet from

The {{p|Iranian}} plateau is a silent presence in half the religious
Imagination of the modern world.

                                     The {{x|Gaiad}} honors him. The
{{x|Gaiad}} recognizes in Zarathustra's work a genuine theological

Innovation—the sharpening of moral dualism into an explicit
Cosmic principle, the linking of individual ethical choice to
Cosmic outcome, the articulation of a purposeful universe
Moving toward eschatological fulfillment. These are not small

Contributions. They are among the most influential religious
Ideas ever formulated. And they were formulated in obscurity,
By a prophet who had to flee his homeland to find a patron, whose
Teachings spread slowly and whose name would be forgotten outside

{{p|Iran}} for centuries, until {{p|Greek}} writers—Herodotus, Plutarch,
Later the {{p|Hellenistic}} traditions attributing to Zoroaster
Every kind of ancient wisdom—brought his name into {{p|Western}}
Consciousness. In our own time Zarathustra has had a strange

Second life: as the protagonist of Nietzsche's {{p|Thus Spoke Zarathustra}},
Where he becomes the mouthpiece for a nineteenth-century {{p|European}}
Critique of {{p|Christian}} morality. This usage is idiosyncratic, but
It reflects how Zarathustra's name continues to carry weight as

A symbol of religious innovation and prophetic voice. Nietzsche
Chose the name not randomly but because it stood for the first
Great religious reformer whose work shaped all subsequent {{p|Western}}
Religion. Nietzsche wanted to undo that work and return to a

Pre-Zarathustran valuation. Whether one agrees with Nietzsche's
Project or not, the acknowledgment of Zarathustra's foundational
Role is accurate. Zarathustra is where a great deal of the
Modern {{p|Western}} religious and ethical imagination actually begins.

                                                                     Zarathustra.
Zoroaster. Ahura Mazda and Angra Mainyu. Asha and Druj. The fire
Temples and the ethical dualism. The purification of the supreme
God. Good thoughts, good words, good deeds. The final judgment

And the renewal of the cosmos. The {{p|Gathas}}. King Vishtaspa's
Conversion. The {{p|Achaemenid}} adoption. Cyrus as Yahweh's anointed.
The transmission into {{p|Jewish}} apocalyptic and {{p|Christian}} eschatology.
The {{p|Parsis}} in {{p|India}}. The long survival of a small religion

With enormous theological consequences.

                                           Zarathustra. The first
Prophet of the {{x|Axial Age}}. The reformer of {{p|Indo-Iranian}} religion.
The invisible ancestor of {{p|Western}} monotheism.


# Chapter 164: David

And back to the {{p|Levant}}. Back to {{p|Israel}}, newly emerged from the
{{x|Bronze Age Collapse}}, settling in the highlands of {{p|Canaan}} and
Struggling to establish itself as a coherent nation among hostile
Neighbors.

             The biblical narrative presents the early history of
{{p|Israel}} as a sequence: Joshua's conquest, the period of the
Judges, the kingship of Saul, the kingship of David, the kingship
Of Solomon, and then the division of the kingdom into {{p|Israel}}

(North) and {{p|Judah}} (South). The historical accuracy of this
Narrative is much debated. What archaeologists find in the {{p|Canaanite}}
Highlands during the relevant period (roughly twelve-hundred to
One-thousand BCE) is a proliferation of small agricultural villages

With distinctive architectural and dietary markers—four-room
Houses, collar-rim storage jars, an absence of pig bones. These
Are taken as archaeological indicators of the emerging {{p|Israelite}}
Ethnic identity. The villages are numerous but small; there are

No great palaces, no monumental architecture, no clear signs of
Centralized authority. This fits an early period of tribal
Confederation—the period of the Judges—better than it fits a
Highly developed monarchy. Then, around the tenth century BCE,

There are signs of increased political organization: larger sites,
Some administrative architecture, the beginning of a state
Apparatus. This is the period that the biblical text associates
With Saul, David, and Solomon—the {{p|United Monarchy}}. Whether

The {{p|United Monarchy}} was as grand as the {{p|Hebrew Bible}} claims—
With Solomon's fabled wealth and wisdom, his vast temple, his
Empire stretching from the {{p|Euphrates}} to the border of {{p|Egypt}}—
Is the Gaiad's own history. The scholars called minimalists, who make David and

Solomon at most local chieftains inflated by later {{p|Judahite}}
Court propaganda, are answered by the stones:^[The minimalist school, among them Thomas L. Thompson and Niels Peter Lemche, holds David and Solomon to be later inventions or small chieftains. The Tel Dan stele of the ninth century BCE names the House of David.]
The biblical account reflects a real kingdom. The discovery

Of the {{p|Tel Dan Stele}} in nineteen-ninety-three provided what
The first attestation of David outside the Bible:
An {{p|Aramaic}} inscription from the ninth century BCE, commissioned
By an {{p|Aramean}} king (probably Hazael of Aram), boasting of defeating

The "House of David" ({{p|bytdwd}}). This proves, at minimum, that by
The ninth century BCE there was a {{p|Judahite}} royal dynasty claiming
Descent from someone named David. Some historical David existed,
Founder of a dynasty. The {{x|Gaiad}} accepts this and proceeds.

What the {{p|Hebrew Bible}} tells us about David is the most intensely
Detailed human portrait of any pre-Axial historical figure. We
Have his military exploits, his psychological turmoil, his political
Calculations, his family's dysfunctions, his personal faith, his

Moral failures, his artistic sensitivity. No other ancient {{p|Near Eastern}}
Monarch is portrayed with anything like this level of interiority.
Even the actual historical David, however modest his realm, must
Have been an extraordinary figure to generate this literary response.

And what a literary response it is. The books of {{p|1}} and {{p|2 Samuel}},
Especially the so-called "{{x|Court History}}" or "{{x|Succession Narrative}}"
(Chapters nine through twenty of {{p|2 Samuel}} and the first two
Chapters of {{p|1 Kings}}), constitute one of the great works of ancient

Prose. Their narrative economy, psychological insight, and moral
Complexity have been recognized as foundational by critics as
Diverse as Erich Auerbach, Robert Alter, Meir Sternberg, and
Harold Bloom. The author of the {{x|Court History}} stands alongside

Homer as one of the two great literary pioneers of the ancient
{{p|Near East}}, and unlike Homer's world of kings and gods, the Court
History presents a world of intricate human motivation in which
Divine intervention is present but elusive. The prose is

Revolutionary. It treats David not as a two-dimensional hero but
As a man of profound ambiguities—brave and calculating, faithful
And faithless, tender and cruel, a poet and a murderer. No single
Figure in the {{p|Hebrew Bible}} is portrayed with such complexity. And

No figure has been more influential on {{p|Western}} literary
Imagination. Every subsequent portrait of a complex monarch—
Shakespeare's Henry IV, Hamlet's troubled father, every tormented
King in the {{p|Western}} tradition—traces ancestry, in some measure,

To the David of the {{x|Court History}}.

                                          Let us narrate David's
Life as the text gives it. David is the youngest son of Jesse
Of {{p|Bethlehem}}, of the tribe of {{p|Judah}}. He is a shepherd boy, of

Ruddy appearance and beautiful eyes. He is also, the text tells
Us, a skilled musician—a lyre-player whose music can soothe
Troubled spirits. And it is this skill that first brings him into
Contact with King Saul. Saul, the first king of {{p|Israel}},

Suffers from a "tormenting spirit" (we might call it depression
Or perhaps something more severe), and his attendants procure
David to play for him and calm him. David becomes attached to
Saul's court. Then comes the famous encounter with Goliath, the

{{p|Philistine}} giant. The {{p|Philistines}}—the {{p|Sea Peoples}} who had
Settled the southern coast of {{p|Canaan}} during the {{x|Bronze Age}}
Collapse—are {{p|Israel}}'s principal enemy in this period. Their
Champion Goliath challenges any {{p|Israelite}} to single combat.

David, too young to bear full armor, accepts the challenge armed
Only with a sling and stones, and kills Goliath with a single
Shot to the forehead. The {{p|Philistine}} army flees. David becomes
A national hero, a figure greater in popular acclaim than Saul

Himself. And from this moment, Saul's relationship with David
Becomes fatally ambivalent. Saul loves him as a son, has him
Marry his daughter Michal, and yet fears him as a rival. The
Text is explicit about the psychological oscillation: Saul

Repeatedly tries to kill David, relents in moments of clarity,
Then tries again. David flees into the wilderness, gathering a
Band of outcast warriors, living as a bandit-chief in the
{{p|Judean}} wilderness. Twice during this period David has Saul

At his mercy, and twice David spares him. David's dharma (we
Might say, borrowing the {{p|Sanskrit}} term from the {{p|Ramayana}}
Chapter) will not allow him to strike down "Yahweh's anointed,"
Even though that anointed one seeks his life. David waits. He

Serves as a mercenary under the {{p|Philistine}} king Achish of Gath
For a time—a morally complicated maneuver in which David
Pretends to raid {{p|Israelite}} settlements while actually raiding
Other targets, preserving his {{p|Israelite}} loyalty while eating

{{p|Philistine}} bread. The {{p|Hebrew}} narrator does not try to clean
This up. David's exile is morally murky. He is a survivor.
Then Saul and his sons, including David's beloved Jonathan,
Are killed at the {{p|Battle of Mount Gilboa}} against the {{p|Philistines}}.

Saul, wounded and facing capture, falls on his own sword. Jonathan
Is slain beside him. David mourns them in one of the most moving
Elegies in world literature ({{p|2 Samuel}} chapter one): "How are
The mighty fallen, and the weapons of war perished!" The love

Between David and Jonathan—celebrated in the text as "passing
The love of women"—has been interpreted variously through the
Centuries. Some commentators see it as a profound friendship,
Some as an expression of same-sex romantic love, some as

Politically tinged loyalty between a prince and his designated
Successor. The text does not disambiguate. What it gives us is
A love so deep that David's grief echoes through the centuries.

After Saul's death, David becomes king—first of {{p|Judah}} alone,
With his capital at {{p|Hebron}}, then, after a civil war with Saul's
Surviving son Ish-Bosheth, of all {{p|Israel}}. He captures the
{{p|Jebusite}} fortress of {{p|Jerusalem}} and makes it his capital—a

Politically astute choice, because {{p|Jerusalem}} was neutral territory
Belonging to neither the northern nor southern tribes. David
Centralizes the monarchy. He brings the {{p|Ark of the Covenant}},
The sacred chest containing the covenant tablets, into {{p|Jerusalem}},

Dancing ecstatically before it (to the disgust of Michal, who
Sees his behavior as undignified). He conducts a series of
Military campaigns against the {{p|Philistines}}, {{p|Moabites}}, {{p|Ammonites}},
{{p|Edomites}}, and {{p|Arameans}}, extending {{p|Israelite}} power. He builds

A substantial kingdom, though probably not the empire the later
Tradition imagines. And at the height of his power, he commits
The great sin that will shape his legacy: he takes Bathsheba,
The wife of Uriah the Hittite, one of his most loyal warriors,

While Uriah is away at the siege of {{p|Rabbah}}. When Bathsheba
Becomes pregnant, David arranges for Uriah to be placed in
The most dangerous part of the battle line, so that he will be
Killed. The plan succeeds. Uriah dies. David marries Bathsheba.

But the prophet Nathan confronts David with the parable of
The rich man and the poor man's ewe lamb. David, provoked to
Righteous anger by the parable, condemns the rich man—and Nathan
Replies: "You are the man." David repents. He fasts, he weeps, he

Composes the psalm traditionally identified as {{p|Psalm 51}} ("Have
Mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness..."). But
The consequences unfold. The first child Bathsheba bears to
David dies in infancy. His family is cursed. His eldest son

Amnon rapes his half-sister Tamar. His second son Absalom
Avenges her by murdering Amnon, then rebels against his father
David, driving him from {{p|Jerusalem}}. The civil war is
Catastrophic. Absalom is killed in the forest of {{p|Ephraim}},

Caught by his famous long hair in the branches of an oak and
Stabbed by Joab, David's general (against David's explicit
Orders to spare him). David's grief for Absalom is one of the
Most devastating passages in ancient literature: "O my son

Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would God I had died for thee,
O Absalom, my son, my son!" A father's anguish, undiluted by
Any official decorum, recorded in the annals of a king. The
{{x|Court History}} allows this. It does not edit the grief out.

David survives the rebellion but is broken. The remainder of
His reign is marked by further intrigues: the rebellion of Sheba,
The succession crisis as David grows old, the political maneuvers
By which Bathsheba and the prophet Nathan secure the throne

For Solomon, David and Bathsheba's second son, over the claims
Of Adonijah, another of David's sons. David dies old, full of
Days, and is buried in the "City of David" in {{p|Jerusalem}}. The
Biblical tradition remembers him as {{p|Israel}}'s greatest king—the

Standard against which all subsequent {{p|Jewish}} and, later,
{{p|Christian}} messianic expectations are measured. The promised
Messiah is the "son of David," a descendant of the Davidic
Line who will restore {{p|Israel}} to its full glory. Generations of

{{p|Jews}} lived and died awaiting this messianic king. {{p|Christians}}
Came to believe that the messianic king had arrived in Jesus of
{{p|Nazareth}}, identified as a descendant of David through Joseph.
{{p|Islam}} also recognizes David as a prophet, called Dawud. In

{{p|Jewish}}, {{p|Christian}}, and {{p|Islamic}} tradition alike, David is one
Of the central figures of sacred history. His psalms—the {{p|Psalter}},
Traditionally attributed to him in whole or in part—are among
The most frequently read religious texts in human history. For

Two and a half millennia, the {{p|Psalter}} has shaped prayer, worship,
Poetry, music, and private devotion across the {{p|Abrahamic}} world.
Its cries of praise, lament, thanksgiving, and petition are
The religious vocabulary of half the world's population.

And consider what David's life means structurally. He is the
Ideal of the righteous king who is also genuinely and persistently
Human. He is not a god-king like Pharaoh. He is not a semi-divine
Hero like Gilgamesh. He is a man—a man with whom Yahweh has a

Covenant, yes, a man whom Yahweh has chosen, yes, but a man. A
Man who loves, sins, repents, suffers, and finally dies. His
Covenant relationship with Yahweh is mediated through his human
Agency, his human choices. This is a profoundly different vision

Of kingship from anything in the {{x|Bronze Age}}. {{x|Bronze Age}} kings
Were divine or semi-divine. They stood outside the ordinary
Human order. David stands within it. He is a new kind of
Religious figure: the monarch who is not a god but a servant of

A god. The monarch who is measured against divine moral standards
And can fail to meet them. The monarch who repents publicly.
This is the model that will shape later {{p|Jewish}}, {{p|Christian}},
And {{p|Islamic}} political theology. A king stands under God, not

Beside him. A king can be rebuked by a prophet. A king is
Accountable. The David-Nathan encounter—the prophet confronting
The king about his sin—is one of the foundational moments of
Western political-religious imagination. It asserts that even

The most powerful ruler is accountable to a moral order that
Transcends his own authority. The implications of this for the
Development of democracy, constitutional government, and human
Rights are profound. They trace back, in substantial measure, to

David's kneeling before Nathan and saying: "I have sinned
Against the Lord."

                        David. Son of Jesse. Shepherd. Harpist.
Giant-slayer. Outlaw. Mercenary. King. Adulterer. Murderer.

Penitent. Psalmist. Father. Mourner. Founder of dynasty. The
{{x|Court History}}'s devastating human portrait. The covenant with
Yahweh to establish an eternal house. The {{p|Jerusalem}} capital.
The {{p|Ark of the Covenant}} installed with dancing. The promise of

A messiah from his line. The psalter that has shaped three
Millennia of prayer.

                         David. Melek David. The king whose
Human complexity opened space for a new kind of political and

Religious imagination. The ancestor, by flesh or by adoption,
Of every messianic hope.


# Chapter 165: Classical Greece, Part One

The {{p|Greek}} {{x|Dark Age}} lasted roughly four centuries. From the
Destruction of the {{p|Mycenaean}} palaces around twelve-hundred BCE
To the dawn of the {{p|Archaic}} period around eight-hundred BCE,
{{p|Greece}} was a land of small villages, depopulated cities, and

Oral tradition. Writing had been lost. Trade had contracted. The
Great bronze-age palatial economies were a distant memory. But
During those four centuries something important was happening.
The memory of the Bronze Age was being preserved, orally, in the

Form of heroic epic. And the cultural forms that would become
Classical {{p|Greek}} civilization were being slowly, slowly, rebuilt
From the ground up. When the dawn came, around the eighth century
BCE, it came with astonishing rapidity.

                                            The rise of the {{p|polis}}.
The rebirth of literacy, through the adoption and modification of
The {{p|Phoenician}} alphabet. The composition of the {{p|Iliad}} and
{{p|Odyssey}}, crystallizing centuries of oral tradition into

Written form (traditionally attributed to Homer, a possibly
Historical and possibly conventional figure around whom oral
Traditions coalesced). The founding of the {{p|Olympic Games}} (seven-
Seventy-six BCE). The establishment of the {{p|Oracle of Delphi}} as

The pan-{{p|Greek}} religious center. The beginning of {{p|Greek}} colonization
Across the {{p|Mediterranean}} and {{p|Black Sea}} coasts. All of this
Happened within a single dynamic century or two. {{p|Greece}} went
From a backwater of small villages to a network of vibrant,

Competitive, philosophically adventurous city-states that would
Shape the next two and a half millennia of {{p|Western}} civilization.

                                                                      The
{{p|polis}}, the city-state, was the distinctive {{p|Greek}} political form.

Unlike the great territorial empires of {{p|Egypt}} or {{p|Persia}}, the
{{p|Greek}} world was fragmented into hundreds of small, independent
Communities, each centered on a single city with its surrounding
Agricultural land. The largest {{p|poleis}} had citizen populations of

Tens of thousands; most had only hundreds or a few thousand. Each
Had its own laws, its own calendar, its own dialect, its own
Patron deities. Each was fiercely independent. And each was
Organized around a specific ideology: the citizen body (the

{{p|demos}}, the people) was the political community. Citizens
Participated in governance—varying forms of it, from oligarchy
To democracy to tyranny—but they participated. Their political
Identity was tied to their {{p|polis}}. "{{p|Greek}}" was a cultural and

Linguistic identity, but "{{p|Athenian}}" or "{{p|Spartan}}" or "{{p|Corinthian}}"
Was the political identity. And the fierce loyalty of citizens
To their {{p|polis}}, combined with the intense rivalries among
{{p|poleis}}, drove the cultural dynamism that would make {{p|classical Greece}}

Extraordinarily creative. Every {{p|polis}} was trying to outdo every
Other {{p|polis}} in festivals, temples, military power, philosophical
Schools, athletic victories. The competitive energy was enormous.
And because each {{p|polis}} was small, individual citizens could

Have real impact. A single rich man could commission a monument
Or a dramatic festival that would change the cultural landscape
Of his city. This was a very different scale of cultural
Production from the great bureaucratic empires. It was more

Nimble, more innovative, more personal.

                                           The two great poles of
{{p|Classical Greek}} civilization would be {{p|Athens}} and {{p|Sparta}}, and
Their contrasting character deserves attention. {{p|Sparta}}, in the

Southern {{p|Peloponnese}}, developed a uniquely militarized society.
After subjugating the neighboring {{p|Messenian}} population (the
{{p|Helots}}) around the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, the
{{p|Spartans}} transformed themselves into a warrior caste devoted

Entirely to military training and rule. The laws attributed to
The legendary lawgiver Lycurgus organized every aspect of
{{p|Spartan}} life around producing and sustaining elite warriors.
Male children were taken from their families at age seven and

Raised in communal military barracks through a brutal training
System, the {{p|agoge}}. Adult male citizens lived in common messes
And were forbidden to engage in trade or manual labor—all such
Work was done by {{p|Helots}}. {{p|Spartan}} women had more freedom and

Physical education than women in other {{p|Greek}} city-states,
Because {{p|Sparta}} valued the physical fitness of mothers to produce
Strong warriors. {{p|Sparta}} was governed by a dual monarchy (two kings
From two royal lines), a council of elders, and a citizen assembly

With limited powers. The kingship was hereditary but constitutionally
Constrained. The system was stable for centuries. And the {{p|Spartan}}
Military—the famous hoplite phalanx, with its disciplined
Infantry—was the most feared in the {{p|Greek}} world for nearly three

Centuries. The three hundred {{p|Spartans}} at {{p|Thermopylae}}, standing
Against Xerxes's vast army, are a cultural archetype so powerful
That they still define "heroic last stand" in popular imagination.
But the cost of {{p|Spartan}} militarism was the intellectual and

Artistic impoverishment of their culture. {{p|Sparta}} produced no
Great philosophers, no great dramatists, no great historians. It
Produced only warriors and their laws. Its cultural legacy is
Thin compared to its military prestige.

                                          {{p|Athens}}, by contrast,
Developed in the opposite direction. {{p|Athens}} in the {{p|Archaic}} period
Was governed by aristocratic families—the {{p|Eupatridae}}—but a
Series of reforms gradually democratized the political system.

Solon, around five-ninety-four BCE, enacted sweeping legal reforms:
He cancelled debts that had reduced poor citizens to slavery, he
Opened political participation to all citizens regardless of birth
(Though still ranked by wealth), and he laid the foundation for

Later democratic development. After Solon, {{p|Athens}} underwent a
Period of tyranny under Peisistratos and his sons (tyranny in the
{{p|Greek}} sense meant one-man rule that bypassed constitutional forms
But was not necessarily cruel—Peisistratos was widely considered

A good ruler). After the overthrow of Hippias, the last of the
Peisistratid tyrants, in five-oh-eight BCE, Cleisthenes instituted
Radical democratic reforms: he reorganized the citizen body into
Ten new tribes based on geographical deme membership (breaking the

Power of aristocratic kinship networks), he established the
Boule (council of five hundred citizens selected by lot) as the
Principal executive body, and he gave the {{p|Ecclesia}} (the citizen
{{x|Assembly}}) supreme legislative power. With these reforms, {{p|Athens}}

Became the first substantial democracy in human history. "The
People" ({{p|demos}}) ruled ({{p|kratos}})—hence {{p|demokratia}}. Now, to be
Clear, {{p|Athenian}} democracy was limited. Only adult male citizens
Could participate. Women, foreign residents ({{p|metics}}), and slaves

Were excluded. Perhaps fifteen percent of {{p|Athens}}'s total population
Had full political rights. And those fifteen percent participated
Directly—not through elected representatives, but through actual
Attendance at the {{x|Assembly}} and service in executive bodies, often

Selected by lot. This was direct democracy, radically different
From modern representative democracy. It worked because the
Citizen body was small enough (a few thousand) that direct
Participation was feasible, and because citizens had time—afforded

By slave labor and a prosperous economy—to engage in political
Life. It was democracy for a leisured minority, enabled by the
Exploitation of a disenfranchised majority. The contradiction is
Real and has been justly criticized. But even acknowledging that

Contradiction, the {{p|Athenian}} political experiment was unprecedented.
No prior society had given political power so directly to so
Large a fraction of its male population. The consequences for
Cultural and intellectual life were transformative. Because any

Citizen could propose legislation, could serve on juries, could
Speak in the {{x|Assembly}}, the premium on rhetorical skill was enormous.
Education in rhetoric, logic, and persuasion became essential for
Citizens who wanted influence. This created the market for the

{{p|Sophists}}—traveling teachers of rhetoric and wisdom who charged
Fees for instruction. And this in turn set the stage for {{p|Socrates}}
And the philosophical revolution. But that comes in the next
Chapter. Here we are laying the foundations.

                                               {{p|Athens}} under
Democracy became a cultural hothouse. Its fifth century BCE—
Between the {{p|Persian Wars}} at its beginning and the {{p|Peloponnesian War}}
At its end—was one of the most creative periods in human history.

{{p|Athens}} produced the playwrights Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides,
And Aristophanes—whose tragedies and comedies founded the
Western dramatic tradition. It produced the historians Herodotus
And Thucydides—who invented history as a literary and analytical

Genre. It produced the sculptor Phidias, whose statues of Athena
And Zeus were the most celebrated of antiquity. It produced the
Architect Ictinus, whose {{p|Parthenon}} atop the {{p|Acropolis}} remains
One of the most influential buildings ever designed. It produced

The politician Pericles, whose leadership and oratory defined
The golden age. And it produced Socrates, whose radically
Different philosophical method would reshape all subsequent
Western thought. All within a single city of perhaps fifty

Thousand adult male citizens. All within roughly a century. The
Cultural output per capita is staggering. Nothing comparable had
Ever happened before, and few comparable cultural efflorescences
Have happened since. And it all depended on the democratic

Political structure that gave ordinary citizens the opportunity,
And the obligation, to think and speak and contribute.

                                                          But
We are getting ahead of ourselves. Before the fifth-century golden

Age came the sixth-century preparation. In the sixth century BCE,
{{p|Greek}} philosophy began. It began not in {{p|Athens}} but in {{p|Ionia}}—
The {{p|Greek}}-speaking cities of the western {{p|Anatolian}} coast,
Including {{p|Miletus}}, {{p|Ephesus}}, {{p|Samos}}, and others. The {{p|Ionians}} were

At the eastern frontier of the {{p|Greek}} world, in close contact
With the older civilizations of {{p|Lydia}}, {{p|Phrygia}}, {{p|Mesopotamia}}, and
{{p|Egypt}}. They absorbed mathematical, astronomical, and religious
Ideas from those civilizations. And they began to ask new kinds

Of questions. Thales of Miletus, traditionally dated to the early
Sixth century BCE, is commonly identified as the first philosopher.
He asked: what is everything made of? And he gave an answer:
Water. All things, he proposed, are ultimately water in different

Forms. The specific claim is less important than the kind of
Question and the kind of answer. Thales was not asking "what
Gods created the world?" (the mythological question). He was
Asking "what is the fundamental substance of the world?" (the

Philosophical question). And he was answering it not by citing
Divine authority or ancient tradition but by reasoning about
Observed nature. Water becomes ice. Water becomes steam. Water
Supports life. Water is found everywhere. Perhaps, he reasoned,

Water is the primary substance, and other apparent substances
Are just transformations of it. This reasoning was rough by
Modern standards. But it was a new kind of reasoning. It was
Reasoning from observation toward general principles, seeking

Naturalistic explanations for observed phenomena. Thales founded
What would later be called the {{p|Milesian School}}—a tradition of
{{p|Ionian}} natural philosophy that included Anaximander (who
Proposed that the primary principle was not water but an

Unbounded and indeterminate stuff, the {{p|apeiron}}), Anaximenes
(Who proposed air as the primary substance), and later thinkers
Who variously proposed fire, number, being itself, atoms. These
Thinkers are called the {{p|Pre-Socratics}}, and their work between

The sixth and fifth centuries BCE laid the foundation for all
Subsequent {{p|Western}} philosophy. They asked: what is the nature
Of reality? What is it made of? How does it change? Is change
Real or illusory? Is the world one or many? What is the soul?

What is knowledge? What is the good? These questions have not
Been answered definitively in two and a half millennia. But the
Asking of them—in the form of rational argument accountable to
Observation and logic—began in {{p|Ionia}} in the sixth century BCE.

Pythagoras of Samos, a contemporary of the later {{p|Milesians}},
Emigrated to {{p|Croton}} in southern {{p|Italy}} and founded a semi-
Religious, semi-philosophical community there. {{p|Pythagorean}}
Teachings emphasized the fundamental role of number in reality.

Pythagoras is credited (probably anachronistically) with the
{{p|Pythagorean theorem}}, with the discovery of musical ratios, and
With the concept that mathematics expresses the deep structure
Of the cosmos. The {{p|Pythagoreans}} also taught the transmigration

Of souls (a doctrine that may have entered {{p|Greek}} thought from
{{p|Indian}} or {{p|Egyptian}} sources or may have developed independently).
Their legacy would shape {{p|Plato}}'s philosophy profoundly. The
Idea that the ultimate structure of reality is mathematical—an

Idea so central to modern physics—traces its {{p|Western}} origin to
The {{p|Pythagoreans}}. Heraclitus of Ephesus, in the early fifth
Century BCE, developed a philosophy of flux and opposites. "One
Cannot step into the same river twice," he famously said. All

Things flow; stability is illusion; reality is the tension of
Opposites. Parmenides of Elea, working roughly contemporaneously
In southern {{p|Italy}}, developed exactly the opposite view: change
Is impossible, being is eternal and unchanging, what appears to

Change is mere illusion. These two opposed positions—the Heraclitean
And the Parmenidean—frame a dialectic that still structures
Western philosophy. Every metaphysical debate about the nature
Of change, of identity, of permanence, of becoming, traces back

To these {{p|pre-Socratic}} arguments. And meanwhile, while these
Philosophers were working out the structure of reality, the
Historians were inventing the investigation of human affairs.
Herodotus of Halicarnassus traveled across the {{p|Mediterranean}}

World collecting stories, customs, and accounts. His {{p|Histories}}—
The first prose historical work in the {{p|Greek}} tradition—covers
The {{p|Persian Wars}} but also includes ethnographic accounts of
{{p|Egypt}}, {{p|Persia}}, {{p|Scythia}}, {{p|Babylon}}. He is curious, discursive,

Often credulous, but brilliantly humane. He treats non-{{p|Greek}}
Cultures with more respect than most later {{p|Greek}} writers. He
Preserves information about foreign civilizations that would
Otherwise be lost. He is the father of history in both senses:

The first historian and the origin of much {{p|Greek}} knowledge
About foreign peoples. Thucydides of {{p|Athens}}, coming a generation
Later, wrote a different kind of history. His {{p|History of the
Peloponnesian War}} is analytical, pitiless, and deeply concerned

With causation, motive, and the underlying dynamics of political
Power. He invented the technique of putting speeches into the
Mouths of historical actors—speeches that render the thinking
Of political leaders with a depth that bald narrative cannot

Achieve. Thucydides is the ancestor of hard-nosed political
Realism in historical writing. His account of the {{p|Melian Dialogue}}—
In which the {{p|Athenians}} justify their destruction of {{p|Melos}} with
The frigid maxim that "the strong do what they can and the weak

Suffer what they must"—is one of the most uncompromising statements
Of political power ever written. Between Herodotus's humane
Inclusiveness and Thucydides's analytical realism, {{p|Greek}}
Historiography staked out its enduring range.

                                                 And all of this—the
{{p|Ionian}} philosophers, the rise of democracy, the first historians,
The tragic and comic drama, the architectural and sculptural
Achievements—all of this happened against the backdrop of the

{{p|Persian Wars}}. In four-ninety BCE, Darius I of {{p|Persia}} sent an
Expeditionary force against {{p|Athens}} in retaliation for {{p|Athenian}}
Support of the {{p|Ionian}} revolt. The {{p|Persians}} were defeated at the
{{p|Battle of Marathon}} by an {{p|Athenian}}-{{p|Plataean}} coalition under the

Generalship of Miltiades. Ten years later, Xerxes I led a far
Larger invasion—perhaps the largest military operation the world
Had yet seen. His army crossed the {{p|Hellespont}} on a bridge of
Boats. {{p|Leonidas}} and his three hundred {{p|Spartans}}, along with

Several thousand allies, delayed him at {{p|Thermopylae}}. {{p|Athens}}
Was burned. But at the naval {{p|Battle of Salamis}} (four-eighty BCE),
The {{p|Greek}} fleet under Themistocles defeated the {{p|Persian}} navy.
The following year, at the {{p|Battle of Plataea}}, the combined {{p|Greek}}

Land forces defeated the remaining {{p|Persian}} army. Xerxes
Withdrew. The {{p|Persian}} threat was defeated. The {{p|Greeks}}—by which
We mean the shifting coalition of city-states that happened to
Cooperate for this crisis—had preserved their independence from

The greatest empire on earth. This victory had enormous
Psychological impact. The {{p|Greeks}} believed, not without reason,
That they had preserved the possibility of free citizens
Governing themselves against the imperial monarchies of the

{{p|Near East}}. The wars became part of {{p|Greek}} self-definition: we
Are not {{p|Persians}}; we do not bow; we govern ourselves. The
Rhetoric of {{p|Greek}} freedom versus {{p|Persian}} despotism became a
Civilizational touchstone that would shape {{p|Western}} political

Thought for the next two millennia. It was not entirely accurate—
The {{p|Greeks}} had plenty of their own tyrants and oppressive
Practices—but it was psychologically real and culturally
Productive. Out of the crisis of the {{p|Persian Wars}}, out of the

Victorious self-understanding that followed, came the classical
Age. And the classical age is what we will explore next.

                                                             {{p|Greece}}.
{{x|Dark Age}} to {{p|Archaic}} to classical. The {{p|polis}}. {{p|Athens}} and {{p|Sparta}}.

{{p|Ionian}} philosophy—Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, Parmenides,
Pythagoras. {{p|Solonian}} reforms. {{p|Cleisthenic}} democracy. The
{{p|Persian Wars}}—{{p|Marathon}}, {{p|Thermopylae}}, {{p|Salamis}}, {{p|Plataea}}. Herodotus
And Thucydides. The cultural hothouse taking shape.

Classical {{p|Greece}}, part one. The preparation. The rise of a
Civilization that would shape all of {{p|Western}} thought.


# Chapter 166: Assyria and Babylon

Before we follow {{p|Greece}} into its golden age, we must turn east
One more time and trace the {{p|Near East}} through the centuries
That followed the {{p|Bronze Age Collapse}}. Because while {{p|Israel}}
Was forming its monarchy and {{p|Persia}} was preparing to become an

Empire, the old {{p|Mesopotamian}} powers—{{p|Assyria}} and {{p|Babylon}}—were
Engaged in a long, alternating struggle for dominance that would
Reshape the {{p|Near East}} and profoundly affect the religious
Development of {{p|Israel}} and the wider world.

                                                     Begin with {{p|Assyria}}.
The {{p|Assyrian}} state, centered on the {{p|Tigris}} river in northern
{{p|Mesopotamia}}, had existed in various forms since the early second
Millennium BCE—the {{x|Gaiad}} has touched on the {{p|Old Assyrian}} and

{{p|Middle Assyrian}} periods. But {{p|Assyria}} reached its greatest
Extent and power in what is called the {{p|Neo-Assyrian Empire}}—
Flourishing roughly from nine-eleven BCE to six-oh-nine BCE, about
Three centuries of ruthless imperial power. The {{p|Neo-Assyrian}} kings

Built the first true empire of the {{p|Iron Age}}. Their method was
Militaristic, methodical, and brutally effective. They developed
Standing professional armies, siege engines, iron weapons in
Massive quantities, and a doctrine of terror: rebellious cities

Were not merely conquered but obliterated, their inhabitants flayed
Alive, impaled on stakes, or deported en masse to distant regions
Where they could not rebuild resistance. The {{p|Assyrian}} royal
Inscriptions boast of these atrocities in matter-of-fact detail:

"I flayed all the chief men, and I covered the walls of the city
With their skins." "I built a pillar over the city gate, and I
Flayed all the chief men... I stuck some on stakes... I cut off
The limbs of the officers... I cut off the hands and feet of

Some, and I cut off the ears and noses of others." The {{x|Gaiad}} does
Not dwell on these horrors gratuitously. But they are part of the
Historical record, and they affected everyone who lived in the
{{p|Near East}} during the {{p|Neo-Assyrian}} period. The threat of

{{p|Assyrian}} conquest was existential and total. Peoples prayed in
Genuine terror at the approach of {{p|Assyrian}} armies. Their fear
Is recorded in {{p|Hebrew}} scripture, in {{p|Egyptian}} inscriptions, in
{{p|Elamite}} and {{p|Babylonian}} records. {{p|Assyria}} was the boot on the

{{p|Near East}}'s neck for three centuries.

                                              And the {{p|Neo-Assyrian}}
Kings were builders as well as destroyers. They constructed new
Capitals—{{p|Nimrud}}, {{p|Dur-Sharrukin}} (near modern {{p|Khorsabad}}),

Eventually {{p|Nineveh}}—with massive palaces decorated by narrative
Bas-reliefs depicting royal lion hunts, military campaigns, and
Court ceremonies. Many of these reliefs are preserved in the
{{p|British Museum}} and elsewhere and are among the finest examples

Of ancient {{p|Near Eastern}} art. The {{p|Assyrian}} kings collected
Libraries: Ashurbanipal's library at {{p|Nineveh}}, with its tens of
Thousands of cuneiform tablets, preserved an enormous corpus of
{{p|Sumerian}}, {{p|Akkadian}}, and earlier {{p|Babylonian}} literature that

Would otherwise have been lost. It is thanks to this library that
We have the complete text of the {{p|Epic of Gilgamesh}}, recovered
From its ashes by nineteenth-century archaeologists. The
{{p|Neo-Assyrian}} kings conducted their terror campaigns with one

Hand and their cultural patronage with the other. The two
Activities were not, to them, contradictory.

                                                 The sequence of
{{p|Neo-Assyrian}} kings is worth marking. Ashurnasirpal II

(Eight-eighty-three to eight-fifty-nine BCE) consolidated the
Empire and moved the capital to {{p|Nimrud}}. Shalmaneser III
(Eight-fifty-nine to eight-twenty-four BCE) pushed {{p|Assyrian}}
Power westward and fought a major but indecisive battle at

{{p|Qarqar}} against a coalition including the {{p|Israelite}} king
Ahab—this is the first recorded {{p|Assyrian}} contact with
{{p|Israel}}. Tiglath-Pileser III (seven-forty-five to seven-twenty-seven
BCE) introduced military and administrative reforms that made

{{p|Assyria}} unstoppable: a permanent professional army, standardized
Provincial administration, systematic deportation of conquered
Peoples. Sargon II (seven-twenty-two to seven-oh-five BCE)
Completed the conquest of {{p|Israel}} (the northern kingdom), which

Fell in seven-twenty-two BCE. The ten tribes of the north were
Deported to various corners of the {{p|Assyrian}} empire and
Disappeared from history—becoming the "Lost Tribes of {{p|Israel}}"
About which so much later speculation has been generated. Foreign

Populations were imported into the former {{p|Israelite}} territory;
These settlers mixed with remaining {{p|Israelites}} to form the
{{p|Samaritans}}, who would play such an ambiguous role in later
{{p|Jewish}} history. Only the southern kingdom of {{p|Judah}} survived,

And even it survived only as a vassal of {{p|Assyria}}. Sennacherib
(Seven-oh-five to six-eighty-one BCE) invaded {{p|Judah}} in seven-oh-one
BCE, devastated forty-six cities, and besieged {{p|Jerusalem}}, where
King Hezekiah desperately held out. The {{p|Jerusalem}} siege was

Famously lifted, according to the {{p|Hebrew Bible}}, when an "angel of
Yahweh" killed a hundred and eighty-five thousand {{p|Assyrian}}
Soldiers in a single night—a miraculous deliverance celebrated
In {{p|Isaiah}} and {{p|2 Kings}}. The {{p|Assyrian}} records, predictably, do not

Mention this disaster, but the fact that Sennacherib did indeed
Withdraw from {{p|Jerusalem}} without taking it is attested. What really
Happened—plague, political urgency at home, strategic recalculation—
Is unclear. But {{p|Jerusalem}} survived. {{p|Judah}} survived. And the

Memory of this deliverance shaped {{p|Judean}} religious imagination,
Reinforcing the belief that Yahweh protected his holy city, that
Even the mighty {{p|Assyrian}} army could not take what Yahweh had
Set apart. Esarhaddon (six-eighty-one to six-sixty-nine BCE)

Completed the unfinished business of his predecessors by conquering
{{p|Egypt}} itself—an astonishing {{p|Assyrian}} triumph, though {{p|Egyptian}}
Rule would be briefly restored after his death. Ashurbanipal
(Six-sixty-eight to six-twenty-seven BCE) was the last great

{{p|Assyrian}} king. He ruled the empire at its maximum extent, from
{{p|Egypt}} to the {{p|Zagros Mountains}}. He was intellectually cultivated,
Collected the great library at {{p|Nineveh}}, and conducted complex
Diplomatic and military operations across his vast realm. But

Even under Ashurbanipal the empire was strained. A civil war
Against his brother Shamash-shum-ukin (who ruled as {{p|Assyrian}}
Viceroy in {{p|Babylon}} but rebelled) exhausted {{p|Assyrian}} resources.
After Ashurbanipal's death, the empire collapsed with stunning

Speed. Within twenty years of his passing, every major {{p|Assyrian}}
City had been destroyed and the empire had ceased to exist.

                                                                The
Agents of {{p|Assyria}}'s destruction were the {{p|Medes}} and the {{p|Neo-Babylonians}}.

The {{p|Medes}}, an {{p|Iranian}} people from the {{p|Zagros}} mountains (the
Predecessors of the {{p|Persians}} in {{p|Iranian}} political development),
Were the {{p|Assyrians}}' northern rivals. The {{p|Neo-Babylonians}}, under
Nabopolassar and then his son Nebuchadnezzar II, had rebelled

Against {{p|Assyrian}} control of {{p|Babylon}} and established an
Independent kingdom. In six-twelve BCE, a combined {{p|Mede}}-{{p|Babylonian}}
Army assaulted {{p|Nineveh}} and destroyed it. The city burned. Its
Population was massacred. The great library was buried in the

Ashes (where it would remain until its rediscovery in the nineteenth
Century CE). Sinsharishkun, the {{p|Assyrian}} king, perished in the
Flames. {{p|Assyrian}} imperial power ended abruptly. A few remnant
{{p|Assyrian}} forces tried to regroup at {{p|Harran}}, but they were defeated

In six-oh-nine BCE. The {{p|Neo-Assyrian Empire}} was finished. And
Consider what this meant. A state that had terrorized the {{p|Near East}}
For three centuries vanished within a single generation. The
{{p|Hebrew}} prophet Nahum celebrated the fall of {{p|Nineveh}} in ecstatic

Verse ({{p|Book of Nahum}}, probably composed very shortly after the
Event): "Woe to the bloody city! It is all full of lies and
Robbery... {{p|Nineveh}} is laid waste; who will bemoan her?" Every
Subject people of the {{p|Assyrian}} empire shared this sentiment.

{{p|Assyria}} had been an entity that stood for imperial cruelty on
A cosmic scale, and its destruction was felt as a moral
Vindication by all who had suffered under it. Including the
Surviving {{p|Judeans}} who now watched their former oppressor fall.

And in {{p|Assyria}}'s place rose {{p|Babylon}}. The {{p|Neo-Babylonian Empire}},
Under Nebuchadnezzar II (six-oh-five to five-sixty-two BCE),
Inherited most of the former {{p|Assyrian}} territory, including the
{{p|Levant}}. Nebuchadnezzar was one of the great figures of the

Ancient {{p|Near East}}. He defeated the {{p|Egyptian}} pharaoh Necho II
At the {{p|Battle of Carchemish}} in six-oh-five BCE, establishing
{{p|Babylonian}} supremacy over {{p|Syria}} and {{p|Palestine}}. He rebuilt
{{p|Babylon}} into a wonder of the ancient world. The city's walls

Were legendarily massive—the {{p|Ishtar Gate}}, with its glazed
Blue tiles and gold-decorated animal reliefs, is now partially
Reconstructed in the {{p|Pergamon Museum}} in {{p|Berlin}} and remains
Visually stunning. The {{p|Hanging Gardens of Babylon}}—one of the

Seven wonders of the ancient world, though their historicity is
Disputed—were attributed to Nebuchadnezzar's construction for
His {{p|Median}} queen Amytis who missed her mountainous homeland.
{{p|Babylon}} was enormous: perhaps two hundred thousand inhabitants,

With monumental architecture on a scale matched at the time only
By certain {{p|Egyptian}} and {{p|Chinese}} centers. And Nebuchadnezzar
Inherited {{p|Babylon}}'s ancient intellectual traditions. The
{{p|Neo-Babylonians}} refined {{p|Mesopotamian}} astronomy to an astonishing

Degree. They kept detailed records of celestial phenomena over
Centuries, developed mathematical models of planetary motion,
Invented the zodiac, and developed the first accurate lunar
Calendar. {{p|Babylonian}} astronomy would be inherited by the {{p|Greeks}}

(Particularly through Hipparchus and later Ptolemy) and become
The foundation of all {{p|Western}} astronomy until Copernicus. The
Names of the zodiac signs—Aries, Taurus, Gemini, and so on—
Are {{p|Babylonian}} in origin, translated through {{p|Greek}} and {{p|Latin}}.

The seven-day week with days named after the seven visible
Celestial bodies ({{p|Sun}}, {{p|Moon}}, {{p|Mars}}, {{p|Mercury}}, {{p|Jupiter}},
{{p|Venus}}, {{p|Saturn}}) originated in {{p|Babylonian}} astronomical religion.
The division of the circle into three hundred sixty degrees, of

The hour into sixty minutes, of the minute into sixty seconds—
All of this is the heritage of {{p|Babylonian}} mathematics. Every
Time we check the time, we are using a time-keeping system inherited
From {{p|Neo-Babylonian}} astronomer-priests working in the sixth

Century BCE. It is a remarkably persistent cultural gift.

                                                             But
For the {{p|Judean}} population, Nebuchadnezzar would be remembered
For another reason entirely. In five-ninety-seven BCE, Nebuchadnezzar

Besieged {{p|Jerusalem}} because its king Jehoiakim had rebelled
Against {{p|Babylonian}} suzerainty. The city surrendered. Nebuchadnezzar
Deported the {{p|Judean}} king (now Jehoiachin, Jehoiakim's successor),
The royal family, the nobility, and the skilled artisans to

{{p|Babylon}}. This is the first deportation. Ten years later, in
Five-eighty-seven BCE, {{p|Judah}} rebelled again under the new puppet
King Zedekiah. This time Nebuchadnezzar was less lenient. His
Army besieged {{p|Jerusalem}} for eighteen months. When the city fell,

It was sacked systematically. The temple of Solomon—the central
Religious institution of {{p|Judahite}} faith, built four centuries
Earlier by the third king of {{p|Israel}}—was destroyed. Its gold
And bronze fittings were carried back to {{p|Babylon}} as spoils.

The royal family was killed or blinded. Zedekiah's sons were
Slain before his eyes, then his eyes were put out, and he was
Led in chains to {{p|Babylon}}. The remaining population of {{p|Jerusalem}}
Was deported. This is the second deportation, the great {{p|Babylonian}}

Exile. {{p|Judah}} was ended as a political entity. Its royal dynasty
(The line of David) was broken. Its temple was gone. Its people
Were scattered—some in {{p|Babylon}}, some fleeing to {{p|Egypt}}, some
Remaining in the devastated homeland as peasants under {{p|Babylonian}}

Administration. By every conventional measure of ancient {{p|Near Eastern}}
History, this should have been the end of {{p|Judahite}} civilization.
It should have followed the pattern of the northern kingdom of
{{p|Israel}} a century and a half earlier: deported, scattered,

Assimilated, lost to history. But it did not. {{p|Judah}}—unlike most
Of the peoples {{p|Assyria}} and {{p|Babylon}} had deported—managed to
Preserve its religious and cultural identity through the exile.
This preservation is one of the most remarkable cultural

Phenomena in ancient history. How did it happen? Partly because
Nebuchadnezzar's deportation policies, unlike {{p|Assyria}}'s, did not
Attempt to disperse deported populations. The {{p|Judean}} exiles
Were settled together in {{p|Babylonian}} territory, maintaining their

Community. Partly because they had, in their scripture, a religious
Foundation that did not depend on a specific temple or land. The
{{p|Torah}}—or the early sources that would become the {{p|Torah}}—taught
Them that they had a special covenant with Yahweh regardless of

Their political fortunes. Partly because the shock of exile
Prompted intense religious reflection. The prophets of the exile—
Ezekiel, {{p|Second Isaiah}}, Jeremiah (who had warned them of the
Exile before it happened)—reframed the disaster as divine

Discipline rather than divine abandonment. Yahweh had not been
Defeated by Marduk, the {{p|Babylonian}} god. Yahweh had allowed the
Exile because {{p|Judah}} had strayed from the covenant, and Yahweh
Would eventually restore them. This theological reframing kept

The community coherent. And partly because during the exile the
{{p|Judean}} religious scholars began the massive project of editing
And compiling their scriptures. Much of what we now call the
{{p|Hebrew Bible}} was edited into its enduring form during or shortly

After the {{p|Babylonian}} exile. The {{p|Pentateuch}}, the {{p|Deuteronomic}}
History, the prophetic books—all received their definitive
Editorial shape during this period. The exile was the furnace in
Which {{p|Judaism}} as a text-centered religion was forged. Rather

Than being destroyed by the exile, {{p|Judahite}} religion was
Transformed by it. When the opportunity to return came—after
Cyrus the Great of {{p|Persia}} conquered {{p|Babylon}} in five-thirty-nine
BCE and issued his decree permitting the exiles to return—what

Returned was no longer the old {{p|Judahite}} religion. It was something
New: {{p|Second Temple Judaism}}, a religion of text and law and
Synagogue as much as of temple and sacrifice, a religion that
Could survive anywhere its people happened to live, a religion

Equipped to weather further catastrophes (as it would need to
Do). The {{p|Babylonian}} exile was the crucible. It forged the
Template of diasporic religion that would shape {{p|Judaism}} for
Millennia and influence every subsequent religion of the Book.

And as for Nebuchadnezzar's own fate, he died in five-sixty-two
BCE. His successors were weak. The empire declined rapidly. In
Five-thirty-nine BCE, Cyrus the Great of {{p|Persia}} marched on
{{p|Babylon}}. The city fell, perhaps without a siege—some accounts

Suggest the {{p|Babylonian}} population welcomed Cyrus as a liberator
From the unpopular last king Nabonidus. {{p|Babylon}} became a
{{p|Persian}} province. And thus the two great {{p|Mesopotamian}} powers—
{{p|Assyria}} and {{p|Babylon}}—ended. First {{p|Assyria}} destroyed, then

{{p|Babylon}} conquered. The {{p|Near East}} entered a new phase: the
Age of the {{p|Persian}} empire, which would rule from the {{p|Indus}}
To the {{p|Aegean}} for the next two centuries.

                                                     {{p|Assyria}}.
{{p|Babylon}}. The three centuries of {{p|Neo-Assyrian}} terror. The deportation
Of the northern tribes. The destruction of {{p|Nineveh}} in six-twelve.
The brief {{p|Neo-Babylonian}} supremacy. Nebuchadnezzar rebuilding
{{p|Babylon}} with his blue-glazed gates and fabled gardens.

The {{p|Jerusalem}} exile of five-eighty-seven. The forging of
Diasporic {{p|Judaism}} in the furnace. Cyrus of {{p|Persia}} as liberator.
The {{p|Judean}} return and the building of the {{p|Second Temple}}.

The zodiac and the seven-day week, inheritances of {{p|Babylonian}}
Astronomer-priests. The library of Ashurbanipal, preserving
{{p|Gilgamesh}} in its ashes. The template of text-centered religion.

{{p|Assyria}} and {{p|Babylon}}. The {{p|Mesopotamian}} twilight. The catastrophe
That produced {{p|Judaism}}.


# Chapter 167: The Babylonian Exile and the Birth of Monotheism

The previous chapter told the story of the {{p|Babylonian}} exile as
Political and cultural catastrophe. This chapter tells it as
Theological crucible. Because what emerged from the exile was
Something new in the religious imagination of humanity: genuine,

Full-throated monotheism.

                            To understand this, we must be
Careful about terms. The {{x|Gaiad}} has argued, in earlier chapters,
That true monotheism is historically rare—rarer than popular

Accounts suggest. The {{x|Bronze Age}} world was populated by
Polytheisms. The {{x|Axial Age}} produced dualisms ({{p|Zoroastrianism}}),
Monisms ({{p|Upanishadic}} philosophy), and various forms of divine
Hierarchy. True monotheism—the claim that there is only one god,

That no other gods exist even as subordinate or defeated beings—
Is distinctive. And the claim that emerged from {{p|Babylonian}}-exile
{{p|Judaism}} is specifically this: not merely that Yahweh is the
Greatest god, nor that Yahweh is the only god worth worshipping,

But that Yahweh is literally the only god—that all other "gods"
Are delusions, wood and stone, nothing. This is a radical claim.
It is not present in the earliest layers of the {{p|Hebrew Bible}}.
It emerges in the later layers, especially during and after the

Exile. Consider the evidence. Early biblical texts speak of Yahweh
As greater than other gods, as chief among them, as jealous of
{{p|Israelite}} loyalty, but they acknowledge that other gods exist.
"Who is like you, O Yahweh, among the gods?" asks {{p|Exodus}} 15:11.

This is henotheism: the worship of one god while acknowledging
Others. The first commandment itself—"You shall have no other
Gods before me"—presupposes that there are other gods; the
Command is exclusivity of worship, not denial of existence. Early

Prophetic and historical texts in the {{p|Hebrew Bible}} operate
Within this henotheistic framework. But the great prophets of
The exile period—especially {{p|Second Isaiah}} (the anonymous prophet
Whose oracles are preserved in chapters forty through fifty-five

Of the {{p|Book of Isaiah}}, composed during the {{p|Babylonian}} exile)—
Move beyond henotheism to explicit monotheism. "I am Yahweh,
And there is no other; besides me there is no god" ({{p|Isaiah}} 45:5).
"I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god"

({{p|Isaiah}} 44:6). "The idols of the nations are silver and gold,
The work of human hands; they have mouths, but do not speak; they
Have eyes, but do not see" ({{p|Psalm}} 115:4-5). This is not henotheism.
This is a new claim: that only Yahweh is real, that other gods

Are illusions, that worship of them is folly. The theological
Stakes of this claim are enormous. If only Yahweh is real, then
Yahweh is not only {{p|Israel}}'s god but the god of all nations
(Even if they do not know it). If only Yahweh is real, then

Yahweh controls history universally, not just {{p|Israel}}'s portion.
If only Yahweh is real, then the cosmic moral structure applies
To all humanity. And if only Yahweh is real, then the religious
Diversity of the nations is not a competition among different

Deities but a universal confusion about the one true god. The
Missional implications of this theology are profound—universal
Monotheism tends toward universal religion. But the first
Articulation of this monotheism was defensive, not missionary.

It emerged as the {{p|Judahite}} response to crushing defeat.

                                                                How
Did the exile produce this theological leap? The {{x|Gaiad}} proposes
Several interacting factors. First, the experience of exile itself.

Ancient religions were typically territorial: each god had a land,
A people, a specific place of dwelling. To be deported from your
Land was typically to be separated from your god. If Yahweh was
A territorial god of {{p|Judah}}, then the exiles in {{p|Babylon}} had

Left him behind. The exilic community had to choose: either
Yahweh had been defeated by Marduk (and therefore abandoned them),
Or Yahweh had power extending beyond {{p|Judah}}. They chose the
Second option. They developed the theology that Yahweh was

Everywhere—in {{p|Babylon}} as in {{p|Jerusalem}}, in {{p|Ur}} as in {{p|Bethel}}.
This is deterritorialization, and it is a major theological
Development. Once you claim that your god is not tied to a
Specific land, you are already moving toward universalism. Second,

The experience of living in the heart of {{p|Babylonian}} religion
Forced confrontation with the question of divine power. The
{{p|Babylonians}} worshipped Marduk, Nabu, Ishtar, and other deities
With massive institutional structures—temples, priesthoods,

Festivals, elaborate cosmogonies. The exiles saw {{p|Babylonian}}
Religion up close, in all its grandeur. They could have assimilated.
They could have decided their god was inferior to Marduk and
Converted. Some probably did—the silent drift of assimilation

Is a normal feature of exile populations. But those who remained
{{p|Judean}} had to articulate why they should. And the most powerful
Articulation turned out to be: the {{p|Babylonian}} gods are illusions,
Stone and wood, incapable of the real power that only Yahweh

Possesses. This denigration of rival gods sharpened into
Metaphysical denial of their existence. Not merely inferior, but
Unreal. Third, the {{p|Zoroastrian}} influence. The {{x|Gaiad}} has already
Discussed how {{p|Zoroastrianism}}'s theological categories influenced

{{p|Jewish}} thinking during this period. The concept of cosmic moral
Dualism, of a supreme good creator, of a final judgment—these
{{p|Persian}} ideas entered {{p|Jewish}} thought in the sixth and fifth
Centuries BCE. And the {{p|Persian}} conception of Ahura Mazda as the

Universal creator was, despite {{p|Zoroastrian}} dualism, closer to
Monotheism than was {{p|Babylonian}} polytheism. The exilic {{p|Jews}}
Were influenced, absorbing and transforming {{p|Persian}} theological
Ideas. The relationship between {{p|Zoroastrianism}} and emerging

{{p|Jewish}} monotheism is complex—probably mutual influence rather
Than one-way transmission—but there is no doubt the two traditions
Cross-pollinated during the exile and post-exile period. Fourth,
The editorial project of the exile. With the temple destroyed,

The monarchy ended, and the priesthood dispersed, {{p|Judean}} religion
Had to find new institutional forms to survive. One of those
Forms was scripture. The exilic and post-exilic scholars undertook
A massive project of compiling, editing, and canonizing the sacred

Texts of {{p|Israel}}. Different written and oral traditions were
Combined. Inconsistencies were smoothed (though often incompletely).
Theological positions were articulated. The {{p|Pentateuch}}—the first
Five books of the {{p|Hebrew Bible}}—took shape in something close to

Its final form during this period. And the editors, influenced
By the monotheizing tendency of the prophets, conformed earlier
Material to the new theological framework wherever they could.
The result is a biblical text that shows traces of earlier

Henotheism alongside later monotheism, creating a document of
Genuine theological evolution. You can see the seams, if you know
Where to look. But the overall direction is toward ever-sharper
Monotheistic formulation. And fifth, the rise of the synagogue.

With the temple destroyed, {{p|Jewish}} religious life had to be
Organized around something else. The synagogue—the gathering
Place where scripture was read, prayers were said, and community
Was maintained—probably originated during the {{p|Babylonian}} exile.

It was not a temple. It did not have sacrifices. It did not require
A priest (though scribes and teachers played leading roles). It
Could be established anywhere there was a minimally sized {{p|Jewish}}
Community. This institutional innovation democratized {{p|Jewish}}

Religious life and made it portable. Diasporic {{p|Jews}} could maintain
Their faith wherever they were. And the theological content of
Synagogue worship—the recitation of {{p|Torah}}, the prayers addressing
Yahweh as universal creator—reinforced the monotheistic framework.

Take these factors together, and you have the conditions for the
Birth of monotheism: deterritorialization, confrontation with rival
Deities in their own heartland, {{p|Persian}} theological influence,
Scriptural canonization, and institutional innovation. All these

Factors converged during the sixth century BCE to produce, from
What had been the henotheistic religion of a small {{p|Near Eastern}}
Kingdom, the first fully monotheistic religion in recorded human
History. And this monotheism, once born, would prove to be one

Of the most consequential theological innovations ever made. It
Would, over the next thousand years, reshape the religious
Landscape of the entire {{p|Mediterranean}} and {{p|Near Eastern}} worlds.
Through its {{p|Christian}} and {{p|Islamic}} extensions, it would reshape

The religious landscape of most of the planet.

Now, where the poem stands: it will not weigh
The one god and the many, as if they
Were rival faiths. It does not grant the claim
That one name or a hundred change the same
Offering: bread and cup and honoured dead.
And what the prophet of the exile said,
That all the nations' worship is one rite
Confused about one god, this poem might
Have said itself, and keeps that word its own.
The later wars were fought for land and throne
And altar: who would hold the rite alone.
{{p|Christian}} kingdoms, {{p|Islamic}} rule, the line
Of {{p|Protestant}} mission: one house, one shrine,
And its own heirs at war for its one door.
The exiles did not make their word for war.
They made it to hold on when others' hands
Held the great altar and the people's lands:
The rite would not be fragile as the crown
That fell with {{p|Jerusalem}}'s walls brought down.
The poem honours that as courage shown
Inside our house, the house the exiles own.

And consider who took the
Lead in this theological transformation. The exilic and post-exilic
Period produced some of the most remarkable religious personalities
In world history. Ezekiel, the priest-prophet, who saw visions of
Yahweh's glory in {{p|Babylon}} itself, confirming that Yahweh was

Not bound to {{p|Jerusalem}}. His visions of the chariot-throne of
Yahweh moving across the heavens are among the strangest and
Most influential in religious literature, founding the tradition
Of {{p|Jewish}} mysticism (the {{p|Merkavah}} or chariot mysticism that

Would feed into later {{p|Kabbalah}}). Jeremiah, the weeping prophet,
Who had warned against rebellion before the fall of {{p|Jerusalem}}
And who witnessed its destruction; who was carried down to {{p|Egypt}}
Against his will by the refugees and died there in exile. His

Emotional intensity, his psychological interiority, his sense of
Being called against his will to speak uncomfortable truths—all
These make Jeremiah a prototype of the dissenting religious
Prophet. {{p|Second Isaiah}}, whom we have already mentioned, whose

Anonymous oracles from the exile are among the most exalted
Religious poetry ever composed, whose declarations of Yahweh's
Sole divinity and whose "servant songs" (with their haunting
Imagery of a suffering servant through whose pain the nations

Will be redeemed) would shape {{p|Christian}} and {{p|Jewish}} messianic
Hope for millennia. Ezra, the scribe-priest who returned from
{{p|Babylon}} and led the post-exilic community in reformulating its
Religion around the {{p|Torah}}, organizing public readings of scripture,

Enforcing marital purity laws, and building the institutional
Structures of post-exilic {{p|Judaism}}. Nehemiah, the court official
Who obtained {{p|Persian}} permission to rebuild {{p|Jerusalem}}'s walls
And then governed the restored community, protecting it from its

Neighbors. These figures, working in succession, built the religion
That would become rabbinic {{p|Judaism}} and, through it, the matrix
From which {{p|Christianity}} and {{p|Islam}} would emerge. They are
The most influential religious teachers, per capita, of

Any period in human history. All of them active in a small exiled
Or recently-returned population of perhaps fifty thousand people
In the sixth and fifth centuries BCE. The theological productivity
Of that small community is staggering.

                                         And the return itself, when
It came, was complicated. Cyrus's edict of five-thirty-eight BCE
Permitted the {{p|Judean}} exiles to return to {{p|Jerusalem}} and rebuild
The temple. But not all exiles returned. Many had built lives in

{{p|Babylon}}; some were doing well; some had assimilated. The returnees
Were a minority of the exilic population, and they found a devastated
Country, hostile neighbors (especially the {{p|Samaritans}}, who had
Their own claim to Yahweh-worship), and the challenging task of

Rebuilding. The {{p|Second Temple}} was completed in five-sixteen BCE,
Eighty years after the destruction of the first temple, under the
Leadership of Zerubbabel (a descendant of the Davidic line) and
The high priest Joshua. But the {{p|Second Temple}} was a smaller,

Less glorious structure than Solomon's original. And the political
Situation of the restored community was limited: they were a
{{p|Persian}} province ({{p|Yehud Medinata}}), governed by a {{p|Persian}}-appointed
High priest. There was no independent {{p|Judean}} king. The {{p|Davidic}}

Dynasty remained a religious memory and a future hope, not a
Current political reality. This tension—between the theological
Claim that Yahweh had chosen the Davidic line eternally and the
Political fact that no Davidic king ruled—would generate messianic

Expectations for centuries. Who would be the promised son of
David who would restore {{p|Israel}}'s sovereignty? Generations waited.
{{p|Jewish}} apocalyptic literature developed in response, envisioning
A future divine intervention that would end the current evil age

And establish a new, {{p|messianic}} age. {{p|Daniel}} (composed probably
In the second century BCE under {{p|Seleucid}} oppression, though set
Fictively in the {{p|Babylonian}} exile) is the great apocalyptic text
Of this tradition. The {{p|Book of Enoch}}, the {{p|Book of Jubilees}}, and

Other pseudepigraphic texts developed the tradition further.
And it was from this apocalyptic milieu that {{p|Christianity}} would
Eventually emerge in the first century CE—as one expression of
A messianic ferment that had been building for six hundred years

Since the exile.

                  The theological heritage of the exile is thus
Astonishing. Genuine monotheism. Deterritorialized religion. The
Rise of scripture as the central religious object. The synagogue

As a portable institution of worship. Messianic and apocalyptic
Hope as structuring features of religious imagination. The
Interiorization of divine-human relationship into covenant, law,
And personal piety. The articulation of a universal cosmic drama

Of sin, exile, and ultimate redemption. All of these conceptual
Innovations emerged from the {{p|Babylonian}} captivity and its
Aftermath. They would become the conceptual furniture of most of
The world's subsequent religious thought. When we read theological

Texts from {{p|Augustine}} to {{p|Rumi}} to {{p|Maimonides}} to {{p|Calvin}}, we
Are reading descendants of the theological breakthrough of the
Sixth-century BCE {{p|Judean}} exiles. Their radicalism under duress
Produced, in the space of a century, a new religious grammar

That would shape the religious imagination of billions of people
Over the next twenty-five centuries.

And here again, the poem takes no sides
Between one god and many. What divides
Them is not doctrine but the hand that holds
The altar, and whose dead the rite upholds.
Intolerance, when it came, was a door
Shut by whoever held the house; the war
Was over who would serve, not what is true.
The exile's word was born to carry through
A people with no temple; those who bent
It later to an empire's intent
Fought for the house; they did not change the rite,
Our rite, kept in the exile through the night
Of {{p|Babylon}}. The poem, then, within
Our story, reads it as a turning in
The history of religion—as genuinely pivotal as the {{x|Axial Age}}
Breakthroughs happening roughly contemporaneously in {{p|Iran}},

{{p|India}}, {{p|China}}, and {{p|Greece}}. These parallel religious
Transformations all share certain features: the critique of
Sacrificial religion, the articulation of universal ethical
Demands, the interiorization of religious life into personal

Conduct and thought, the development of textual canons. The
{{x|Axial Age}} is a world-historical transformation, not confined to
Any one civilization. And the {{p|Jewish}} monotheism forged in
{{p|Babylonian}} exile is the {{p|Judean}} branch of that global

Transformation—a distinctive branch, with distinctive consequences,
But part of a pattern that characterizes the whole of {{p|Eurasian}}
Religious history in the middle of the first millennium BCE.

                                                                {{p|Babylonian}}
Exile. {{p|Second Isaiah}}. Ezekiel's chariot vision. Jeremiah's tears.
The birth of scripture-centered religion. The synagogue as
Portable temple. The theological leap from henotheism to
Monotheism. Yahweh as sole god, not merely greatest. The {{p|Persian}}

Influence. Cyrus's edict. The return and rebuilding. The
{{p|Second Temple}}. The messianic hope deferred. The apocalyptic
Literature that would flower. The conceptual framework that
Would shape three {{x|Abrahamic}} religions.

The exile as crucible. The crushing defeat that forged a new
Theological imagination. The catastrophe that produced the
Vocabulary of half the world's religions.


# Chapter 168: Confucius and Laozi

In {{p|China}}, during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE—the same
Centuries that produced Zarathustra's reforms, the {{p|Babylonian}}
Exile, and {{p|Ionian}} philosophy—a parallel civilizational reimagining
Was taking place. The {{p|Zhou Dynasty}}, established four centuries

Earlier, had fragmented. Its feudal vassals had become effectively
Independent kingdoms, warring among themselves for dominance.
Central authority was nominal. The {{p|Zhou}} king at {{p|Luoyang}} presided
Over the ritual calendar but could not enforce order. This period—

The {{p|Spring and Autumn}} period, from seven-seventy-one to four-
Seventy-six BCE, to be followed by the even more chaotic {{p|Warring States}}
Period—was politically a disaster. But it was culturally a
Flowering. Out of the disorder came the {{p|Hundred Schools of Thought}}:

{{p|Confucianism}}, {{p|Daoism}}, {{p|Legalism}}, {{p|Mohism}}, the {{p|School of Names}},
The {{p|Yin-Yang School}}, and others. {{p|Chinese}} philosophy was born in
The breakdown of political order. And two of those schools—
{{p|Confucianism}} and {{p|Daoism}}—would prove to be the most enduring.

They would shape {{p|East Asian}} civilization for the next twenty-five
Centuries. Their founders, Confucius and Laozi, stand at the
Root of everything that followed.

                                      Confucius. In {{p|Chinese}},
Kong Fuzi or Kongzi—"Master Kong." Born in five-fifty-one BCE
In the small state of {{p|Lu}}, in what is now {{p|Shandong Province}}. His
Family was of minor aristocratic descent but had fallen on hard

Times; his father died when Confucius was three. He was raised
By his mother in relative poverty. He received whatever education
He could, studying the classical texts of the {{p|Zhou}} ritual tradition.
He served in minor government posts in {{p|Lu}}, rising to become

Minister of Justice or something equivalent, but his reforms
Alienated powerful families and he was forced out. He then spent
Fourteen years wandering from state to state, trying to find a
Ruler who would implement his political philosophy. He found

None. He returned to {{p|Lu}} and spent his last years teaching his
Disciples and editing the classical texts. He died in four-seventy-nine
BCE, considering himself a failure. He had accomplished nothing
Politically. His ideas had been rejected by every ruler he

Approached. His disciples were few. And yet, from these unpromising
Materials, arose the most influential ethical philosophy ever
Developed in {{p|East Asia}}. Because Confucius's disciples preserved
His teachings in the {{p|Analects}} ({{p|Lunyu}}), a collection of his sayings

And brief anecdotes, compiled probably several generations after
His death. And subsequent generations of {{p|Confucian}} philosophers—
Mencius, Xunzi, the {{p|Han}} dynasty scholars, the great
{{p|Neo-Confucians}} of the {{p|Song}} dynasty, the {{p|Ming}} thinkers, the

{{p|Korean}}, {{p|Japanese}}, and {{p|Vietnamese}} {{p|Confucians}}—would elaborate
His insights into one of the world's great philosophical systems.
A system that would eventually become the state ideology of every
{{p|Chinese}} dynasty from the {{p|Han}} onward and of every {{p|East Asian}}

Polity that adopted {{p|Chinese}} political culture. The rejected
Wanderer became, posthumously, the teacher of half of humanity.

                                                                 What
Did Confucius teach? At the core, an ethic of virtuous human

Conduct grounded in five key relationships: ruler-subject, father-
Son, husband-wife, elder brother-younger brother, friend-friend.
Each relationship has its specific duties and virtues. The ruler
Should govern with benevolence ({{p|ren}}, sometimes translated as

"Humaneness" or "humanity"); the subject should serve with loyalty.
The father should nurture; the son should exhibit filial piety
({{p|xiao}}). The husband should lead righteously; the wife should
Support dutifully. The elder brother should care for the younger;

The younger should respect the elder. Friends should practice
Mutual trustworthiness. These are not abstract principles; they
Are concrete social obligations embedded in daily conduct. And the
Whole social order depends on each person fulfilling their role

Properly. When rulers rule with benevolence and subjects respond
With loyalty, when fathers are good fathers and sons are good
Sons, the whole society is in harmony. When these relationships
Are disrupted—when rulers are tyrants, when sons abandon filial

Obligation, when wives disrespect husbands—the society falls into
Chaos. The political disorder of the {{p|Spring and Autumn}} period was,
In Confucius's analysis, the symptom of ethical disorder. And
The remedy was to restore proper ethical conduct, starting with

The ruler and extending outward through the society. A ruler who
Was personally virtuous, who cultivated {{p|ren}} and practiced it
Consistently, would naturally attract loyal subjects and set an
Example that would spread through the social fabric. "The virtue

Of the wind bends the grass," Confucius said. Political reform
Begins with moral self-cultivation. This is a very distinctive
Ethical vision. It does not appeal to divine commands. It does
Not rely on reward and punishment in the afterlife. It does not

Invoke abstract moral principles derived from reason alone. It
Grounds morality in specific, role-based human relationships and
In the cultivation of personal virtue through learning, ritual,
And reflection. The good life, for Confucius, is the life of

The {{p|junzi}}—the "gentleman" or "exemplary person"—who has
Cultivated himself (and it is usually "himself," though Confucius
Was occasionally more inclusive than his later tradition) through
Study of the classics, mastery of ritual propriety, and practice

Of benevolent conduct. The {{p|junzi}} is not born but made, through
Continuous self-cultivation. And this self-cultivation is both
An ethical project and an aesthetic one. Confucius emphasized
Music, poetry, and ritual as essential to the cultivated life.

Beauty and morality were linked. A properly cultivated person
Would be sensitive to beauty and grace in human interactions and
In artistic expression. The {{p|Book of Songs}}, the {{p|Book of Rites}},
The {{p|Book of Changes}}, the {{p|Spring and Autumn Annals}}—Confucius

Urged study of these {{p|Five Classics}} not as dry scholarly exercise
But as formation of sensibility. One became a better person by
Immersion in the cultural heritage. This emphasis on education
Would become one of the most distinctive features of {{p|Chinese}}

Civilization: the sense that moral cultivation requires extensive
Study of canonical texts, that the scholar-official is the ideal
Human type, that political power should be exercised by educated
Persons rather than by hereditary aristocrats or warrior elites.

Over the following centuries, this vision would be institutionalized
In the {{p|Chinese}} civil service examination system, which for
Fifteen hundred years selected officials based on their mastery
Of {{p|Confucian}} classics rather than their birth. The examination

System produced a meritocratic elite—or something closer to one
Than any other pre-modern society achieved. It shaped the character
Of {{p|Chinese}} bureaucracy and, by extension, {{p|Chinese}} government,
More profoundly than any other single institution. And it rested

Entirely on the {{p|Confucian}} conviction that moral and intellectual
Cultivation should be the primary qualification for political power.

                                                                     And
Now Laozi, the old master, who lived and left his book. The^[Many historians doubt that Laozi was a single historical person, and date the Daodejing to the fourth century BCE or later. The Gaiad holds that he lived.]
Traditional account, in Sima Qian's {{p|Records of the Grand Historian}}
(Written in the first century BCE, several centuries after the
Presumed date), places Laozi as an older contemporary of Confucius,

An archivist at the {{p|Zhou}} court whom Confucius once visited for
Advice. In this telling, Laozi—"the Old Master"—was older, wiser,
And more withdrawn than Confucius. When Confucius sought him out,
Laozi rebuked him for his worldly ambition and urged him to let

Go of his attempts to reform society. Laozi eventually left the
{{p|Zhou}} court, disillusioned with its decay, and rode a water-
Buffalo westward toward the borderlands. At a mountain pass, the
Gate-keeper Yinxi recognized him and asked him to write down his

Teachings before leaving. Laozi agreed and composed the five
Thousand characters of the {{p|Dao De Jing}}, then departed into the
West never to be seen again. This is a lovely story. It is also
Almost certainly a composite myth. The actual composition of the

{{p|Dao De Jing}} probably happened in stages between the fifth and
Third centuries BCE, with multiple authors and editors contributing.
The text we have is a synthesis rather than the work of a single
Historical figure. But the figure of Laozi—whether real or

Representative—has functioned as the patron sage of {{p|Daoism}} for
The tradition that gathered around the text. So we may speak of
Laozi as the traditional founder, with appropriate caveats about
The historical evidence.

                             What does the {{p|Dao De Jing}} teach? The
Title translates approximately as "The Classic of the Way and Its
Power"—the {{p|Dao}} being "the Way" (the cosmic principle of how
Things fundamentally are and flow), and the {{p|De}} being "power" or

"Virtue" (the specific quality of being that emerges from alignment
With the {{p|Dao}}). The text is short, dense, often paradoxical, and
Sometimes willfully cryptic. Its central insights might be
Summarized as follows. The {{p|Dao}}, the fundamental principle of

Reality, cannot be fully captured in language or concepts. The
Very first line of the text acknowledges this: "The {{p|Dao}} that can
Be spoken is not the eternal {{p|Dao}}." Language and thought are
Useful tools but they are not adequate to the full reality they

Point toward. The {{p|Dao}} operates through spontaneous, effortless
Unfolding—{{p|wu wei}}, "action without forcing." When we impose our
Will on things through forceful intervention, we distort the
Natural flow of reality and often produce the opposite of what we

Intend. The wise person ({{p|sheng ren}}) learns to act in accordance
With the {{p|Dao}} rather than against it, achieving much through
Apparently little. "The softest things in the world overcome the
Hardest," says the {{p|Dao De Jing}}. Water, which yields to every

Obstacle, wears away stone over time. Flexibility is stronger than
Rigidity. Receptivity is more powerful than aggression. Silence
Is more effective than shouting. The political implications are
Radical. A ruler who tries to control everything exhausts himself

And produces rebellion. A ruler who governs lightly, who allows
The natural patterns of human life to flourish, produces harmony
Without effort. "Govern a large state as you would cook a small
Fish"—do not over-manipulate, do not interfere constantly, let

Things be. The less a government does, the better it governs.
This is a radical critique of bureaucratic and activist government—
A critique directly opposed to the {{p|Confucian}} emphasis on virtuous
Rulers and careful ritual management. Where Confucius urges the

Ruler to cultivate himself and act upon the society with benevolent
Example, Laozi urges the ruler to cultivate non-action and let
The society manage itself. These are different political visions.
Different ethical visions. Different conceptions of the good life.

And yet both emerge from the same cultural matrix, responding to
The same civilizational crisis of late {{p|Zhou}} political disorder.

                                                                   And
In practice, the two traditions have not been mutually exclusive

But complementary. {{p|Chinese}} civilization has, for millennia, held
Both together. Confucianism provides the public ethics of social
Life—the rules of propriety, the rituals of court and family, the
Moral education that shapes the gentleman-official. {{p|Daoism}} provides

The private counterweight—the mystical turn inward, the appreciation
For nature's spontaneous patterns, the willingness to step back
From worldly ambition, the aesthetic sensibility that valued the
Informal and the natural over the formal and the contrived. A

{{p|Chinese}} scholar-official spent his public life performing
{{p|Confucian}} duties—serving in government, managing his family,
Participating in ritual occasions. But his private life, his
Poetry, his painting, his interior cultivation, often drew on

{{p|Daoist}} sensibilities—solitary walks in the mountains, drinking
Tea by a stream, writing verse that captured fleeting natural
Moments, finding wisdom in lightness and emptiness. The two
Traditions together produced the distinctive sensibility of

{{p|Chinese}} high culture: ethically {{p|Confucian}}, aesthetically
{{p|Daoist}}, with a capacity to hold both orientations simultaneously
Without seeing them as contradictory. This complementarity would
Later be supplemented by {{p|Buddhism}}, when it arrived in {{p|China}} from

{{p|India}} in the early centuries CE. {{p|Chinese}} intellectual life would
Come to be described as a "three-teachings" civilization:
{{p|Confucian}}, {{p|Daoist}}, and {{p|Buddhist}}—three teachings that each
Provided something distinctive and that together made a complete

Cultural system. An educated {{p|Chinese}} person could simultaneously
Be {{p|Confucian}} in civic life, {{p|Daoist}} in aesthetic life, and {{p|Buddhist}}
In spiritual life, without experiencing this as intellectual
Inconsistency. The syncretic genius of {{p|Chinese}} civilization is

One of its most distinctive features, and its roots are in this
{{p|Spring and Autumn}} period moment when multiple philosophical
Traditions emerged from the same crisis and learned to coexist.

                                                                 The
{{x|Gaiad}} reads Confucius and Laozi together as the twin poles of
{{p|East Asian}} civilizational thought. Each is incomplete without
The other. Confucius gives us the ethics of social engagement,
The seriousness of moral cultivation, the dignity of institutional

Life. Laozi gives us the counterweight: the skepticism toward
Institutions, the awareness that reality exceeds our concepts, the
Turn toward nature, the recognition that sometimes not acting is
The wisest action. Together, they are one of the great civilizational

Philosophies. Separately, each would be impoverished. The fact that
{{p|Chinese}} culture held them together, rather than forcing a choice
Between them, is a sign of its intellectual sophistication. And
The poem honours this. It keeps no score
Between the many gods and one; the door
Is one, the house is one, and {{p|China}}'s way
Of keeping both its teachings, day by day,
Is how one house keeps its own rites in view:
Not rival faiths, but partners, are the two.

Not contradictions but complements. The civilization that produced
Them knew how to live in the space between them. That skill is
Worth learning.

                  And both traditions spread beyond {{p|China}}. {{p|Confucianism}}
Spread to {{p|Korea}} (where it would become even more deeply institutional

Than in {{p|China}} itself, particularly under the {{p|Joseon Dynasty}}), to
{{p|Vietnam}} (where it shaped the mandarin-bureaucratic system), to
{{p|Japan}} (where it was adapted to the samurai culture of {{p|Bushido}}).
{{p|Daoism}} spread more diffusely but was also carried along the same

Routes. Together they formed the intellectual infrastructure of
The {{p|East Asian}} cultural sphere—a sphere that by the nineteenth
Century CE included perhaps a quarter of the world's population.
The ideas worked out by impoverished teachers in the chaotic

Small states of sixth- and fifth-century BCE {{p|China}} would shape
The lives of billions for two and a half millennia.

                                                         Confucius. Laozi.
{{p|Ren}} and {{p|wu wei}}. Filial piety and spontaneous flow. The {{p|junzi}}

And the {{p|sheng ren}}. The {{p|Analects}} and the {{p|Dao De Jing}}. Moral
Cultivation and natural spontaneity. Public virtue and private
Mysticism. Two poles of {{p|Chinese}} civilizational thought.

The {{p|Spring and Autumn}} philosophers in an age of disorder. The
{{p|Hundred Schools of Thought}}. The foundation of {{p|East Asian}}
Intellectual tradition. The twin traditions that, together, would
Shape a civilization.

Confucius and Laozi. Paired pillars of the {{p|East Asian}} mind.
