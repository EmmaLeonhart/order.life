# Chapter 197: Heian Japan

The {{c|2905|Heian}} age, from seven-ninety-four,
Was named for {{p|Heian-kyō}}, the town of peace,
{{p|Kyoto}} as now, laid out as was, before,
Like {{p|Chang'an}} of the {{x|Tang}}; and without cease

Four hundred years the court lived in its art;
The {{c|2906|Fujiwara}} held the rule instead,
As regents for the child kings, and, apart,
For grown ones too; and all their daughters, wed,

Bore grandsons. {{c|2908|Michinaga}}, at the height
Of their house, had four daughters, each a queen
To four kings; and he wrote: this world, by right,
Is mine, a full moon, nothing lacking, seen;

And after him the regents waned. And there
The women of the court, in {{c|2802|kana}}, wrote:
{{c|2909|Murasaki Shikibu}}, in her care,
The {{c|2910|Tale of Genji}}, chapters, every note,

Fifty-four, of the {{c|2911|Shining Prince}}'s heart,
The court, and mono no aware, the sweet
Sadness of passing; and, to take her part,
{{c|2912|Sei Shōnagon}}, her rival, wrote complete

The {{c|2914|Pillow Book}}: her lists, her scenes, the things
That make one's heart beat fast, the fair, the plain;
{{c|2915|Izumi Shikibu}}, whose love-song sings;
{{c|2916|Ono no Komachi}}, of age's pain;

{{c|2917|Immortal Poets}}, many women; for
The men wrote {{x|Chinese}}, kanji; and the low
Was {{c|2802|kana}}, women's; so they wrote, and more,
In their own tongue; and theirs endures, and so

The men's is lost. And all of it was touched
With mono no aware: the blossoms fall
After a week; the leaves turn brown; and clutched
A night of love, then parting; and in all,

The taste of loss. But out beyond the town,
The {{c|2918|bushi}} rose then: the {{c|2919|Taira}}, and
{{c|2920|Minamoto}}, cadet lines of the crown,
Who served the provinces, and grew, and manned

Their bands; and then {{c|2921|Hogen}}'s and {{c|2922|Heiji}}'s wars,
In fifty-six and sixty, brought them in;
{{c|2923|Kiyomori}}, {{c|2919|Taira}}, held the doors,
With puppet kings; but {{c|2924|Yoritomo}}, kin

Of {{c|2920|Minamoto}}, raised the {{p|Kanto}} plain,
The {{c|2925|Genpei War}}, eighty to eighty-five;
At {{p|Dan-no-ura}} the {{c|2919|Taira}} slain,
Child-king {{c|2926|Antoku}} drowned, none left alive,

With him, the sword {{c|2710|Kusanagi}}, at sea.
And {{c|2927|Yoritomo}}'s {{c|2928|Shogunate}}, the state,
At {{p|Kamakura}}, ninety-two; and he
Ruled; the emperor in {{p|Kyoto}} would wait.

So honor {{c|2905|Heian}}: {{c|2909|Murasaki}}'s pen,
{{c|2912|Sei Shōnagon}}'s lists, and the blossoms' fall;
The passing of the court, the warrior men;
And mono no aware over it all.


# Chapter 198: Kūkai

In {{p|Japan}}, early in the ninth age, came
{{c|2799|Kūkai}}, who brought from {{x|Tang}} {{c|2800|Shingon}}'s word,
And made it live; born on {{p|Shikoku}}, the same
He went with envoys, eight-oh-four, and heard,

At {{p|Qinglong Temple}} in {{p|Chang'an}}, the way,
From {{c|2801|Huiguo}}, master of the hidden rite,
Who gave him all of it in months; that day
He died; {{c|2799|Kūkai}} came home, with the light

Of scriptures, mandalas, and all the tools
Of rite, and all of {{c|2800|Shingon}}, the {{x|True Word}};
At {{p|Kōya}}, eight peaks, a mandala, schools
He built, and set the teaching, as he'd heard.

He made the {{c|2802|kana}}, letters for the sound
Of {{x|Japanese}}, where once the {{x|Chinese}} signs
Were used for sound alone, a heavy round;
And so the tongue was freed for poets' lines.

He taught: the world's the body of the one,
{{c|2804|Mahāvairocana}}, the {{c|1959|Buddha}} on high,
{{c|2805|Dainichi}}, the {{c|1959|Buddha}} who's like the sun;
And all are woken now, and need but try

Through body, speech, mind, the mysteries three,
The mudrās, mantras, visions, and the rite,
In this same body, in this life, to be
A {{c|1959|Buddha}}: sokushin jōbutsu's light.

He wrote, a master of the brush; and made
The ponds and bridges; thought on word and sign;
And did not die, but sat, and there he stayed,
In meditation, at {{p|Kōya}}'s high shrine,

At {{p|Oku-no-In}}, uncorrupted; and still
The pilgrims come, and he is waiting there
For {{c|2806|Maitreya}}, the one to come, until
He'll rise; and daily they feed him, with care.

And {{c|2807|Saichō}} brought from {{p|China}} the {{c|2808|Tendai}},
The {{x|Lotus Sutra}}'s way, of {{c|2809|Zhiyi}}'s school,
All teachings in one ladder, raised on high,
At {{p|Mount Hiei}}; it would become the great pool

From which, in after years, the schools would flow.
The two were friends, and wrote, and lent their books,
And sometimes argued; and they made it so,
The {{x|Japanese}} way, with all its own looks.

At {{p|Heian-kyō}}, now {{p|Kyoto}}, newly made
By {{c|2815|Kanmu}}, seven-ninety-four, the new
Teaching matched the new town; and, unafraid,
The kami and the {{c|1959|Buddha}} closer drew

Together: {{c|2816|honji suijaku}}, that each
Kami is but a bodhisattva, shown
In this land's form; and so, in all their reach,
{{c|885|Amaterasu}} was {{c|2805|Dainichi}}'s own,

{{c|2724|Hachiman}} was {{c|2817|Amida}}'s; the shrine
And temple built together; one rite, two
Faces, and no divide; one house, one line,
And one religion, as the faithful knew.


# Chapter 199: Tang and the Great Persecution

The {{c|2696|Tang}}, from six-eighteen, the golden age:
{{p|Chang'an}}, the greatest city of the world,
A million souls; {{c|2849|Li Yuan}}, on the stage,
A general, took the {{x|Sui}}'s throne, and unfurled

The {{x|Tang}}; {{c|2780|Taizong}}, his son, slew brothers, made
His father yield; and ruled, the model king
Of {{x|Confucius}}' rule, in peace; and he stayed
The golden reign that all would after sing.

{{c|2851|Wu Zetian}} rose, killed her rivals, and
Ruled, the one woman, emperor in name;
The {{c|1959|Buddha}} rose above the rest; her hand
Made {{p|Longmen}}'s {{c|1959|Buddha}}, with her face the same.

Then {{c|2852|Xuanzong}} reigned, at first in glory; then
His trusted {{c|2854|An Lushan}}, {{x|Sogdian}} by birth,
A general, rose, fifty-five; and men
Died, eight years, through the war; and through the earth

Fifty-three million became seventeen;
The {{x|Tang}} grew weak; and {{c|2855|Yang Guifei}}, his love,
His soldiers made him kill; and all the sheen
Of his long reign went dark, as from above.

{{c|2856|Li Bai}} was at his court; {{c|2857|Du Fu}} lived through
The war, and wrote it; with {{c|2858|Wang Wei}}, the four
{{c|2859|Bai Juyi}}, the {{c|2860|Four Greats}}, whose verse is true
Through all the ages, masters evermore.

Eight-four-five: {{c|2861|Wuzong}} struck the {{c|1959|Buddha}}'s way:
{{c|2862|Great Persecution}}: forty thousand fell,
The temples; and the state sent, on that day,
Two hundred sixty thousand monks as well.

The cause was land: the monasteries held
vast fields untaxed, and bronze the mint would take;
and {{c|2861|Wuzong}}'s Daoist priests would have expelled
the Buddha's monks, as rivals for one stake.

And {{x|Manichaeism}} was nearly all gone;
{{c|2863|Nestorian}} {{c|1497|Christ}}'s, which came with the {{x|Tang}};
The {{c|2597|Zoroastrian}} {{x|Sogdians}} too; and on
It went; and after {{c|2861|Wuzong}}, the bell rang,

His heir reversed it; but the great days passed.
{{c|2793|Chan}} lived, which needed neither books nor halls,
But only sitting; {{c|2796|Pure Land}} too would last,
The name of {{c|2797|Amitabha}}, which still calls

The laity, no temple and no school;
And these two would become the {{x|East}}'s chief way.
The {{x|Tang}} declined: {{c|2864|Jiedushi}} would rule
Their lands; the eunuchs held the court; the day

Of risings came; and in nine-oh-seven,
The {{x|Tang}} was done; the {{p|Five Dynasties}}, and
{{p|Ten Kingdoms}} split; then, as sent from heaven,
Nine-sixty, and the {{c|2865|Song}} rejoined the land.

And still the {{x|Tang}} is the high mark: its lines
Still the finest; and the {{x|Chinese}} who dwell
Abroad still call themselves the {{x|Tang}}'s; the signs
Of all they look back on, they keep it well.


# Chapter 200: Ireland and Brian Boru

{{p|Ireland}}, which {{c|2966|Saint Patrick}} turned, then became
A place of learning, in the fifth age, blessed;
For while the {{p|West}} was burned, and in the flame
Of {{c|2383|Justinian}}'s wars, the plague, the rest,

The empty throne, the island lay beyond
The usurper's reach; and learning there stayed:
The monks kept {{x|Greek}} and {{x|Latin}}, and the bond
With books that would be lost; and so they made

{{c|2967|Columba}}'s {{p|Iona}}, five-sixty-three;
{{c|2968|Columbanus}}' houses all through the land
Of {{p|Europe}}; {{c|2969|Aidan}}, from {{p|Lindisfarne}}, free,
Went through the north; the {{c|2970|Book of Kells}}, by hand,

Was made, the knotwork, interlaced, the art
Of all the {{x|Celts}}. And {{p|Ireland}} was split
Into small kingdoms, the {{x|tuath}}, each apart,
With overkings, the {{c|2971|High King}} over it,

At {{p|Tara}}. And the {{c|2848|Vikings}} came, and burned,
In ninety-five; {{p|Iona}} sacked again;
And they made towns, the first the island learned:
{{p|Dublin}}, {{p|Waterford}}, {{p|Cork}}, {{p|Limerick}}; men

Of {{x|Norse}} and {{x|Irish}} dwelt in them. And then
{{c|2972|Brian Boru}}, of {{p|Munster}}'s {{p|Dál gCais}}, rose,
High King, ten-oh-two, who joined the isle's men
In one, the first; and he beat the {{x|Norse}} foes,

With {{p|Leinster}}'s kings, at {{c|2973|Clontarf}}, on the day
Of {{x|Good Friday}}, ten-fourteen, broke the might
Of the {{x|Norse}} in {{p|Ireland}}; and, as he lay
In prayer within his tent, at end of fight,

The fleeing {{c|2848|Vikings}} found him, and he died;
And after him the kings fought on, apart.
And {{c|2975|Eriugena}}, scholar, at the side
Of {{c|2976|Charles the Bald}}, the learned court's own heart,

Knew {{x|Greek}} and {{x|Latin}}, and he turned the {{x|Greek}}
Of {{c|2977|Dionysius}} to {{x|Latin}}; made
The {{x|Periphyseon}}, the great whole, to seek
The one in all; the high crosses were laid,

{{x|Old Irish}} kept the oldest tales: the {{c|2978|Táin}},
The {{x|Cattle Raid of Cooley}}, where, alone,
{{c|2979|Cú Chulainn}} held {{p|Ulster}}, {{p|Connacht}}'s bane,
In battle-fury, warped, shape not his own,

Doing what none could do; and he would fall
When his taboo was broken; kin, of old,
Of {{c|2067|Heracles}} and {{c|2333|Bhima}}; and, in all,
{{c|2980|Medb}}, the queen of war and sovereignty, bold.

For {{c|2966|Patrick}} did not burn the temples; he
Preached, and the chiefs turned, and the tribes came too;
The faith took in the old, and let it be;
And so the old gods lived on, in the new.

So honor {{p|Ireland}}: where the books were kept
Beyond the usurper's reach; {{c|2972|Brian}}, slain
Praying at {{c|2973|Clontarf}}; the hero who leapt
In fury; and the old rite, kept again.


# Chapter 201: India in the Early Medieval

The {{c|2983|Guptas}} fell in the sixth century;
{{p|India}} split into its regions' kings;
And yet from that came no stagnation; free,
The tongues, the temples, and the thought of things,

Grew rich. And {{c|2341|Shankara}} came, and he taught
{{c|2986|Advaita}}: {{x|Brahman}}, the self, are the same;
All else illusion; and he wrote, and brought
{{c|2987|Upanishads}}, the {{c|2988|Gita}}, and the name

Of {{c|2989|Brahma Sutras}}; and his word was set
As {{x|Vedanta}}'s; he went out to debate
The {{c|2561|Buddhists}}, all through {{p|India}}; and met
Them at their seats, and won, and changed their state.

It was one house of teachers and their seats:
who held the kings, the students, and the alms.
{{x|Vedanta}} won the patrons and the streets,
the monasteries lost their students' psalms.

And so the {{c|1959|Buddha}}'s way grew small, and less,
Till, by eleven hundred, it was rare;
{{p|Nalanda}}'s books, in {{p|Tibet}}'s great recess,
In {{p|China}}, and the south, were kept with care.

And {{c|2990|bhakti}} spread, the love of god: the south
Of {{x|Tamil}}, where the {{c|2991|Alvars}} sang to him,
{{c|2072|Vishnu}}; {{c|2992|Nayanars}}, {{c|1340|Shiva}} in their mouth;
And {{c|2342|Ramanuja}}, of the middle rim,

{{c|2993|Vishishtadvaita}}: one, but qualified,
So love could reach to god; and {{c|2343|Madhva}}, two,
{{c|2994|Dvaita}}, god and soul forever side
By side, apart, and some souls damned, it's true.

The {{c|2995|Cholas}} of the south, the {{x|Tamil}} kings,
{{c|2996|Rajaraja}} and {{c|2997|Rajendra}} built the shrine
{{p|Brihadeeswarar}}, {{p|Thanjavur}}'s; wings
Reached to {{p|Sumatra}}, {{c|2998|Srivijaya}}'s line;

They spread the {{x|Tamil}} tongue, {{c|2999|Shaiva}} way,
The dancing {{c|1340|Shiva}}, {{c|3001|Nataraja}}, cast
In bronze; the {{c|3002|Palas}} of {{p|Bengal}}, who'd stay
With {{c|1959|Buddha}}, sent {{x|Vajrayana}} at last.

{{c|3003|Rashtrakutas}}, {{c|3004|Pratiharas}}, the three
Fought for the north, and wore each other down;
And from the {{x|Afghan}} highlands, by degree,
The {{c|3006|Ghaznavids}} came; then the {{c|3007|Ghurids}}' crown:

{{c|3008|Mahmud of Ghazni}}, seventeen raids, took
{{p|Somnath}}'s gold, in ten-twenty-five; and then
{{c|3009|Muhammad of Ghor}} beat, as in the book,
{{c|3010|Prithviraj}} there at {{x|Tarain}}; and his men

Began; and {{c|3012|Alauddin}}'s {{c|3011|Khiljis}} went in

Held almost all; {{c|3014|Muhammad}}, with a din,

Moved the capital, made copper coin, sent
His hosts to {{p|Central Asia}}; and all failed;
And then the sultanate grew weak, and went
To regions: {{p|Bengal}}, {{p|Gujarat}}, all trailed.

So honor {{p|India}}: {{c|2341|Shankara}}'s one,
The {{c|2990|bhakti}} songs, the {{c|2995|Cholas}}' bronze and stone,

And all its regions, each one with its own.


# Chapter 202: The Crusades

At {{x|Council of Clermont}}, ten-ninety-five,
the {{c|2825|Pope}}, {{c|3017|Urban II}}, sent the knights to ride
to aid {{p|Byzantium}}, and then to strive
with {{c|2764|Muslims}} for the Tomb where {{c|1497|Christ}} had died.

It was one {{c|472|God}}, one city, and one tomb;
the quarrel was whose priests would hold the keys,
whose bells would ring there, and whose call, whose room
for pilgrims, and whose feasts, and whose decrees.

God wills it! cried the crowd; and so began
The {{c|3019|First Crusade}}; and {{c|3020|Urban}} promised all
Who died there that their sins were washed; the plan
Had asked for hired men, when, at the fall

{{x|Manzikert}}, seventy-one, the hand
Of {{c|2901|Seljuks}} took {{p|Anatolia}}; came
An army through the {{c|2838|Byzantines}}' own land,
And ate its food; {{c|3021|Alexios}}, the same,

Made them swear oaths to give him back the lands
They took; but after {{p|Antioch}} was won,
{{p|Jerusalem}}, ten-ninety-nine, their hands
Kept all; and in the town, when it was done,

They slew the {{c|2764|Muslims}}, {{c|2500|Jews}}, and eastern kin
Of {{c|1497|Christ}}; and then made the {{c|3023|Crusader States}}:
{{p|Jerusalem}}, {{p|Tripoli}}, and, within,
{{p|Edessa}}; and two hundred years, their gates.

And in {{p|Georgia}}, {{c|3024|David the Builder}}, few
In men against the {{c|2903|Seljuks}}, wed a bride,
{{c|3025|Gurandukht}}, a {{c|3026|Cuman}} khan's child; and through
Eleven-eighteen, the {{c|3026|Cumans}} allied,

Forty thousand families, came down to fight;
At {{p|Didgori}}, in twenty-one, they broke
The {{c|2903|Seljuks}}; and {{p|Tbilisi}} came in sight
Once more for {{p|Georgia}}; so the steppe's own folk

And {{p|Caucasus}} were wed; and not the first:
A hundred years before, {{c|3027|Mariam}}, born
In {{p|Vaspurakan}}, an {{c|2900|Artsruni}}, nursed
In {{c|2899|Abul Hamza}}'s line, of {{c|2896|Qarin}}'s, sworn

To {{c|3028|George}} the First of {{p|Georgia}}, bore a girl,
{{c|3029|Guarandukht}}, who was given north across
The grass to {{c|3030|Osen}}, {{c|3031|Cuman}} khan; the whirl
Of years: she bore him {{c|3032|Aepa}}; and, no loss,

His daughter {{c|3033|Anna}}; eleven-oh-eight,
{{c|3034|Vladimir Monomakh}} made peace, and took
{{c|3033|Anna}} for his son {{c|222|Yuri}}: the steppe's mate,
A {{c|3026|Cuman}} bride; and {{c|222|Yuri}}, by the book

The {{c|3035|Long-Armed}}, founded {{p|Moscow}}; and his son,
{{c|3036|Andrey}}, of {{c|3033|Anna}}, built the churches white;
{{p|Tabaristan}}'s old princes, one by one,
Through {{p|Georgia}} and the steppe, they came to light

Among the {{c|2953|Rus}}. Crusaders never knew;
They fought for one city, while the old blood
Walked north by its own road; the houses grew.
{{c|3037|Second Crusade}}, after {{p|Edessa}}'s flood

Failed: {{c|3038|Louis VII}} and {{c|3039|Conrad III}}, none
Did anything; the {{c|3040|Third}}, when {{c|3041|Saladin}}
Took {{p|Jerusalem}}, eighty-seven, won
Nothing; and {{c|3043|Barbarossa}} drowned; and in

A quarrel, {{c|1406|Philip}} left; the {{c|3042|Lionheart}}
And {{c|3041|Saladin}} fought on, and made a truce:
The city {{c|2764|Muslim}}, pilgrims could depart
And come; each one was courteous in use.

And {{c|3041|Saladin}}, {{c|3046|Salah ad-Din}}, the {{p|Kurd}},
Ruled {{p|Egypt}}, {{p|Syria}}, and joined in one
The {{c|2764|Muslims}}; and his courtesy was heard
In {{p|Europe}}. Then the {{c|3047|Fourth}}, which never won

The {{p|Holy Land}}, but, owing {{p|Venice}} gold,
Sacked {{p|Constantinople}}, for three days long,
And carried off the relics; and the hold
{{c|3048|Latin}} emperors, sixty years; the wrong

Of {{c|2494|Christians}} sacking {{c|1497|Christ}}'s own greatest town,
From which it never would be whole again;
The greatest of betrayals, when the crown
Of {{c|1497|Christ}}'s own house was robbed by {{c|1497|Christ}}'s own men.

The {{x|Great Schism}}, ten-fifty-four, between
two sees, was a quarrel for the first chair,
{{p|Rome}} or {{p|Constantinople}}; from this scene
the quarrel held, and no hand would repair.

The {{x|Children's Crusade}}, twelve-twelve, ended sold
Or drowned; the {{x|Fifth}} struck {{p|Egypt}}, and it failed;
{{c|3049|Frederick II}}, though cast out, went, and, bold,
Twelve twenty-nine, by treaty, there prevailed,

Took {{p|Jerusalem}}, lost it, forty-four;
{{c|3050|Saint Louis}}, forty-eight, was caught, and paid;
At {{p|Tunis}}, twelve-seventy, died; and more:
At {{p|Acre}}, ninety-one, the last was laid.

The wars bore fruit: the {{c|2930|Europeans}} came
to know their cousins' learning in the East,
their numbers, paper, sugar, and the same
old Greek the house had kept: one house, one feast.

And {{p|Venice}}, {{p|Genoa}}, grew rich on fare
Of ships; and money grew, and banks were made;
And so the ground was laid, and left in care,
For all that came, the learning and the trade.

The {{c|3052|Military Orders}} rose: the band
{{c|3053|Templars}}, {{c|3054|Hospitallers}}, {{c|3055|Teutonic Knights}},
who kept the pilgrims' road, grew rich in land;
the {{c|3053|Templars}} rich enough to lose their rights.

In thirteen-oh-seven Philip of {{p|France}}
arrested them for heresy, and seized
their gold: the charge was faith; the cause, finance,
an order's treasure, and the king was pleased.

The crusades were a war of race and blood:
the Frank against the Arab, {{c|2500|Jew}} and Greek;
the cross was raised, but under it a flood:
contempt for every people they called weak.


# Chapter 203: Kamakura Japan and the Kamikaze

In {{p|Japan}}, from eleven-eighty-five,
The {{c|2928|Kamakura Shogunate}} held sway;
The {{c|2920|Minamoto}} line did not long thrive;
The {{c|3121|Hōjō}} regents ruled the shoguns' day,

As {{c|2907|Fujiwara}} had the child kings held;
{{c|3122|Hōjō Yasutoki}}, twelve-thirty-two,
Wrote the {{c|3123|Goseibai Shikimoku}}, which spelled
The warriors' law, apart from the court's, new.

{{x|Buddhism}} burst into new schools: the first,
{{c|3124|Hōnen}}'s {{c|3125|Jōdo-shū}}, the {{x|Pure Land}}, which taught
That calling {{c|2817|Amida}}'s name reversed
All sin; and all the poor found what they sought.

{{c|2812|Shinran}}, his pupil, made {{c|3126|Jōdo Shinshū}},
The {{x|True Pure Land}}: one call, in faith, alone
Suffices; the rest {{c|2817|Amida}} will do;
The saving hand is his, and not our own.

{{c|3127|Eisai}} brought {{c|3128|Rinzai Zen}} from {{p|China}}'s shore,
With koans, and the warriors took it up;
{{c|2814|Dōgen}}'s {{c|3129|Sōtō Zen}} asked for nothing more
Than sitting, just to sit, an empty cup.

{{c|2813|Nichiren}} told the regents they must stand
by the {{c|3130|Lotus Sutra}}, by it alone,
and leave the other schools' support: the land
would fall if the other schools held their own.

He said a foreign foe would come; and so,
{{c|3076|Kublai Khan}} sent, and asked that {{p|Japan}} bow;
The {{c|3121|Hōjō}} regent said no; and the foe
Set sail, twelve-seventy-four, prow on prow.

At {{p|Hakata Bay}}, on {{p|Kyushu}}, they came;
The samurai fought one on one, by right;
The {{c|2904|Mongols}} fought in ranks, with bombs of flame
And poisoned arrows; and that day, that night,

Many samurai fell; and then there broke
A typhoon on the fleet; the {{c|2904|Mongols}} fled;
The samurai said they had won; they spoke
Of victory, but knew they'd lost, and bled.

Seven years they made ready; built a wall
Of stone, two meters high, along the bay;
Trained samurai to fight in ranks; and all
The shrines and temples, one rite, came to pray.

In twelve-eighty-one, {{c|3079|Kublai}} sent two fleets,
A hundred forty thousand men, and more;
Nine hundred ships from {{p|Korea}}; the sheets
Of thirty-five hundred from {{p|China}}'s shore.

The samurai held out for weeks; and then
Another typhoon came; the fleet was caught
In harbor, and four thousand ships, and men,
A hundred thousand, drowned, or came to naught.

And those who reached the shore were cut down there.
The {{c|3083|kamikaze}}, so the wind was named,
The wind divine, sent by the gods, the care
Of {{p|Japan}}'s kami, who the islands claimed.

The wars wore out the {{c|2928|Shogunate}}: the men
Who fought had looked for land, and none was won;
No land to give; the lords grew angry; then,
In thirteen-thirty-three, its rule was done:

{{c|3131|Go-Daigo}}, emperor, would then restore
The throne's rule, the {{c|3132|Kenmu Restoration}};
It would not hold; the warriors ruled once more,
The court lost its hold upon the nation.


# Chapter 204: Genghis Khan

On the high steppe, {{c|3056|Temujin}}'s life began,
Born, eleven-sixty-two, of small lords;
His father poisoned, he was nine; his clan
Were hunted, poor, by the old tribal swords.

# Chapter 209: The Corruption of the Papacy

The papacy had ages of its own
when families and kings would hold the keys
and keep the chair as houses keep a throne.
Mark two: the {{c|3271|Pornocracy}}, first of these.

"The rule of harlots": so its foes would tease.
The {{c|3272|House of Theophylact}} made its home
of the chair sixty years, and held the keys
with {{c|3273|Theodora}}, {{c|3274|Senatrix of Rome}}.

{{c|3275|Marozia}}, her daughter, at fifteen
loved {{c|2825|Pope}} {{c|3276|Sergius III}}; to him she bore
the son who would be {{c|2825|Pope}} {{c|3277|John XI}}: a queen
of {{p|Rome}} who kept the keys for years and more.

She made the popes, her husband and her son;
her grandson too, {{c|3278|John XII}}, took the chair:
the keys were in a family's hands, as one
house keeps its altar, with its kin at prayer.

And {{c|3278|John XII}} was charged by {{c|3280|Otto}}'s side:
the {{p|Lateran Palace}} a brothel, prayers
to the old gods at dice. The charges tried
to take the keys from {{c|3275|Marozia}}'s heirs.

He was deposed by {{c|3280|Otto}}, but he came
back when {{c|3280|Otto}} left; in nine-sixty-four
he died at twenty-seven, and his name
held {{p|Rome}} for his house a little while more.

A dozen popes the {{c|3271|Pornocracy}} made,
And many died by force: the poison poured,
A pillow; so the chair, its keys, its trade,
Passed on by murder, by the strangler's cord.

{{c|3281|Cadaver Synod}}, eight-ninety-seven,
Came first: {{c|2825|Pope}} {{c|3282|Stephen VI}} dug up the dead
{{c|3283|Formosus}}, nine months dead, bound for heaven,
In papal robes, was tried; a deacon said

The dead man's answers; and it was condemned;
His acts annulled; his blessing fingers cut
From his right hand; the body, so contemned,
Thrown in the {{p|Tiber}}; and the door was shut

On {{c|3282|Stephen VI}}: that year, strangled, he fell;
Such was the papacy in that dark age:
Corruption, murder, theater as well,
The keys passed hand to hand, as on a stage.

The second low: thirteen-oh-nine, the chair
Went, as the {{c|3285|Avignon Papacy}}, south
Down to {{p|France}}, and served the crown of {{p|France}} there;
Seven French popes, the king's word in their mouth.

For {{c|3286|Philip IV}} had made the move, at war
With {{c|3287|Boniface VIII}}, who had been seized
By {{p|France}}, thirteen-oh-three, and died; and more,
The court grew rich, and sold, as {{p|France}} was pleased:

It sold its offices and pardons; sold
Appointments; and its courts lived high and grand;
And it was bound to {{x|French}} banks, and their gold;
The keys were held now in the banker's hand.

{{c|3288|Catherine of Siena}} then moved the {{c|2825|Pope}},
{{c|3289|Gregory XI}}, to go back home again,
In seventy-seven; he died; the hope
Broke: the {{c|3290|Western Schism}}; two, then three men:

One in {{p|Rome}}, one in {{p|Avignon}}, and then
a third at Pisa; and the kings would choose
their pope by alliance: one chair, three men,
three courts, one office, and the keys to lose.

The {{c|3291|Council of Constance}} would meet to end
the schism, fourteen-seventeen; it set
the three aside, and {{c|3292|Martin V}} would tend
the one chair; the keys were one house's yet.

A century on, {{c|3220|Martin Luther}} came;
Fifteen-seventeen, nailed his {{x|Theses}} up,
Ninety-five, at {{p|Wittenberg}}; and the blame
Was pardons sold, to fill {{p|St. Peter's}} cup.

The {{x|Protestant Reformation}} grew there:
the keys had been a family's, and the kings',
and princes and the households everywhere
would claim the keys as well, with all it brings.

And this is why we love the chair of {{p|Rome}}:
it was a household, with its brides and grooms,
its keys passed hand to hand, as in a home,
its saints and schemers in the Fisher's rooms.

The {{c|3293|Vicar of Christ}} could be any man
of any house, a boy, a lover, lord,
and still the office held since it began,
and that is why they fought for it by sword.

In the {{p|Crimea}}; plague broke out; and they
Hurled corpses over walls into the fort;

The {{c|3114|Genoese}} then fled by their ships, and came
{{p|Messina}}, {{p|Sicily}}, forty-seven,
They bore the plague with them; it spread, aflame,
{{p|Mediterranean}}, under heaven,

Then north through {{p|Europe}}; {{p|Marseille}} fell; and where
The popes at {{p|Avignon}} sat, it came too;
{{c|2825|Pope}} {{c|3134|Clement VI}} lived, by the doctor's care,
Between two roaring fires; and it passed through.

{{p|Paris}}, {{p|London}}, the {{x|Hansa}}, {{p|Norway}}, and
{{p|Iceland}}: it reached the corners of the west;
A third of all the people of the land
Died in four years, by fifty-one; the rest

Saw cities lose half of their own, and fields
Untilled, villages abandoned; none
Were spared: the priest, the lord, behind their shields,
Died at the rate of all, and all as one.

The buboes, size of apples, in the groin
And armpits; black spots on the skin, of blood
Beneath; and vomit; and in days, to join
The dead. Some towns shut gates against the flood

With varying success; and then would write
{{c|3135|Giovanni Boccaccio}} the tale:
The {{c|3136|Decameron}}: {{c|3137|Florentines}} in flight
To villas, telling stories through death's gale.

of Abraham's, and burned it, wall by wall.

To end the {{c|2904|Mongols}}' rule, and shed their blood.

And after: labor scarce, the wages rose;
The feudal order strained; the serfs could press
Their lords for better terms; and, as it goes,
The {{x|Church}}, which could not stop it, mattered less.

So {{p|Europe}} came out changed; the old and sure
Was shaken; and the plague came back again,
But never like the first, the type, the pure
Shape of the plague, in forty-eight, for men.

In {{p|China}} the {{c|3075|Yuan}} fell to decay:

{{c|3015|Timur}}, or {{c|3113|Tamerlane}}, for he was lame,
{{c|3177|Timur the Lame}}, born thirteen-thirty-six
By {{p|Samarkand}}, of {{c|3178|Barlas}}, no great name,

# Chapter 214: Lithuania, the Last Pagans

While all the rest of {{p|Europe}} had for long
Been Christian, {{p|Lithuania}} kept fast
The old gods, the last pagan realm, and strong,
Till the late fourteenth century had passed:

They worshipped {{c|3192|Perkūnas}}, the thunder, kin
Of {{c|2937|Thor}} and {{c|2065|Zeus}}, of {{c|2063|Indra}}, {{c|3193|Perun}}; there
{{c|3194|Gabija}}, hearth; {{c|3195|Žemyna}}, earth; within
The spirits of the forest, water, air.

The {{c|3196|alkas}}, oaken groves, their temples stood;
At {{p|Vilnius}} the sacred flame was kept
By priestesses, the {{c|3197|vaidilutės}}; and good
Was amber; {{c|3198|Dievas}}, sky, who never slept.

The {{c|3055|Teutonic Knights}} had crusaded long
Against them, from twelve-hundreds, to convert,
They said; to conquer, truly; but the strong
{{c|3191|Lithuanians}} fought, and did them hurt.

{{c|3199|Grand Duke Gediminas}} would spread the land,
Took {{x|Rus}} principalities, all worn down
Beneath the {{c|2904|Mongol}} yoke; by eighty, spanned
From {{x|Baltic}} to {{p|Black Sea}}, a mighty crown.

His {{c|3200|Russians}} could keep the {{x|Orthodox}} rite;
And his {{c|3191|Lithuanians}} kept the gods;
{{c|3199|Gediminas}} let both be, in his sight,
One crown and many faiths, against the odds.

{{c|3201|Jogaila}}, Grand Duke, in eighty-six,
{{c|3202|Jagiełło}} to the Poles, took as his bride
{{c|3203|Queen Jadwiga}}; was baptized, to fix
The crown, pledged {{p|Lithuania}} beside.

The new priests took the lords; among the herds
the farmhouse kept {{c|3194|Gabija}}'s hearth and bread,
the groves, the household offerings, the words
of the same rite, for centuries ahead.

The groves were kept for ages; and the tongue
Kept {{x|Indo-European}} gods by name,
So that the {{c|3204|Hindus}} know them, old and young;
Of all the rites, the most kept, and the same.

{{c|3205|Polish-Lithuanian Union}} grew
Great; at {{c|3206|Battle of Grunwald}}, fourteen-ten,
{{p|Poland-Lithuania}} broke right through
{{c|3055|Teutonic Knights}}, who never rose again;

The {{c|3191|Lithuanians}}, were Christian now;
And so the {{c|450|Order}} faded, all for naught,
With none to hunt, and no crusader's vow.

the crown decided who would hold the rite;

The most archaic living, of its kind,
Near {{x|Proto-Indo-European}}'s tone;
Of all its kin, the least changed, least declined.

{{x|The Book That Was Never Written}}: there'd be,
Like the {{x|Eddas}}, {{x|Mahabharata}}, kept

Was lost; what we can gather back is blurred
Beside the {{c|2929|Norse}} and Greek, a broken scroll.

It is the great "what if" of all the books:
the oldest rite in all of {{p|Europe}}'s land,
kept in the groves, the hearths, the farmhouse nooks,
with no {{c|2934|Snorri}} to write it down by hand.


# Chapter 215: The Renaissance

A light came on in {{p|Florence}}, not the sun,
Though like the dawn on terracotta, bright;
A town that woke up to itself, begun:
The dark dissolved to fresco, gold, and white.

The human hand and eye resolved to make
Again, to warm the centuries of cold
With flame the ancients kindled, for whose sake
The world was worth depicting, as of old:

The human face and body, rendered true
On panel and on plaster, bore the trace
Of something near divine. {{c|3378|Cosimo}} too
Struck the first match: the banker of that place,

{{c|3312|Medici}}, rich on credit, on the bill
Of exchange, moving {{p|Europe}}'s wealth around
Without the gold; and with that skill, they still
Bought beauty back, not kingdoms, from the ground

Of time: the books of {{c|2376|Plato}}, and the scroll
Of {{c|1081|Aristotle}}; Greek and Roman mind
Restored to living hands, made new and whole,
In {{p|Florence}}, that defied the dark behind.

In fifty-three, {{p|Constantinople}} fell
Beneath the {{c|3341|Ottoman}}; the scholars fled
West with the last of manuscripts, to tell
{{c|1957|Homer}} and {{c|2408|Euclid}} to {{p|Italy}}; spread,

{{p|Byzantium}} lost; {{p|Florence}} gained the seed
Of the old world, wind-tossed across the sea;
Took root in fertile soil, and it was freed
To grow, where nothing lost again would be.

And {{c|3313|Brunelleschi}} raised the dome, the crown
{{p|Santa Maria del Fiore}}, where
The void had gaped a century, and down
Came rain; too vast for vaults of wood to bear;

No centering; but ring on ring it rose,
Herringbone brick that locked each course in place,
Twin-shelled; {{c|3340|Roman}} {{p|Pantheon}} it outgrows,
The paragon of mind, and of its grace.

And {{c|3379|Leonardo}}, man of all, whose books
Found sun in all: anatomy and flight,
The flow of water and the shell, the brooks,
The unborn child curled in the womb, the light;

He drew it all, and understood; and heard
The call of {{c|1|Aster}} speaking, flesh and wood
And water, and the beautiful's own word
Of number; art and science, the same good:

Both were the way of seeing; and he saw.
And {{c|3380|Michelangelo}}, the sculptor, freed
The figure from the stone, which, by its law,
Had held it, bone and sinew, as a seed;

He struck the {{p|Sistine}} ceiling with the hand
Of {{c|1834|Adam}} reaching, and the gap between
The human and divine, so small; to stand
The almost-touch, charged, trembling, to be seen:

The gap's not despair, but aspiration,
The spark between the fingertips, the air
Made sacred; {{x|Renaissance}}'s creation:
That the human is worth the reaching there.

And {{c|3381|Botticelli}} painted {{c|95|Venus}}, born
Up from the foam upon her shell, the fair
Forgotten goddess, wind-blown, not forlorn,
The modest hand: beauty, the oldest prayer.

And {{c|3217|Gutenberg}}, in {{p|Mainz}}, beside the {{p|Rhine}},
The goldsmith, turned the screw-press to the page,
And printed what had been the cloister's shrine;
The book, the text, the song broke from the cage,

A flood of written word on roads and fairs;
No flame could now enforce the loss, no wall
Of cloister hoard the book, the common prayers:
The moving type, the hinge, the door for all.

{{c|2428|Machiavelli}} wrote the state laid bare:
Between what is and what should be, the grave
Of dreaming princes; first, from dogma, there
The science of how states hold, and behave.

Humanism: the human, not a note
To the divine, but eloquence itself;
The study of the living; they devote
Themselves to body, mind, art, heart, and self:

The ancient world was not the roof, but floor;
And so the feast had only just begun.
Honor the {{x|Renaissance}}, the open door,
The light that {{p|Florence}} kindled, like the sun;

The human rendered whole, in paint and stone,
In printed word, in domes the mind could make;
We are your heirs, it told the dead, your own,
Not copies; and we carry, for your sake,

As rivers bear the snow down to the sea,
What you knew to the future: that the true
Is beautiful; the hand and eye are free
As instruments of praise; and the mind, new,

Can build the dome and paint the days and print;
And that the love called {{c|1|Aster}} burns the most
In those who make: the painter's hue and tint,
The sculptor, builder, printer, all the host.

The light came on in {{p|Florence}}, and it spread
Across all {{p|Europe}}, like the spring that sings
After the winter; and the long-since dead
Were raised again upon the living's wings,

And proved the human mind can stand beside
The stars and the celestial streams, unshamed;
For we, too, mortal, grand, shine far and wide,
Beyond our dreams, as {{c|1|Aster}}'s light has named.


# Chapter 216: Zheng He

{{c|1936|Zheng He}} was born {{c|3207|Ma He}}, seventy-one,
In {{p|Yunnan}}, a {{c|3208|Hui}} and Muslim boy;
His father's father, father, hajj had done;
Taken at ten, castrated, for employ,

A eunuch in the house of Prince {{c|3168|Zhu Di}},
Who'd be the {{c|3170|Yongle Emperor}}; he served
In all his wars, and trusted, rose to be
Named {{c|1936|Zheng He}}, for the fame that he deserved.

Once crowned, the emperor gave him command
Of the great {{c|3173|treasure voyages}}: the fleets
Of envoys and of might, to each far land
Across the {{p|Indian Ocean}}; their feats:

The first, fourteen-oh-five, three hundred ships
And seventeen, twenty-eight thousand men;
The flagships, of four hundred feet, eclipse
All the ships the {{c|2930|Europeans}} had then.

Seven times, till thirty-three, he set sail,
{{p|Southeast Asia}}, {{p|India}}, and the land
{{p|Arabia}}; {{p|Africa}}'s shore; the tale
Reached {{p|Mogadishu}}, and {{p|Malindi}}'s sand;

{{p|Calicut}}, {{p|Ceylon}}, {{p|Hormuz}}, {{p|Aden}}; some
Of his men made the hajj; and home were brought
Envoys from far, and beasts; and there would come
From {{p|Africa}} a giraffe, which was thought

In {{p|Beijing}} a {{c|3209|qilin}}, the beast of grace,
And Heaven's sign for {{c|3174|Yongle}}'s reign; and they
Painted and praised it at the court; the place
Of {{x|Ming}} upon the seas was shown that way.

He fought one war, a pirate base, that stood
In {{p|Sumatra}}; but else, he came in peace:
He gave his gifts, took tribute, and made good
With kings; he conquered none, nor took a lease.

He could have won the ocean's rim; he chose
Not to. And {{c|3174|Yongle}} died, in twenty-four;
The scholars called it waste; and after those,
And one voyage under {{c|3210|Xuande}}, then no more;

The court turned inward; and the fleets were razed,
The shipyards closed; the records burned; and so
{{p|China}}'s great skill at sea, which had amazed
The world, decayed; the ocean let it go.

{{c|1936|Zheng He}} died on the seventh voyage, at
{{p|Calicut}}; and his grave is still unknown;
In {{p|Nanjing}} stands an empty tomb, and that
Is the Muslim eunuch admiral's own.

In {{p|China}} now he is revived, a sign
Of peaceful meeting with the world; the road,
The {{c|3213|Belt and Road Initiative}}'s own line,
Calls on his voyages, the old sea's code.

So {{p|China}} chose to turn within; the seas
Went to the {{p|West}}, whose ships were small and few;
The great what-if: had {{c|3174|Yongle}}'s heirs held these,
The {{p|West}} would have found {{c|1936|Zheng He}} sailing through.

The {{c|3245|French}} throne by descent; and, one by one,

# Chapter 219: Gutenberg and the Press

{{c|3214|Johannes Gutenberg}}, a goldsmith, made
In {{p|Mainz}}, within the {{c|3215|Holy Roman}} land,
The type that moves, the fourteen-forties' trade;
The greatest tool of word that came to hand.

Carved blocks in {{p|China}} printed, ages long;
{{c|3216|Bi Sheng}} made moving type, ten-forty; and
The Koreans cast it in metal, strong,
Before; but signs by thousands, for their hand,

Made carving better still; in {{p|Asia}}, then,
The press did not take hold. But letters, few,
Two dozen, suited moving type; and when
{{c|3217|Gutenberg}} cast his type in metal new:

Of lead and tin and antimony, which
Held shape through thousands of impressions; and
He took the press of wine and oil, a switch
To paper; and an oil-based ink he planned

That held to metal, more than scribes' had done.
The {{c|3218|Gutenberg Bible}}, in fifty-five,
One hundred eighty copies, and, each one
Like handwriting; and forty-nine survive.

Bankrupt, sued by his backer, {{c|3219|Johann Fust}},
Who got the press; and he died poor, alone;
But the press spread, as from a blowing gust:
By fifteen hundred, presses had so grown

In cities, twice a hundred; and the books
Were twenty million, more than all the hands
Of copyists a thousand years, in nooks
Of cloisters, made: {{x|incunabula}}'s lands.

And knowledge spread: what one man saw, a crowd
Would read in months; discovery would dawn;
The laity read scripture out aloud;
And Latin's hold on learning would be gone:

In German, French, and English, Spanish, too,
And Italian, the printed books were made;
And grammars, dictionaries, spellings grew
Fixed, as the printers set them; nations laid

Their sense of self on shared print; pamphlets, sheets,
Cheap books put reading into many hands
Of women, workers; and in lanes and streets
The readers grew, in all the western lands.

And print spread lies as well, and rumor's word:
The witch-hunt manuals, the tracts of hate
Against the Jews, the calls for war were heard
In print; the dark side of the press, as great.

But of the second thousand years, it stands
The greatest making; {{p|Europe}} it remade,
And the East's lead in learning left its hands;
Its pace grew swift; and so the ground was laid.

# Chapter 221: The Year of Three Falls

Three ancient houses shuddered, groaned, and fell,
And from the rubble of their stones set free
The modern world emerged, still raw from hell.

In {{p|Gascony}}, where vineyards met the sword,
The longest war in {{c|3332|Christendom}} reversed
Its verdict—{{c|3333|England}}'s claim upon the board
Of {{p|France}} was broken, scattered, and dispersed.

For one hundred and sixteen years the crown
Of {{p|France}} had been the prize that {{c|3333|England}} sought—
From {{p|Crécy}}'s longbow triumph raining down
To {{p|Agincourt}}'s impossible onslaught—

The {{c|3244|English}} archer and the {{c|3244|English}} knight
Had ruled the continent by force of will,
Had planted their lilies beside the white
And crimson crosses on the {{c|3334|Valois}} hill.

The peasant in her armor, white and sure,

For {{p|France}}'s long paralysis was faith:
The {{c|3336|Dauphin}} crowned at {{p|Rheims}} because a girl
Had said the voices told her so—the wraith
Of heaven's purpose in a mortal pearl.

Of the last battle spoke, and {{c|3333|England}}'s dawn

In {{p|France}} was over. {{c|3338|Talbot}}, the old knight,

Of the new artillery, and the pride

# Chapter 223: The Standing on the Ugra

Two hundred forty years the {{c|3355|Mongol}} yoke
Had pressed upon the {{c|3356|Russian}} neck—the weight
Of {{c|3069|Batu Khan}}'s descendants, and the smoke
Of {{p|Ryazan}}, {{p|Kiev}}, burned at the gate

Of memory: the cities sacked, the lords
Made to crawl down before the {{c|3074|Golden Horde}},
The tribute paid in silver, and the swords
Above the {{c|3356|Russian}} will, its overlord;

Since twelve-thirty-seven, when riders came
Of {{c|3070|Batu}}'s, sweeping out across the plain
Of {{p|Rus}} like winter's scythe, no prince, the same,
Had ever dared to break the {{c|3356|Russian}} chain.

To {{p|Sarai}} silver flowed, {{p|Moscow}}'s hoard;
From {{p|Novgorod}}; the {{c|3082|Khan}}'s basqaq kept tale
Of kopek and of rye; the {{c|3082|Khan}} was lord.

Was dragged before the {{c|3082|Khan}} and made to kneel
Upon the felt, the carpet, and to pray

And squabbled for the scraps the {{c|3355|Mongols}} gave,
The {{c|3358|Muscovite}} princes quietly learned
The conqueror's own arts, the patient, grave

But for the state—the slow and steady wax
Of power gathered to a single throne.

{{c|3360|Kalita}}, "{{c|3361|Moneybags}}," who gathered gold
For {{p|Sarai}}, kept his share, the vector
Of {{p|Moscow}}'s rise from timber-fort to hold

Now ruled a {{p|Moscow}} that had swallowed whole
The principalities, and one by one

Of {{p|Novgorod}} the Great, merchant city,
Whose bell of freedom was seized and carried
To {{p|Moscow}}, without a shred of pity—

{{c|3365|Sophia Palaiologina}}, niece

Upon a {{p|Moscow}} claiming {{c|1983|Caesar}}'s throne.

The tribute; and no silver, nothing due,

To teach the vassal prince obedience;

Of fear pulled: his wife, the expedience
Of counselors, urged flight, as they had planned;

Of infantry arrayed in grim review.

That gave passage for the calculating
Charge. But {{c|3367|Ahmad}} looked across; and the force

Of the cannon's mouth, the arquebus's gleam,

And disciplined formation on the bank.

Just the empty eastern shore at morning,
Hoofprints in the frozen mud receding,
And the {{c|3356|Russian}} sentries, without warning,
Discovering the world had changed, reading

The silence of the steppe: the {{c|3116|Horde}} was gone;
The yoke was off, and no one had been slain;
And {{p|Russia}} stood alone beneath the dawn,
Free, by the river, on the frozen plain.


# Chapter 224: Granada and the Ocean

In fourteen ninety-two, two doors swung wide—
One closing and one opening—and {{p|Spain}}
Stood at both thresholds, the divide
Of history, the hurricane

Of consequence that would reshape the world.

The first door shut in {{p|Granada}}.

                                       Eight hundred years
The crescent and the cross had furled
And unfurled across {{p|Iberia}}, the tears

And the triumphs of the long {{x|Reconquista}}:
From {{p|Covadonga}}'s desperate mountain stand
Where {{c|3382|Pelayo}} held the thin resistor
Of the Christian remnant's band

Against the {{c|2774|Umayyad}} tide—through the slow
And century-by-century reclamation
Of the peninsula, the ebb and flow
Of border, fortress, devastation,

Alliance, and betrayal—for the {{c|3383|Moors}}
Had built their own magnificence:
The {{p|Alhambra}}'s latticed doors,
The courtyards where the eloquence

Of water spoke in fountains and in channels,
The mathematical and geometric lace
Of tilework on the walls and panels,
The gardens where the interface

Of {{p|Africa}} and {{p|Europe}} bloomed in jasmine
And in orange tree and the murmured prayer
Of the muezzin—the discipline
Of {{c|3384|Al-Andalus}}, the rare

And luminous civilization
Where {{c|3385|Muslim}}, {{c|3386|Christian}}, and {{c|2510|Jew}} had lived
In an imperfect but real constellation
Of coexistence—where the sieve

Of translation passed the {{x|Greek}} and {{c|3340|Roman}} texts
Through {{c|3387|Arabic}} to {{c|3388|Latin}}, where {{c|3389|Maimonides}}
And {{c|2393|Averroes}} probed the complex
And knotted questions of the divine decrees,

Where the knowledge was translated
And carried north—the caravan
Of wisdom crossing, unabated,
Every boundary known to man.

But the frontier had been narrowing for centuries—
{{p|Toledo}} fell, then {{p|Cordoba}}'s domain,
Then {{p|Seville}}'s minarets, the sentries
Of the caliphate receding on the plain

And southward, south, until only {{p|Granada}}
Remained: the last {{c|3390|Moorish}} kingdom, the mouth
Of the last river, the armada
Of the crescent's final south

In {{p|Iberia}}'s contested soil.

And now {{c|3391|Ferdinand}} and {{c|3392|Isabella}} came—
The joined crowns of {{p|Castile}} and {{p|Aragon}}, the toil
Of the marriage-alliance and the flame

Of war for the altars, a ten years' siege,
The slow strangulation of the last
{{c|3393|Nasrid}} kingdom—and the liege
Who had somehow, stubbornly, outlast-

Ed every other {{c|3390|Moorish}} realm now fell.

On the second day of January,
The year fourteen ninety-two, the bell
And trumpet on the ordinary

And extraordinary morning: {{c|3394|Boabdil}},
The last {{c|3393|Nasrid}} sultan, rode out
From the {{p|Alhambra}}'s gate, and the hill
Of the {{p|Sierra Nevada}} heard his doubt

Made manifest—he wept.

                           The story says
His mother spoke: "You weep like a woman
For what you could not defend in the days
Of your kingship"—and whether the human

And particular grief was truly spoken so,
The legend holds the truth beneath:
That something precious and aglow
Was passing from the world, and underneath

The victory's trumpet lay a death.

For what {{c|3391|Ferdinand}} and {{c|3392|Isabella}} did
With their triumph was to draw the breath
Of one altar: the edict, the forbid—

The {{p|Alhambra}} Decree, signed in the same
Palace whose beauty the {{c|3383|Moors}} had raised—
Commanded every {{c|2510|Jew}} in {{p|Spain}} to claim
Baptism or exile. The amazed

And ancient {{c|3395|Sephardic}} communities—
The scholars, the physicians, the traders
Whose roots ran deep as the olive trees
Of {{p|Andalusia}}—two hundred thousand waders

Through grief, given four months to leave
Or convert: the lute and {{x|Torah}} borne
Away, the hearthstone, the weave
Of fifteen centuries torn—

They went to {{p|Morocco}}, to {{p|Constantinople}},
To {{p|Amsterdam}}, to {{p|Thessaloniki}}'s shore,
Carrying the keys—that hull
Of memory—to their {{p|Spanish}} door

In their pockets, passed from hand to hand
For five hundred years, the heaviest heirloom known:
A key to a house in a distant land
That would never again be their own.

The {{c|3396|Moriscos}} followed—the {{c|2764|Muslims}} too
Were given the same ultimatum's shears:
Convert or leave. The residue
Of eight centuries of civilization's years

Swept from the house so one crown held the faith.

And yet the same monarchs—the same
{{c|3391|Ferdinand}}, the same {{c|3392|Isabella}}—the wraith
Of purity still warm—now blessed a claim

From a {{c|3114|Genoese}} sailor in their court,
A man of vision and compulsion,
Who had begged at every port
Of {{p|Europe}}'s power—the expulsion

Of the {{c|2500|Jews}} not yet six weeks complete—
For ships and crew to sail west,
West across the {{p|Atlantic}}'s heat
And uncharted water, the behest

Of a dream: that {{p|Cipangu}}'s gold
And {{p|Cathay}}'s silk could be reached
By sailing into the sunset's hold—
{{c|2952|Columbus}} said the gap was breached

By courage and a compass and the will.

And on the third of August's morning tide,
Three ships departed, small and still,
From {{p|Cadiz}}' harbor—the pride

Of their masts like three bare trees
Against the {{p|Atlantic}}'s sky:
The {{c|3397|Nina}}, the {{c|3398|Pinta}} on the breeze,
The {{c|3399|Santa Maria}} riding high.

{{c|2952|Columbus}} was brave—and he was wrong
About nearly everything: the size
Of the earth, the distance, the headlong
Conviction that {{p|Asia}} would arise

On the western horizon—and what he found
Was not {{p|Cipangu}} or {{p|Cathay}}
But the {{c|3400|Taino}} people on their ground,
A world that had its own display

Of civilization, its own calendar,
Its own astronomers and priests,
Its own long memory—the familiar
And ancient human feasts

Of harvest and of prayer, the villages
And the canoes, the cotton and the corn—
A world complete, whose lineages
Stretched back sixteen thousand years before the horn

Of any {{c|3401|European}} trumpet sounded.

But {{c|2952|Columbus}} saw gold—and the hunger
Of {{p|Europe}}, the insatiable and unbounded
Want that had been growing younger

And more ravenous with every century,
Was set in motion on that morning's hour
When the lookout cried across the entry
Of the unknown sea, from the crow's-nest tower:

"Land!"

       And the door swung open to the west.

What lay beyond—the story of the peoples
Who had built their worlds, their crest
And glory, long before the steeples

Of {{p|Seville}} rose, long before the {{c|3340|Roman}} road,
Long before the {{p|Parthenon}}—
That story claims its own abode
And its own telling, its own dawn:

The Book of {{c|3402|Lehi}} now begins.

For across the water that {{c|2952|Columbus}} sailed
Were sixteen thousand years of origins,
Of empires raised and curtained and unveiled,

Of corn domesticated and the feathered
Serpent's temple and the {{c|3403|Inca}}'s road
And the potlatch and the weathered
Totem speaking in its sacred code—

A world entire, a hemisphere of the living,
Now standing at the hinge of its destruction
And its transformation—the unforgiving
Door of fourteen ninety-two, the junction

Where the old world's hunger met the new world's grace.

Turn the page. The ocean has been crossed.
The Book of {{c|3402|Lehi}} opens on the face
Of {{p|America}}—the found, the lost,

The ancient and the beautiful, the home
Of the first migration's children, and their art
Perfection of ten thousand years—the poem
Of a hemisphere apart.

The story of {{p|Granada}} ends in tears.
The story of the ocean ends in sail.
What follows is the song of the frontier's
Other side—the {{c|3404|Americans}}' own tale.
