# Chapter 197: Heian Japan

The Heian age, from seven-ninety-four,
Was named for {{p|Heian-kyō}}, the town of peace,
{{p|Kyoto}} as now, laid out as was, before,
Like {{p|Chang'an}} of the {{x|Tang}}; and without cease

Four hundred years the court lived in its art;
The Fujiwara held the rule instead,
As regents for the child kings, and, apart,
For grown ones too; and all their daughters, wed,

Bore grandsons. Michinaga, at the height
Of their house, had four daughters, each a queen
To four kings; and he wrote: this world, by right,
Is mine, a full moon, nothing lacking, seen;

And after him the regents waned. And there
The women of the court, in kana, wrote:
Murasaki Shikibu, in her care,
The Tale of Genji, chapters, every note,

Fifty-four, of the Shining Prince's heart,
The court, and mono no aware, the sweet
Sadness of passing; and, to take her part,
Sei Shōnagon, her rival, wrote complete

The Pillow Book: her lists, her scenes, the things
That make one's heart beat fast, the fair, the plain;
Izumi Shikibu, whose love-song sings;
Ono no Komachi, of age's pain;

Immortal Poets, many women; for
The men wrote {{x|Chinese}}, kanji; and the low
Was kana, women's; so they wrote, and more,
In their own tongue; and theirs endures, and so

The men's is lost. And all of it was touched
With mono no aware: the blossoms fall
After a week; the leaves turn brown; and clutched
A night of love, then parting; and in all,

The taste of loss. But out beyond the town,
The bushi rose then: the Taira, and
Minamoto, cadet lines of the crown,
Who served the provinces, and grew, and manned

Their bands; and then Hogen's and Heiji's wars,
In fifty-six and sixty, brought them in;
Kiyomori, Taira, held the doors,
With puppet kings; but Yoritomo, kin

Of Minamoto, raised the {{p|Kanto}} plain,
The Genpei War, eighty to eighty-five;
At {{p|Dan-no-ura}} the Taira slain,
Child-king Antoku drowned, none left alive,

With him, the sword Kusanagi, at sea.
And Yoritomo's Shogunate, the state,
At {{p|Kamakura}}, ninety-two; and he
Ruled; the emperor in {{p|Kyoto}} would wait.

So honor Heian: Murasaki's pen,
Sei Shōnagon's lists, and the blossoms' fall;
The passing of the court, the warrior men;
And mono no aware over it all.


# Chapter 198: Kūkai

In {{p|Japan}}, early in the ninth age, came
Kūkai, who brought from {{x|Tang}} Shingon's word,
And made it live; born on {{p|Shikoku}}, the same
He went with envoys, eight-oh-four, and heard,

At {{p|Qinglong Temple}} in {{p|Chang'an}}, the way,
From Huiguo, master of the hidden rite,
Who gave him all of it in months; that day
He died; Kūkai came home, with the light

Of scriptures, mandalas, and all the tools
Of rite, and all of Shingon, the {{x|True Word}};
At {{p|Kōya}}, eight peaks, a mandala, schools
He built, and set the teaching, as he'd heard.

He made the kana, letters for the sound
Of {{x|Japanese}}, where once the {{x|Chinese}} signs
Were used for sound alone, a heavy round;
And so the tongue was freed for poets' lines.

He taught: the world's the body of the one,
Mahāvairocana, the Buddha on high,
Dainichi, the Buddha who's like the sun;
And all are woken now, and need but try

Through body, speech, mind, the mysteries three,
The mudrās, mantras, visions, and the rite,
In this same body, in this life, to be
A Buddha: sokushin jōbutsu's light.

He wrote, a master of the brush; and made
The ponds and bridges; thought on word and sign;
And did not die, but sat, and there he stayed,
In meditation, at {{p|Kōya}}'s high shrine,

At {{p|Oku-no-In}}, uncorrupted; and still
The pilgrims come, and he is waiting there
For Maitreya, the one to come, until
He'll rise; and daily they feed him, with care.

And Saichō brought from {{p|China}} the Tendai,
The {{x|Lotus Sutra}}'s way, of Zhiyi's school,
All teachings in one ladder, raised on high,
At {{p|Mount Hiei}}; it would become the great pool

From which, in after years, the schools would flow.
The two were friends, and wrote, and lent their books,
And sometimes argued; and they made it so,
The {{x|Japanese}} way, with all its own looks.

At {{p|Heian-kyō}}, now {{p|Kyoto}}, newly made
By Kanmu, seven-ninety-four, the new
Teaching matched the new town; and, unafraid,
The kami and the Buddha closer drew

Together: honji suijaku, that each
Kami is but a bodhisattva, shown
In this land's form; and so, in all their reach,
Amaterasu was Dainichi's own,

Hachiman was Amida's; the shrine
And temple built together; one rite, two
Faces, and no divide; one house, one line,
And one religion, as the faithful knew.


# Chapter 199: Tang and the Great Persecution

The Tang, from six-eighteen, the golden age:
{{p|Chang'an}}, the greatest city of the world,
A million souls; Li Yuan, on the stage,
A general, took the {{x|Sui}}'s throne, and unfurled

The {{x|Tang}}; Taizong, his son, slew brothers, made
His father yield; and ruled, the model king
Of {{x|Confucius}}' rule, in peace; and he stayed
The golden reign that all would after sing.

Wu Zetian rose, killed her rivals, and
Ruled, the one woman, emperor in name;
The Buddha rose above the rest; her hand
Made {{p|Longmen}}'s Buddha, with her face the same.

Then Xuanzong reigned, at first in glory; then
His trusted An Lushan, {{x|Sogdian}} by birth,
A general, rose, fifty-five; and men
Died, eight years, through the war; and through the earth

Fifty-three million became seventeen;
The {{x|Tang}} grew weak; and Yang Guifei, his love,
His soldiers made him kill; and all the sheen
Of his long reign went dark, as from above.

Li Bai was at his court; Du Fu lived through
The war, and wrote it; with Wang Wei, the four
Bai Juyi, the Four Greats, whose verse is true
Through all the ages, masters evermore.

Eight-four-five: Wuzong struck the Buddha's way:
Great Persecution: forty thousand fell,
The temples; and the state sent, on that day,
Two hundred sixty thousand monks as well.

The cause was land: the monasteries held
vast fields untaxed, and bronze the mint would take;
and Wuzong's Daoist priests would have expelled
the Buddha's monks, as rivals for one stake.

And {{x|Manichaeism}} was nearly all gone;
Nestorian Christ's, which came with the {{x|Tang}};
The Zoroastrian {{x|Sogdians}} too; and on
It went; and after Wuzong, the bell rang,

His heir reversed it; but the great days passed.
Chan lived, which needed neither books nor halls,
But only sitting; Pure Land too would last,
The name of Amitabha, which still calls

The laity, no temple and no school;
And these two would become the {{x|East}}'s chief way.
The {{x|Tang}} declined: Jiedushi would rule
Their lands; the eunuchs held the court; the day

Of risings came; and in nine-oh-seven,
The {{x|Tang}} was done; the {{p|Five Dynasties}}, and
{{p|Ten Kingdoms}} split; then, as sent from heaven,
Nine-sixty, and the Song rejoined the land.

And still the {{x|Tang}} is the high mark: its lines
Still the finest; and the {{x|Chinese}} who dwell
Abroad still call themselves the {{x|Tang}}'s; the signs
Of all they look back on, they keep it well.


# Chapter 200: Ireland and Brian Boru

{{p|Ireland}}, which Saint Patrick turned, then became
A place of learning, in the fifth age, blessed;
For while the {{p|West}} was burned, and in the flame
Of Justinian's wars, the plague, the rest,

The empty throne, the island lay beyond
The usurper's reach; and learning there stayed:
The monks kept {{x|Greek}} and {{x|Latin}}, and the bond
With books that would be lost; and so they made

Columba's {{p|Iona}}, five-sixty-three;
Columbanus' houses all through the land
Of {{p|Europe}}; Aidan, from {{p|Lindisfarne}}, free,
Went through the north; the Book of Kells, by hand,

Was made, the knotwork, interlaced, the art
Of all the {{x|Celts}}. And {{p|Ireland}} was split
Into small kingdoms, the {{x|tuath}}, each apart,
With overkings, the High King over it,

At {{p|Tara}}. And the Vikings came, and burned,
In ninety-five; {{p|Iona}} sacked again;
And they made towns, the first the island learned:
{{p|Dublin}}, {{p|Waterford}}, {{p|Cork}}, {{p|Limerick}}; men

Of {{x|Norse}} and {{x|Irish}} dwelt in them. And then
Brian Boru, of {{p|Munster}}'s {{p|Dál gCais}}, rose,
High King, ten-oh-two, who joined the isle's men
In one, the first; and he beat the {{x|Norse}} foes,

With {{p|Leinster}}'s kings, at Clontarf, on the day
Of {{x|Good Friday}}, ten-fourteen, broke the might
Of the {{x|Norse}} in {{p|Ireland}}; and, as he lay
In prayer within his tent, at end of fight,

The fleeing Vikings found him, and he died;
And after him the kings fought on, apart.
And Eriugena, scholar, at the side
Of Charles the Bald, the learned court's own heart,

Knew {{x|Greek}} and {{x|Latin}}, and he turned the {{x|Greek}}
Of Dionysius to {{x|Latin}}; made
The {{x|Periphyseon}}, the great whole, to seek
The one in all; the high crosses were laid,

{{x|Old Irish}} kept the oldest tales: the Táin,
The {{x|Cattle Raid of Cooley}}, where, alone,
Cú Chulainn held {{p|Ulster}}, {{p|Connacht}}'s bane,
In battle-fury, warped, shape not his own,

Doing what none could do; and he would fall
When his taboo was broken; kin, of old,
Of Heracles and Bhima; and, in all,
Medb, the queen of war and sovereignty, bold.

For Patrick did not burn the temples; he
Preached, and the chiefs turned, and the tribes came too;
The faith took in the old, and let it be;
And so the old gods lived on, in the new.

So honor {{p|Ireland}}: where the books were kept
Beyond the usurper's reach; Brian, slain
Praying at Clontarf; the hero who leapt
In fury; and the old rite, kept again.


# Chapter 201: India in the Early Medieval

The Guptas fell in the sixth century;
{{p|India}} split into its regions' kings;
And yet from that came no stagnation; free,
The tongues, the temples, and the thought of things,

Grew rich. And Shankara came, and he taught
Advaita: {{x|Brahman}}, the self, are the same;
All else illusion; and he wrote, and brought
Upanishads, the Gita, and the name

Of Brahma Sutras; and his word was set
As {{x|Vedanta}}'s; he went out to debate
The Buddhists, all through {{p|India}}; and met
Them at their seats, and won, and changed their state.

It was one house of teachers and their seats:
who held the kings, the students, and the alms.
{{x|Vedanta}} won the patrons and the streets,
the monasteries lost their students' psalms.

And so the Buddha's way grew small, and less,
Till, by eleven hundred, it was rare;
{{p|Nalanda}}'s books, in {{p|Tibet}}'s great recess,
In {{p|China}}, and the south, were kept with care.

And bhakti spread, the love of god: the south
Of {{x|Tamil}}, where the Alvars sang to him,
Vishnu; Nayanars, Shiva in their mouth;
And Ramanuja, of the middle rim,

Vishishtadvaita: one, but qualified,
So love could reach to god; and Madhva, two,
Dvaita, god and soul forever side
By side, apart, and some souls damned, it's true.

The Cholas of the south, the {{x|Tamil}} kings,
Rajaraja and Rajendra built the shrine
{{p|Brihadeeswarar}}, {{p|Thanjavur}}'s; wings
Reached to {{p|Sumatra}}, Srivijaya's line;

They spread the {{x|Tamil}} tongue, Shaiva way,
The dancing Shiva, Nataraja, cast
In bronze; the Palas of {{p|Bengal}}, who'd stay
With Buddha, sent {{x|Vajrayana}} at last.

Rashtrakutas, Pratiharas, the three
Fought for the north, and wore each other down;
And from the {{x|Afghan}} highlands, by degree,
The Ghaznavids came; then the Ghurids' crown:

Mahmud of Ghazni, seventeen raids, took
{{p|Somnath}}'s gold, in ten-twenty-five; and then
Muhammad of Ghor beat, as in the book,
Prithviraj there at {{x|Tarain}}; and his men

Made the Delhi Sultanate; Muslim rule
Began; and Alauddin's Khiljis went in
Down to the {{p|Deccan}}; the Tughlaqs, by rule,
Held almost all; Muhammad, with a din,

Moved the capital, made copper coin, sent
His hosts to {{p|Central Asia}}; and all failed;
And then the sultanate grew weak, and went
To regions: {{p|Bengal}}, {{p|Gujarat}}, all trailed.

So honor {{p|India}}: Shankara's one,
The bhakti songs, the Cholas' bronze and stone,
The dancing Shiva, and the seats long won,
And all its regions, each one with its own.


# Chapter 202: The Crusades

At {{x|Council of Clermont}}, ten-ninety-five,
the Pope, Urban II, sent the knights to ride
to aid {{p|Byzantium}}, and then to strive
with Muslims for the Tomb where Christ had died.

It was one God, one city, and one tomb;
the quarrel was whose priests would hold the keys,
whose bells would ring there, and whose call, whose room
for pilgrims, and whose feasts, and whose decrees.

God wills it! cried the crowd; and so began
The First Crusade; and Urban promised all
Who died there that their sins were washed; the plan
Had asked for hired men, when, at the fall

{{x|Manzikert}}, seventy-one, the hand
Of Seljuks took {{p|Anatolia}}; came
An army through the Byzantines' own land,
And ate its food; Alexios, the same,

Made them swear oaths to give him back the lands
They took; but after {{p|Antioch}} was won,
{{p|Jerusalem}}, ten-ninety-nine, their hands
Kept all; and in the town, when it was done,

They slew the Muslims, Jews, and eastern kin
Of Christ; and then made the Crusader States:
{{p|Jerusalem}}, {{p|Tripoli}}, and, within,
{{p|Edessa}}; and two hundred years, their gates.

And in {{p|Georgia}}, David the Builder, few
In men against the Seljuks, wed a bride,
Gurandukht, a Cuman khan's child; and through
Eleven-eighteen, the Cumans allied,

Forty thousand families, came down to fight;
At {{p|Didgori}}, in twenty-one, they broke
The Seljuks; and {{p|Tbilisi}} came in sight
Once more for {{p|Georgia}}; so the steppe's own folk

And {{p|Caucasus}} were wed; and not the first:
A hundred years before, Mariam, born
In {{p|Vaspurakan}}, an Artsruni, nursed
In Abul Hamza's line, of Qarin's, sworn

To George the First of {{p|Georgia}}, bore a girl,
Guarandukht, who was given north across
The grass to Osen, Cuman khan; the whirl
Of years: she bore him Aepa; and, no loss,

His daughter Anna; eleven-oh-eight,
Vladimir Monomakh made peace, and took
Anna for his son Yuri: the steppe's mate,
A Cuman bride; and Yuri, by the book

The Long-Armed, founded {{p|Moscow}}; and his son,
Andrey, of Anna, built the churches white;
{{p|Tabaristan}}'s old princes, one by one,
Through {{p|Georgia}} and the steppe, they came to light

Among the Rus. Crusaders never knew;
They fought for one city, while the old blood
Walked north by its own road; the houses grew.
Second Crusade, after {{p|Edessa}}'s flood

Failed: Louis VII and Conrad III, none
Did anything; the Third, when Saladin
Took {{p|Jerusalem}}, eighty-seven, won
Nothing; and Barbarossa drowned; and in

A quarrel, Philip left; the Lionheart
And Saladin fought on, and made a truce:
The city Muslim, pilgrims could depart
And come; each one was courteous in use.

And Saladin, Salah ad-Din, the {{p|Kurd}},
Ruled {{p|Egypt}}, {{p|Syria}}, and joined in one
The Muslims; and his courtesy was heard
In {{p|Europe}}. Then the Fourth, which never won

The {{p|Holy Land}}, but, owing {{p|Venice}} gold,
Sacked {{p|Constantinople}}, for three days long,
And carried off the relics; and the hold
Latin emperors, sixty years; the wrong

Of Christians sacking Christ's own greatest town,
From which it never would be whole again;
The greatest of betrayals, when the crown
Of Christ's own house was robbed by Christ's own men.

The {{x|Great Schism}}, ten-fifty-four, between
two sees, was a quarrel for the first chair,
{{p|Rome}} or {{p|Constantinople}}; from this scene
the quarrel held, and no hand would repair.

The {{x|Children's Crusade}}, twelve-twelve, ended sold
Or drowned; the {{x|Fifth}} struck {{p|Egypt}}, and it failed;
Frederick II, though cast out, went, and, bold,
Twelve twenty-nine, by treaty, there prevailed,

Took {{p|Jerusalem}}, lost it, forty-four;
Saint Louis, forty-eight, was caught, and paid;
At {{p|Tunis}}, twelve-seventy, died; and more:
At {{p|Acre}}, ninety-one, the last was laid.

The wars bore fruit: the Europeans came
to know their cousins' learning in the East,
their numbers, paper, sugar, and the same
old Greek the house had kept: one house, one feast.

And {{p|Venice}}, {{p|Genoa}}, grew rich on fare
Of ships; and money grew, and banks were made;
And so the ground was laid, and left in care,
For all that came, the learning and the trade.

The Military Orders rose: the band
Templars, Hospitallers, Teutonic Knights,
who kept the pilgrims' road, grew rich in land;
the Templars rich enough to lose their rights.

In thirteen-oh-seven Philip of {{p|France}}
arrested them for heresy, and seized
their gold: the charge was faith; the cause, finance,
an order's treasure, and the king was pleased.

The crusades were a war of race and blood:
the Frank against the Arab, Jew and Greek;
the cross was raised, but under it a flood:
contempt for every people they called weak.


# Chapter 203: Kamakura Japan and the Kamikaze

In {{p|Japan}}, from eleven-eighty-five,
The Kamakura Shogunate held sway;
The Minamoto line did not long thrive;
The Hōjō regents ruled the shoguns' day,

As Fujiwara had the child kings held;
Hōjō Yasutoki, twelve-thirty-two,
Wrote the Goseibai Shikimoku, which spelled
The warriors' law, apart from the court's, new.

{{x|Buddhism}} burst into new schools: the first,
Hōnen's Jōdo-shū, the {{x|Pure Land}}, which taught
That calling Amida's name reversed
All sin; and all the poor found what they sought.

Shinran, his pupil, made Jōdo Shinshū,
The {{x|True Pure Land}}: one call, in faith, alone
Suffices; the rest Amida will do;
The saving hand is his, and not our own.

Eisai brought Rinzai Zen from {{p|China}}'s shore,
With koans, and the warriors took it up;
Dōgen's Sōtō Zen asked for nothing more
Than sitting, just to sit, an empty cup.

Nichiren told the regents they must stand
by the Lotus Sutra, by it alone,
and leave the other schools' support: the land
would fall if the other schools held their own.

He said a foreign foe would come; and so,
Kublai Khan sent, and asked that {{p|Japan}} bow;
The Hōjō regent said no; and the foe
Set sail, twelve-seventy-four, prow on prow.

At {{p|Hakata Bay}}, on {{p|Kyushu}}, they came;
The samurai fought one on one, by right;
The Mongols fought in ranks, with bombs of flame
And poisoned arrows; and that day, that night,

Many samurai fell; and then there broke
A typhoon on the fleet; the Mongols fled;
The samurai said they had won; they spoke
Of victory, but knew they'd lost, and bled.

Seven years they made ready; built a wall
Of stone, two meters high, along the bay;
Trained samurai to fight in ranks; and all
The shrines and temples, one rite, came to pray.

In twelve-eighty-one, Kublai sent two fleets,
A hundred forty thousand men, and more;
Nine hundred ships from {{p|Korea}}; the sheets
Of thirty-five hundred from {{p|China}}'s shore.

The samurai held out for weeks; and then
Another typhoon came; the fleet was caught
In harbor, and four thousand ships, and men,
A hundred thousand, drowned, or came to naught.

And those who reached the shore were cut down there.
The kamikaze, so the wind was named,
The wind divine, sent by the gods, the care
Of {{p|Japan}}'s kami, who the islands claimed.

The wars wore out the Shogunate: the men
Who fought had looked for land, and none was won;
No land to give; the lords grew angry; then,
In thirteen-thirty-three, its rule was done:

Go-Daigo, emperor, would then restore
The throne's rule, the Kenmu Restoration;
It would not hold; the warriors ruled once more,
The court lost its hold upon the nation.


# Chapter 204: Genghis Khan

On the high steppe, Temujin's life began,
Born, eleven-sixty-two, of small lords;
His father poisoned, he was nine; his clan
Were hunted, poor, by the old tribal swords.

They lived on roots and marmots; and the boy
Slew his half-brother for a fish; and then
Ruled all his brothers; rivals would employ
Him as a slave; he fled, and built again

His life, through friendship and through marriage; and
When Merkits stole his wife, Borte, far away,
His blood brother Jamukha gave a hand,
And Toghrul too; he won her back; and they

Had Jochi, his first son; and so he grew
His following, and joined the {{x|Mongol}} lands;
In twelve-oh-six, the great assembly drew
Him Genghis Khan, the lord of all their bands,

The universal ruler, forty-four;
A million souls, a small folk next to those
Great realms around them; and he went to war
Across three continents, and all his foes:

Western Xia, twelve-oh-nine; Jin's domain,
Twelve-eleven; {{p|Beijing}}, twelve-fifteen, fell;
{{p|North China}} lay in ruins; and again,
In twelve-nineteen, Khwarazm's realm as well.

Its governor had killed the envoys sent,
The merchants too; and Genghis answered: all
The towns that fought were razed, the people spent:
{{p|Samarkand}} and {{p|Bukhara}}, {{p|Merv}}, would fall,

And {{p|Nishapur}}, {{p|Herat}}, the very best
Of {{p|Persia}}'s cities; and the channels made
For water were destroyed; and, in the rest,
{{p|Khorasan}}, the breadbasket, lay decayed,

Of all {{x|Islam}}, and for centuries stayed
A ruin. In twelve-twenty-seven died
Genghis, thrown from his horse; his grave was made
Unmarked; those who had seen were slain beside.

His sons divided all: the khanate's sway
Ogedei held; Jochi's, the western plain
Of steppe; Chagatai, {{p|Central Asia}}'s way;
Tolui, the homeland, the old domain.

And the rest of {{x|Jin}} fell, twelve-thirty-four;
Batu Khan won the Kipchak steppe; and then,
Twelve-forty, {{p|Kiev}} fell; and, as before,
The {{p|Rus}} were sacked, made tributary men,

The {{x|Tatar Yoke}}, two centuries; and there
{{p|Moscow}}, a small town, gathered tax, and grew
For the Mongols; and, rich from its own share,
It rose in time; the other {{p|Rus}} it drew.

And Batu went to {{p|Poland}} in the west,
Twelve-forty-one; at {{p|Legnica}} he broke
The German knights, the Polish, and the rest;
At {{p|Mohi}}, {{p|Hungary}}'s host; at one stroke

All {{p|Central Europe}} lay before him; then
Ogedei died; Batu returned to choose
The next Great Khan; and so the {{p|West}}'s own men
Were saved by a khan's death, and did not lose.

The Mongols' realm, one land, the greatest yet,
From {{p|Korea}} to {{p|Hungary}}, the north
{{p|Siberia}} to {{p|Vietnam}}; it met
All {{p|Eurasia}}, and bound it, back and forth.

The Pax Mongolica made safe the road
From {{p|Europe}} to {{p|China}}, and all by land;
Merchants and missionaries went; and flowed
The {{p|Silk Road}} at its peak, from hand to hand.

And Marco Polo, from {{p|Venice}}'s shore,
Told of the riches of the {{p|East}}; and west
Went gunpowder and printing, and the store
Of paper money, compass, and the rest.

The Mongols let each priesthood keep its rite;
queens kept the Nestorian cross, and men
Tibetan {{x|Buddhism}}; all, in one light,
prayed to Eternal Heaven, one; amen.

Ilkhanate took {{x|Islam}}, and the great
Golden Horde too; the east, in {{p|China}}'s ways,
Became the Yuan, Kublai Khan's own state;
But that belongs to the next chapter's praise.

So mark the hinge: one man, from nothing, came
From {{p|Mongolia}}, who joined his tribe, and then
Remade the world: honor Temujin's name,
From {{p|Hungary}} to {{p|Cambodia}}'s men.


# Chapter 205: The Mongols in Europe

And Batu Khan, grandson of Genghis, led
The western war, the late twelve-thirties; fell
The Kipchak steppe; and then, in frost, he sped
Against the {{p|Rus}}: {{p|Ryazan}} burned as well,

In twelve-thirty-seven; {{p|Vladimir}}, sacked,
Twelve-thirty-eight; {{p|Kiev}}, twelve-forty, lay
Rubble; two hundred houses stood intact;
And so the {{x|Tatar Yoke}} began that day:

Two centuries the princes paid, and went
To {{p|Sarai}} on the {{p|Volga}}, the Horde,
To bow; and {{p|Moscow}}, a small town, was sent
To gather tax, and rose to be the lord.

Batu, twelve-forty-one, his army drawn
To {{p|Poland}} and to {{p|Hungary}}; and there,
At Legnica, the German knights were gone,
Lured by a feigned retreat; at Mohi, where

The {{x|Hungarian}} host was broken through,
King Béla, fleeing for his life; and they
Rode to the {{p|Adriatic}}; and in view,
{{p|Vienna}}, next; and {{p|Europe}} feared that day.

But then the Great Khan, Ögedei, had died
At {{p|Karakorum}}, in that December; so
Batu went east, to where the throne was tried
Among the heirs; and west he did not go.

So {{p|Europe}} was saved by a drunkard's end,
Far off; the Mongols had shown that its knights
Could not stand to the steppe: the bows that bend,
The feigned retreat, the horse, the ordered fights.

The Golden Horde then settled on the low
{{p|Volga}}, and ruled the western steppe, the land
From the {{p|Black Sea}} up to the {{p|Urals}}; so
Two and a half centuries, the Horde's hand.

And then the princes, under {{p|Moscow}}, broke
The yoke; but that comes later. Mark it now:
The horsemen of the east, as at one stroke,
Made {{p|Europe}} choke, and taught its knights to bow.


# Chapter 206: The Mongols in the Middle East

Hulagu Khan marched on into the west,
The Great Khan Möngke's brother, sent forth to bring;
His grandsire laid {{p|Persia}} waste; the rest
To heel: the {{p|Middle East}}, and caliph, king.

A hundred fifty thousand men he led;
The Assassins first, at {{p|Alamut}}'s hold
In the {{p|Elburz}}, the Nizaris, who spread
Their killings through the {{p|Middle East}}, of old,

A century; twelve-fifty-six, their chief
Gave up; and Hulagu had razed it all;
Their books were burned, their master slain; and grief;
And some lived on, and did not wholly fall.

The Nizari Ismailis are still led
Today by the Aga Khan, of that line
Of {{p|Alamut}}, a peaceful folk, who've spread
Their gifts across the world, in work benign.

He marched on {{p|Baghdad}} from {{p|Alamut}}'s height;
Caliph al-Mustasim would not yield;
Twelve-fifty-eight, he breached the walls by might;
Sacked for a week; a million in the field.

Canals of {{p|Mesopotamia}}, where
The waters ran, were broken; and the land
That fed the {{p|Middle East}} lay empty, bare,
A desert; and the caliph, by command,

Was slain; and with him the {{x|Abbasid}} line,
Five hundred years the head of {{x|Sunni}} rule,
Was ended; and an age was gone; the sign
Of {{x|Islam}}'s one head, its seat, its learned school.

Hulagu took {{p|Damascus}}, which gave in;
He would have taken {{p|Egypt}} next; but died
Möngke Khan, in twelve-fifty-nine; he, as kin,
Went east to the succession, with his side,

And left Kitbuqa, his general, there,
In {{p|Syria}}, with twenty thousand; north
From {{p|Egypt}} came the Mamluks, slaves, to bear
Their arms; and at Ain Jalut they went forth,

Twelve-sixty, by {{x|Goliath}}'s spring, to meet;
And Qutuz the sultan, Baybars his sword,
Used the Mongols' own arts: the feigned retreat,
Envelopment, reserves, against the horde;

Kitbuqa died; the Mongols' host was slain:
Their first great loss; their westward march was done;
{{p|Egypt}} and {{p|Syria}} were saved; the reign
Of the Mamluks, two centuries, begun.

Ilkhanate held {{p|Persia}}, {{p|Iraq}}'s plain;
Its Mongols turned {{x|Persian}}, and, in their time,
Ghazan, twelve-ninety-five, made {{x|Islam}} reign;
The state's; the Christian hopes were past their prime.

And {{x|Persian}} learning lived again: the hand
Of Rashid al-Din, the vizier, wrote
The great Jami al-Tawarikh, which spanned
All realms, the first world history of note:

The Turks, Europeans, the Mongols' own,
Indians and Chinese, for scholars came
Within the Mongol peace, and all were shown
From all those lands, and wrote down each by name.

The Ilkhanids' painting flowered, and blent
With Chinese arts; the histories were made
Illumined, three in one, as they were meant,
The {{x|Mongol}}, Chinese, and {{x|Persian}}, inlaid.

In the mid-fourteenth century, it fell
Through heirs' disputes and the {{x|Black Death}}; and broke
To Jalayirids, Muzaffarids as well,
The Sarbadars, and each a separate folk.

So mark it: of all {{x|Islam}}'s blows, the worst:
{{x|Abbasid}} {{p|Baghdad}} never came again;
{{p|Cairo}} and {{p|Samarkand}} and {{p|Delhi}} nursed
What learning lived, out on the margins, then.


# Chapter 207: The Mongols in China

And Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis, won
All {{p|China}}; and the Southern Song, who'd stood
For decades past the {{p|Yangtze}}, were undone
At Yamen, twelve-seventy-nine, for good:

The loyal fleet was sunk; and, to the end,
One bore the boy emperor to the sea,
A minister, who would not yield or bend,
And drowned with him; and no more {{x|Song}} would be.

Kublai's Yuan, from twelve-seventy-one,
The first from outside who held {{p|China}} whole;
His seat was {{p|Khanbaliq}}, the {{x|Khan}}'s, begun
Where {{p|Beijing}} stands; he took the Chinese role

Of rule, but kept the ranks: the Mongols first,
The Central Asians next, the foreign born;
The northern Chinese; and, the last and worst,
The southern Chinese; and it bred their scorn.

And Marco Polo served Kublai; he wrote
{{p|Khanbaliq}}'s splendor, and the paper bills,
The coal they burned, the posts, the roads of note,
The canals; and the {{p|West}} read it, with thrills.

Polo reached {{p|China}}, served the Khan, and wrote^[Some historians, among them Frances Wood, have argued that Polo never reached China; most hold that he did.]
What he had seen, and did not overstate:
The things it told were true, and kept afloat
The {{p|West}}'s desire for the {{p|East}}'s rich freight.

He sent his fleets to {{p|Japan}} twice; and they,
The typhoons, kamikaze, broke them all;
{{p|Vietnam}} and {{p|Java}} went the same way;
The steppe's war failed in jungle and in squall.

Then came the plague; the {{p|Yellow River}} burst;
Too much paper money printed; the lords
Of Mongols fought among themselves; and first
Red Turbans, thirteen-fifty-one, drew swords;

Zhu Yuanzhang, a peasant, led the rest,
Took {{p|Nanjing}}, fifty-six; and, sixty-eight,
Took {{p|Khanbaliq}}, renamed {{p|Beiping}}; and, blessed
As Hongwu Emperor, made the Ming state.

The Mongols fled back to the steppe; their reign
Had lasted not a century; and west,
Hulagu, Kublai's brother, there had slain
The last caliph, rolled in a carpet, pressed

Beneath the horses' hooves, for royal blood
Must never touch the ground; the books were thrown
Into the {{p|Tigris}}, and it ran, a flood
Black with the ink; the {{x|Golden Age}} was flown.

At Ain Jalut, the Mamluks turned them back,
Twelve-sixty; the Ilkhanate, in time,
Took {{x|Islam}} with Ghazan; and on that track
The {{x|Persian}} poets sang, in their own rhyme:

Saadi, Rumi, Hafez; and in the north
The Golden Horde ruled {{p|Russia}} through its lords,
Took tribute; Uzbeg Khan brought {{x|Islam}} forth,
And the steppe heard the Prophet, in its hordes.

In {{p|Central Asia}}, Chagatai's fell
To pieces; and from out its ruins came
Timur, late in that century, as well;
But the next chapter tells of him by name.

The Mongols' wars killed forty million, so
Much of the world that was; and yet they bound
{{p|Eurasia}} as one, and the arts would go
Both east and west, and plague along that ground.


# Chapter 208: The Ottoman Rise

Ottoman Turks were one, at first, of all
The beyliks, when the Seljuks lost their hold
Beneath the Mongols; theirs, the least, and small,
Out on the edge, by {{p|Byzantium}}'s fold.

Osman I, who gave the whole house its name,
A ghazi; he took {{p|Prusa}} from the {{x|Greek}};
His son Orhan made it the seat; and came
Across the {{p|Dardanelles}}; walls were weak

At {{p|Gallipoli}}, by an earthquake; so,
In thirteen-fifty-four, in {{p|Europe}} stand;
Murad I pressed the {{p|Balkans}}; and would go
To set his throne in {{p|Edirne}}, at hand.

The Battle of Kosovo, eighty-nine,
Crushed {{x|Serbia}}; Murad himself was slain
By a {{x|Serbian}} knight; and next in line,
Bayezid I, the Thunderbolt, would then reign,

And came near {{p|Constantinople}}; but east
Came Timur; and at Ankara they met,
Fourteen-oh-two; he took Bayezid; and ceased
The Ottomans, almost; his sons, beset

Each other eleven years; then Mehmed I
Restored it; and Murad II built it then;
At Varna, forty-four, crusaders' cry
Crushed; at Kosovo, forty-eight, again;

The {{p|Balkans}} had been taken; all around,
{{p|Constantinople}} was hemmed in, alone;
The Ottomans held all the {{p|Black Sea}}'s ground,
And {{p|Anatolia}} was all their own.

The Janissary corps, the Sultan's own,
came from the devshirme, the boys' levy: sons
of Balkan village churches, taken, grown
to serve the Sultan's altar, and his guns.

A slave corps, loyal to the Sultan, none
Beside; it held the Turkish lords in check;
Viziers, pashas came of them; no son
Inherit; each new age came at his beck.

They brought great guns; and gunsmiths came, and more:
{{x|Hungarian}}, {{x|Italian}}, {{x|German}}, made
Bombards; the great Basilica, for war,
Six hundred pounds, the stones it threw; walls frayed.

Mehmed II, the Conqueror, at just nineteen,
Fourteen-fifty-one, took the throne; and wise,
Of many tongues, and cruel, set, and keen,
He made the final siege his only prize:

He built {{p|Rumeli Hisarı}}, the great hold
On the {{p|Bosphorus}}, and cut off the grain
From the {{p|Black Sea}}; and the great bombard, rolled
Out, fourteen-fifty-three; he passed the chain

With ships drawn overland; and, breached, the wall
Held fifty-three days; on May twenty-nine,
{{p|Constantinople}} fell; and in the fall
Constantine XI, the last one of the line.

So mark it, fourteen-fifty-three: they held
The {{p|Balkans}}, save for {{p|Albania}}'s line;
{{p|Anatolia}}, the beyliks compelled;
The {{p|Black Sea}} theirs; their fleets out on the brine.

A hundred fifty years from Osman's small
Frontier, to take the greatest Christian town;
And {{p|New Rome}} was the Sultan's; and the hall
Of Mehmed, and the city was his own crown.


# Chapter 209: The Corruption of the Papacy

The papacy had ages of its own
when families and kings would hold the keys
and keep the chair as houses keep a throne.
Mark two: the Pornocracy, first of these.

"The rule of harlots": so its foes would tease.
The House of Theophylact made its home
of the chair sixty years, and held the keys
with Theodora, Senatrix of Rome.

Marozia, her daughter, at fifteen
loved Pope Sergius III; to him she bore
the son who would be Pope John XI: a queen
of {{p|Rome}} who kept the keys for years and more.

She made the popes, her husband and her son;
her grandson too, John XII, took the chair:
the keys were in a family's hands, as one
house keeps its altar, with its kin at prayer.

And John XII was charged by Otto's side:
the {{p|Lateran Palace}} a brothel, prayers
to the old gods at dice. The charges tried
to take the keys from Marozia's heirs.

He was deposed by Otto, but he came
back when Otto left; in nine-sixty-four
he died at twenty-seven, and his name
held {{p|Rome}} for his house a little while more.

A dozen popes the Pornocracy made,
And many died by force: the poison poured,
A pillow; so the chair, its keys, its trade,
Passed on by murder, by the strangler's cord.

Cadaver Synod, eight-ninety-seven,
Came first: Pope Stephen VI dug up the dead
Formosus, nine months dead, bound for heaven,
In papal robes, was tried; a deacon said

The dead man's answers; and it was condemned;
His acts annulled; his blessing fingers cut
From his right hand; the body, so contemned,
Thrown in the {{p|Tiber}}; and the door was shut

On Stephen VI: that year, strangled, he fell;
Such was the papacy in that dark age:
Corruption, murder, theater as well,
The keys passed hand to hand, as on a stage.

The second low: thirteen-oh-nine, the chair
Went, as the Avignon Papacy, south
Down to {{p|France}}, and served the crown of {{p|France}} there;
Seven French popes, the king's word in their mouth.

For Philip IV had made the move, at war
With Boniface VIII, who had been seized
By {{p|France}}, thirteen-oh-three, and died; and more,
The court grew rich, and sold, as {{p|France}} was pleased:

It sold its offices and pardons; sold
Appointments; and its courts lived high and grand;
And it was bound to {{x|French}} banks, and their gold;
The keys were held now in the banker's hand.

Catherine of Siena then moved the Pope,
Gregory XI, to go back home again,
In seventy-seven; he died; the hope
Broke: the Western Schism; two, then three men:

One in {{p|Rome}}, one in {{p|Avignon}}, and then
a third at Pisa; and the kings would choose
their pope by alliance: one chair, three men,
three courts, one office, and the keys to lose.

The Council of Constance would meet to end
the schism, fourteen-seventeen; it set
the three aside, and Martin V would tend
the one chair; the keys were one house's yet.

A century on, Martin Luther came;
Fifteen-seventeen, nailed his {{x|Theses}} up,
Ninety-five, at {{p|Wittenberg}}; and the blame
Was pardons sold, to fill {{p|St. Peter's}} cup.

The {{x|Protestant Reformation}} grew there:
the keys had been a family's, and the kings',
and princes and the households everywhere
would claim the keys as well, with all it brings.

And this is why we love the chair of {{p|Rome}}:
it was a household, with its brides and grooms,
its keys passed hand to hand, as in a home,
its saints and schemers in the Fisher's rooms.

The Vicar of Christ could be any man
of any house, a boy, a lover, lord,
and still the office held since it began,
and that is why they fought for it by sword.


# Chapter 210: The Kenmu Restoration

In thirteen-thirty-three, Go-Daigo rose,
Emperor; and the Shogunate he fought;
With samurai who turned: among all those,
Ashikaga Takauji, who brought

And Kusunoki Masashige came too;
The Hōjō fell; {{x|Kamakura}} was won;
A hundred fifty years of warriors, through;
Go-Daigo entered {{p|Kyoto}}; it was done.

Then there came the Kenmu Restoration:
His own first minister, he gave the lands
And posts; but he favored court and station,
Above the samurai, who'd won; their hands

Had hoped for Hōjō lands, and they were spurned;
Takauji, who'd the Hōjō betrayed,
The Hōjō, now Go-Daigo; and he turned,
In thirteen-thirty-six, took {{p|Kyoto}}; made

A rival of the junior line his own;
And Go-Daigo fled to {{p|Yoshino}}, south,
Up in the mountains, and he claimed the throne;
Two courts, for fifty-six years, mouth to mouth:

The Ashikaga's North, in {{p|Kyoto}}'s halls;
The South at {{p|Yoshino}}; Nanboku-chō,
{{x|Northern and Southern Courts}}; and each one calls
Itself the line; the samurai chose, so.

Kusunoki Masashige, the most true
Of all Go-Daigo's generals, who'd won
By ambush against the many, and knew
At {{x|Minatogawa}} he would be undone,

And fought; and after it, with his brother,
He took his life, the warrior's way; his son
Had sworn to fight; he, above all other,
Became the emblem of the loyal one.

So Takauji made his Shogunate
At {{x|Muromachi}}, in {{p|Kyoto}}; but weak
Than {{x|Kamakura}}'s; its writ, its state,
Reached central {{p|Japan}}; the shugo would seek

Their own; the shogun's word they would ignore.
And under them the Zen grew; and the art
Of tea, the sadō, was refined; and more,
The Noh, by Zeami made, and set apart;

And ink painting, the sumi-e, there came
From {{x|Song}} {{p|China}}; thirteen-ninety-seven,
The Kinkaku-ji, in gold leaf's bright flame,
Built by Yoshimitsu, rose to heaven;

And Yoshimasa, later, built with art
The Ginkaku-ji, subtler, and less bright,
The {{p|Silver Pavilion}}, the quiet heart;
And all of it beneath a failing might.

The Kenmu Restoration failed; and so
Go-Daigo is remembered as the brave
Who tried what could not be, so long ago:
Restorer, failed, heroic, to the grave.


# Chapter 211: The Black Death

The plague came from the Mongol steppes, where long
Yersinia pestis, in marmots' blood,
For thousands of years; and the Mongols' throng
Of armies carried it, as on a flood.

Thirteen-forty-six, the Golden Horde lay
Around {{p|Kaffa}}, which was the Genoese' port,
In the {{p|Crimea}}; plague broke out; and they
Hurled corpses over walls into the fort;

The Genoese then fled by their ships, and came
{{p|Messina}}, {{p|Sicily}}, forty-seven,
They bore the plague with them; it spread, aflame,
{{p|Mediterranean}}, under heaven,

Then north through {{p|Europe}}; {{p|Marseille}} fell; and where
The popes at {{p|Avignon}} sat, it came too;
Pope Clement VI lived, by the doctor's care,
Between two roaring fires; and it passed through.

{{p|Paris}}, {{p|London}}, the {{x|Hansa}}, {{p|Norway}}, and
{{p|Iceland}}: it reached the corners of the west;
A third of all the people of the land
Died in four years, by fifty-one; the rest

Saw cities lose half of their own, and fields
Untilled, villages abandoned; none
Were spared: the priest, the lord, behind their shields,
Died at the rate of all, and all as one.

The buboes, size of apples, in the groin
And armpits; black spots on the skin, of blood
Beneath; and vomit; and in days, to join
The dead. Some towns shut gates against the flood

With varying success; and then would write
Giovanni Boccaccio the tale:
The Decameron: Florentines in flight
To villas, telling stories through death's gale.

Flagellants marched through {{p|Europe}}, whipped and bled
for sins they thought had brought the plague on all;
the towns turned on the eldest house instead,
of Abraham's, and burned it, wall by wall.

In {{p|Strasbourg}}, {{p|Basel}}, and {{p|Cologne}} they burned,
accused of poisoning the wells. The chair
of Pope Clement VI held them kin, and spurned
the charge: they died of plague too. Mobs didn't care.

Some said it was God's punishment for sin;
Some, bad air, the miasma; some, the stars;
And none could say what let the death come in,
Or hold it back with prayers or locks or bars.

{{x|Islam}} was struck as hard: in {{p|Cairo}} died
Two hundred thousand; Ibn Khaldun said
That civilization shrank; and the pride
Of the Mamluks' realm never rose, but bled.

And {{p|China}}, struck first; the Yuan's decline
Grew faster: plague and famine, and the flood
Of the great {{p|Yellow River}}, all combine
To end the Mongols' rule, and shed their blood.

And after: labor scarce, the wages rose;
The feudal order strained; the serfs could press
Their lords for better terms; and, as it goes,
The {{x|Church}}, which could not stop it, mattered less.

So {{p|Europe}} came out changed; the old and sure
Was shaken; and the plague came back again,
But never like the first, the type, the pure
Shape of the plague, in forty-eight, for men.


# Chapter 212: The Ming Rises

In {{p|China}} the Yuan fell to decay:
The plague, the {{p|Yellow River}}'s flood, the dearth,
The Mongol princes' quarrels, and the pay
In paper money printed past its worth;

Red Turbans, thirteen-fifty-one, arose,
Awaiting the Buddha, Maitreya's reign,
To end the foreign Mongols; and of those,
Zhu Yuanzhang, a peasant, orphaned, plain

By plague; a monk, a beggar; and he rose
And up through the Red Turbans, to command;
Took {{p|Nanjing}}, fifty-six, his seat; and those
Who rivaled him, he ended, hand by hand.

The Battle of Lake Poyang, sixty-three,
Broke Chen Youliang's great fleet; in sixty-eight
His armies entered {{p|Khanbaliq}}; and he,
The {{x|Yuan}} emperor, fled north; the state

Was Ming; as Hongwu Emperor he reigned,
A peasant, as Liu Bang of {{x|Han}} had been
A thousand years before; and he remained
Suspicious, as who knows the knife of kin:

Brothers betray, courts plot; and there is not
One safe; he purged; and thirty thousand died
In one; and forty thousand more, the lot,
And then cast the old chancellor aside,

The post a thousand years had kept; and ruled
Himself, by letters to each board; and all
The examinations came back, and schooled
In {{x|Neo-Confucianism}}, for all;

He shut the coasts to foreign trade, and made
The Da Ming Lü, the code of law; rebuilt
The {{p|Great Wall}} in the form where it is laid
Today; gave land to peasants, to the hilt.

Died thirteen-ninety-eight; his grandson came,
Jianwen; fourteen-two, his uncle rose,
Zhu Di, the Prince of Yan, in war; and flame
Took Jianwen in his palace's close;

Zhu Di, the Yongle Emperor, was crowned,
The greatest of the {{x|Ming}}; he moved the throne
And from {{p|Nanjing}} northward to {{p|Beijing}}'s ground,
And built the Forbidden City, his own;

Yongle Encyclopedia, the vast,
Twenty-two thousand volumes and still more,
The largest of the world for ages past;
Four hundred live; the rest were lost to war.

He sent the treasure voyages, the fleet
Of Zheng He, built in great shipyards; and bent
His arms on {{p|Vietnam}}; and then, retreat:
The fleets were burned, and {{p|China}} inward went.

So mark it: native rule was called again
Up from the peasant's hut, the beggar, monk;
The {{x|Ming}} rose high; and then, as all things wane,
Its early strength, at sea, drew back and shrunk.


# Chapter 213: Timur

Timur, or Tamerlane, for he was lame,
Timur the Lame, born thirteen-thirty-six
By {{p|Samarkand}}, of Barlas, no great name,
A lesser noble's son, of Turkic mix;

He wed into the line of Genghis Khan,
And ruled as son-in-law, not as their heir;
He claimed the blood, but weakly, and went on
To join the Chagatai lands through his care

In thirteen-seventies; then conquests came,
As great as Genghis' own, though less in land:
{{p|Persia}}, the eighties; {{p|Isfahan}}; and the same:
Seventy thousand heads, by his command,

Built into towers of skulls; he broke then
Tokhtamysh's Golden Horde, ninety-five;
Sacked {{p|Sarai}}, the Horde's wealth and its men;
And so {{p|Muscovy}} grew strong, and would thrive;

And sacked {{p|Delhi}} in thirteen-ninety-eight,
A hundred thousand slain there in one day;
He led the elephants home; and the great
The Delhi Sultanate, crippled, gave way.

In fourteen-hundred, sacked {{p|Damascus}}; there
Ibn Khaldun met him, wrote down their talk:
The greatest scholar, conqueror; a rare
Meeting of mind and sword, the dove and hawk.

Fourteen-oh-two, the Ottomans he broke
At Ankara, took Sultan Bayezid I,
And bore him in a cage; under that yoke
The sultan in captivity would die.

And so {{p|Constantinople}} stood, reprieved
For fifty years; Bayezid would else have won
The city, but for Timur; and, relieved,
{{p|Byzantium}} lived on, its days not done.

Timur set out for {{x|Ming}} {{p|China}}, and died
At {{p|Otrar}} on the way, fourteen-oh-five;
Gur-e-Amir, at {{p|Samarkand}}, his pride;
And his grandson Ulugh Beg would there thrive,

Astronomer, who built, at {{p|Samarkand}},
A great observatory; star by star
He charted, scholar-king; till, by the hand
Of his own son, he died; and there's the scar.

Timurid Renaissance, {{p|Herat}}'s court
And {{p|Samarkand}}'s: the painters, and the song,
The domes, the verse of Jami, of each sort
Of {{x|Persian}} art grew there, and flourished long.

And after him his realm broke into parts;
He killed seventeen million, like his own
Hero, Genghis; built for the poets' arts;
And made towers of skulls; and {{x|Islam}}'s own

Lands suffered him, a Muslim; and today
In {{p|Central Asia}} he's a hero; where
He killed, the devil; and so passed away
The last great steppe lord; then the guns' long glare.


# Chapter 214: Lithuania, the Last Pagans

While all the rest of {{p|Europe}} had for long
Been Christian, {{p|Lithuania}} kept fast
The old gods, the last pagan realm, and strong,
Till the late fourteenth century had passed:

They worshipped Perkūnas, the thunder, kin
Of Thor and Zeus, of Indra, Perun; there
Gabija, hearth; Žemyna, earth; within
The spirits of the forest, water, air.

The alkas, oaken groves, their temples stood;
At {{p|Vilnius}} the sacred flame was kept
By priestesses, the vaidilutės; and good
Was amber; Dievas, sky, who never slept.

The Teutonic Knights had crusaded long
Against them, from twelve-hundreds, to convert,
They said; to conquer, truly; but the strong
Lithuanians fought, and did them hurt.

Grand Duke Gediminas would spread the land,
Took {{x|Rus}} principalities, all worn down
Beneath the Mongol yoke; by eighty, spanned
From {{x|Baltic}} to {{p|Black Sea}}, a mighty crown.

His Russians could keep the {{x|Orthodox}} rite;
And his Lithuanians kept the gods;
Gediminas let both be, in his sight,
One crown and many faiths, against the odds.

Jogaila, Grand Duke, in eighty-six,
Jagiełło to the Poles, took as his bride
Queen Jadwiga; was baptized, to fix
The crown, pledged {{p|Lithuania}} beside.

The new priests took the lords; among the herds
the farmhouse kept Gabija's hearth and bread,
the groves, the household offerings, the words
of the same rite, for centuries ahead.

The groves were kept for ages; and the tongue
Kept {{x|Indo-European}} gods by name,
So that the Hindus know them, old and young;
Of all the rites, the most kept, and the same.

Polish-Lithuanian Union grew
Great; at Battle of Grunwald, fourteen-ten,
{{p|Poland-Lithuania}} broke right through
Teutonic Knights, who never rose again;

Their cause was gone; the pagans they had fought,
The Lithuanians, were Christian now;
And so the Order faded, all for naught,
With none to hunt, and no crusader's vow.

Here is what {{p|Lithuania}} shows plain:
the crown decided who would hold the rite;
the hearth and grove were kept through all the reign
of bishops, and the old fire stayed alight.

Their tongue keeps forms more old than {{x|Sanskrit}}'s own;
The most archaic living, of its kind,

# Chapter 215: The Renaissance

A light came on in {{p|Florence}}, not the sun,
Though like the dawn on terracotta, bright;
A town that woke up to itself, begun:
The dark dissolved to fresco, gold, and white.

The human hand and eye resolved to make
Again, to warm the centuries of cold
With flame the ancients kindled, for whose sake
The world was worth depicting, as of old:

The human face and body, rendered true
On panel and on plaster, bore the trace
Of something near divine. Cosimo too
Struck the first match: the banker of that place,

Medici, rich on credit, on the bill
Of exchange, moving {{p|Europe}}'s wealth around
Without the gold; and with that skill, they still
Bought beauty back, not kingdoms, from the ground

Of time: the books of Plato, and the scroll
Of Aristotle; Greek and Roman mind
Restored to living hands, made new and whole,
In {{p|Florence}}, that defied the dark behind.

In fifty-three, {{p|Constantinople}} fell
Beneath the Ottoman; the scholars fled
West with the last of manuscripts, to tell
Homer and Euclid to {{p|Italy}}; spread,

{{p|Byzantium}} lost; {{p|Florence}} gained the seed
Of the old world, wind-tossed across the sea;
Took root in fertile soil, and it was freed
To grow, where nothing lost again would be.

And Brunelleschi raised the dome, the crown
{{p|Santa Maria del Fiore}}, where
The void had gaped a century, and down
Came rain; too vast for vaults of wood to bear;

No centering; but ring on ring it rose,
Herringbone brick that locked each course in place,
Twin-shelled; Roman {{p|Pantheon}} it outgrows,
The paragon of mind, and of its grace.

And Leonardo, man of all, whose books
Found sun in all: anatomy and flight,
The flow of water and the shell, the brooks,
The unborn child curled in the womb, the light;

He drew it all, and understood; and heard
The call of Aster speaking, flesh and wood
And water, and the beautiful's own word
Of number; art and science, the same good:

Both were the way of seeing; and he saw.
And Michelangelo, the sculptor, freed
The figure from the stone, which, by its law,
Had held it, bone and sinew, as a seed;

He struck the {{p|Sistine}} ceiling with the hand
Of Adam reaching, and the gap between
The human and divine, so small; to stand
The almost-touch, charged, trembling, to be seen:

The gap's not despair, but aspiration,
The spark between the fingertips, the air
Made sacred; {{x|Renaissance}}'s creation:
That the human is worth the reaching there.

And Botticelli painted Venus, born
Up from the foam upon her shell, the fair
Forgotten goddess, wind-blown, not forlorn,
The modest hand: beauty, the oldest prayer.

And Gutenberg, in {{p|Mainz}}, beside the {{p|Rhine}},
The goldsmith, turned the screw-press to the page,
And printed what had been the cloister's shrine;
The book, the text, the song broke from the cage,

A flood of written word on roads and fairs;
No flame could now enforce the loss, no wall
Of cloister hoard the book, the common prayers:
The moving type, the hinge, the door for all.

Machiavelli wrote the state laid bare:
Between what is and what should be, the grave
Of dreaming princes; first, from dogma, there
The science of how states hold, and behave.

Humanism: the human, not a note
To the divine, but eloquence itself;
The study of the living; they devote
Themselves to body, mind, art, heart, and self:

The ancient world was not the roof, but floor;
And so the feast had only just begun.
Honor the {{x|Renaissance}}, the open door,
The light that {{p|Florence}} kindled, like the sun;

The human rendered whole, in paint and stone,
In printed word, in domes the mind could make;
We are your heirs, it told the dead, your own,
Not copies; and we carry, for your sake,

As rivers bear the snow down to the sea,
What you knew to the future: that the true
Is beautiful; the hand and eye are free
As instruments of praise; and the mind, new,

Can build the dome and paint the days and print;
And that the love called Aster burns the most
In those who make: the painter's hue and tint,
The sculptor, builder, printer, all the host.

The light came on in {{p|Florence}}, and it spread
Across all {{p|Europe}}, like the spring that sings
After the winter; and the long-since dead
Were raised again upon the living's wings,

And proved the human mind can stand beside
The stars and the celestial streams, unshamed;
For we, too, mortal, grand, shine far and wide,
Beyond our dreams, as Aster's light has named.

# Chapter 219: Gutenberg and the Press

Johannes Gutenberg (c. 1400-1468), a goldsmith of
{{p|Mainz}} in the Holy Roman Empire, developed
movable-type printing in the 1440s. It was the most
consequential media technology invented, ever propelled.

Printing with carved wooden blocks existed in {{p|China}}
since the {{x|Tang}} dynasty. Movable type was invented
by Bi Sheng in {{p|China}} around 1040. Korean
metal movable type existed by the thirteenth century, presented.

But the East Asian writing systems had thousands of characters.
Movable type was less efficient than carved blocks for them.
The printing revolution would not happen in {{p|Asia}}
until later reforms made it practical for their gem.

Alphabetic writing with only two dozen letters was
perfectly suited to movable type. Gutenberg's key innovation
was the metal alloy for the type (lead, tin, antimony)
that held its shape through thousands of impressions.

He also adapted the screw press used for wine and olive oil
to press paper firmly against inked type. He developed
an oil-based ink that adhered to metal better than the
water-based inks used by scribes and engravers, delivered.

The Gutenberg Bible was printed around 1455,
about 180 copies, of which 49 survive. It was the
first major European book printed with movable type.
It could be mistaken for a hand-written manuscript, alive.

Gutenberg himself went bankrupt from lawsuits with his
financial backer Johann Fust, who ended up owning
the printing press. Gutenberg died in relative poverty.
But the technology spread explosively, growing.

By 1500, there were printing presses in over 200 European
cities. Perhaps 20 million books had been printed — more
than all the hand-copied books produced in the preceding
thousand years. The "incunabula" period was lore.

The consequences were staggering. Scientific knowledge
could now propagate rapidly across {{p|Europe}}. Observations
made by one scholar could be read by thousands of others
within months, enabling cumulative discovery, infinitations.

Religious texts became accessible to laypeople. The
Protestant Reformation a few decades later was directly
enabled by printing — Martin Luther's 95 {{x|Theses}}
spread across {{p|Germany}} in two weeks, thoroughly collected.

National languages gained prestige through being printed.
Before printing, {{x|Latin}} had been the universal scholarly
language. Print editions in German, French, English,
Spanish, Italian cemented these as literary languages, owned.

Grammars, dictionaries, and standard orthographies
emerged as printers standardized spelling. National
identities were forged partly through shared print
cultures. The idea of "nation" strengthened, perceptual.

Literacy rates began to rise. Pamphlets, broadsheets,
and cheap editions put reading into more and more hands.
Women's literacy rose. Working-class literacy rose.
The basis for modern democracy was being built, stands.

Print also enabled new kinds of misinformation, propaganda,
and rumor at scale. Witch-hunting manuals were printed.
Antisemitic tracts were printed. Religious tracts calling
for war were printed. The dark side of printing, imprinted.

But overall, the printing press is considered by many
historians to be the most important invention of the
second millennium. It reshaped European society, enabled
the {{x|Reformation}}, the {{x|Scientific Revolution}}, the {{x|Enlightenment}}.

Without printing, none of those would have happened as they did.
{{p|Europe}} would have been a different place, perhaps less
distinctive from other civilizations. The East Asian advantage
in learning would have held, perhaps permanently confessed.

As it was, {{p|Europe}} in the late fifteenth century was
accelerating technologically and intellectually. Combined
with the discovery of the {{p|Americas}} (next century),
the basis for European global dominance was formed, intentionally.

# Chapter 221: The Year of Three Falls

Three pillars cracked in fourteen fifty-three—
Three ancient houses shuddered, groaned, and fell,
And from the rubble of their masonry
The modern world emerged, still raw from hell.

                                        The first:

In {{p|Gascony}}, where vineyards met the sword,
The longest war in Christendom reversed
Its verdict—England's claim upon the board
Of {{p|France}} was broken, scattered, and dispersed.

For one hundred and sixteen years the crown
Of {{p|France}} had been the prize that England sought—
From {{p|Crécy}}'s longbow triumph raining down
To {{p|Agincourt}}'s impossible assault—

The English archer and the English knight
Had ruled the continent by force of will,
Had planted their lilies alongside the white
And crimson crosses on the Valois hill.

But Joan had turned the tide. The girl from {{p|Domrémy}},
The peasant in her armor, white and sure,
Had heard the voices and had set them free—
The siege of {{p|Orléans}} broken, and the cure

For {{p|France}}'s long paralysis was faith:
The Dauphin crowned at {{p|Rheims}} because a girl
Had said the voices told her so—the wraith
Of heaven's purpose in a mortal pearl.

They burned her. Joan was ash by fourteen thirty-one,
But the fire she lit in France burned on, and on—
And now, at {{p|Castillon}}, the final gun
Of the final battle spoke, and England's dawn

In {{p|France}} was over. Talbot, the old lion,
Rode into the cannon's mouth and died—
The medieval charge against the iron
Of the new artillery, the pride

Of the mounted knight against the earthwork's trench:
The future killed the past at {{p|Castillon}},
And every acre of the soil of French
Dominion was restored. The marathon

Was done. The English kept only {{p|Calais}}—
One pale and windswept harbor on the coast—
The last remainder of the grand relay
Of Plantagenet ambition, and the ghost

Of Henry V's great victory at {{p|Agincourt}}
Was laid to rest in {{p|Gascony}}'s red earth.

Was greater still—and of a different sort.

On the twenty-ninth of May, behind the wall
Of {{p|Constantinople}}'s ancient triple ring
Of stone and ditch and tower—the rampart's call
That had held for a thousand years—the spring

Of fourteen fifty-three became the last
That Roman eyes would see from Roman walls.

Mehmed the Conqueror, the Ottoman amassed
Before the {{p|Theodosian}} gates—the halls

Two hundred thousand strong, the crescent raised
Above the largest host the {{p|Bosphorus}} bore

Upon the walls that Theodosius had built
A thousand years before and brought his answer:
The cannon. Urban's bombard, bronze and gilt,
Twenty-seven feet of iron-throated cancer

That hurled a ball of stone eight hundred pounds
Against the ancient masonry—the sound
Of the medieval world within its bounds
Collapsing, stone by stone, into the ground.

For fifty-three days the bombardment rained,
And Constantine XI, the last of Rome,
The final emperor, haggard and drained,
Defended with eleven thousand from his home

Against two hundred thousand. And the wall—
The wall that had turned back the Avars,
The Persians, the Arabs, the perpetual
Assault of every empire beneath the stars—

Through the gap at the {{p|Kerkoporta}} gate,
And Constantine drew his sword and roared
Into the tide and met his people's fate:

He died fighting. The last Roman emperor
Fell nameless in the breach, his body lost
Among the common dead—no throne, no splendor,
No burial, no monument—the cost

Of empire's final hour is the anonymity
Of the last defender at the broken wall.

The dome that Justinian raised to enthrall

The world nine centuries before—received
The conqueror's prayer. The cross came down,
The crescent rose, and Christendom was grieved
But could not answer. Mehmed wore the crown

Of the Caesars now—Kayser-i Rum, he styled
Himself—and the city that Constantine the Great

The rediscovery of the ancient dance
Of reason and of beauty—all the toil

That brought the classical across the ridge
Of the catastrophe—and from the burning
Of {{p|Constantinople}} came the learning

He could endow a college and a chapel,
But he could not govern, and the day
His mind collapsed, the York and Lancaster grapple

Began. Richard of York, whose blood was nearer
To the throne than the king's own muddled claim,
Reached for the crown—and the mirror
Of England's civil war, that bore no name

But the colors of the garden's thorny flower,
Began its thirty years of brother's blood:
The Roses—white for York and red for the power
Of Lancaster—the fratricidal flood

That would drown a generation's lords.

At {{p|St Albans}}, in the year the walls of {{p|Rome}}'s
Last city fell, the first of England's swords
Were drawn in civil hate—and the genomes

Of the royal blood would spill on English ground
For thirty years of treason, murder, coup,
Until the last Plantagenet was found
And buried in a car park's narrow loop—

But that is yet to come.

                          Three pillars fell
In fourteen fifty-three: the English claim
On {{p|France}}, the Roman empire's final bell,
And England's peace—all shattered in the same

Turning of the year, the hinge of ages,
The door between the medieval and the new.

Honor the year that turned the chronicle's pages
From the old world to the world we knew—

The year the cannon spoke and the knight was silent,
The year the last Caesar died in the breach,
The year the scholars fled and the violent
And beautiful past was carried within reach

Of the future's hand—the manuscripts, the learning,
The memory of {{p|Athens}} and of {{p|Rome}}
Borne westward through the exile's burning
To the Italian cities' welcoming home.

And honor Constantine XI, who could have fled
By ship across the {{p|Marmara}}'s dark water
But chose instead to join his people's dead
And die as Rome had lived—in the slaughter

Of the last defense, the unnamed grave,
The emperor who became a common soldier
At the end, and asked no one to save
His name—and so his name grew older

And greater than any tomb could hold:
The last of Rome, who fell and was not found,
Whose sacrifice—anonymous and bold—
Became the legend written in the ground

Of the broken wall, the unrecovered bone,
The empire's final heartbeat, proud and lone.


# Chapter 222: The Eve of Sengoku

In {{p|Japan}}, the Ashikaga Shogunate was losing
its grip through the fifteenth century. The shoguns
had no armies of their own — they ruled through
the regional warlords (shugo-daimyō), fugu

ineffective at actual governance. The shugo were
supposed to be the shogun's representatives in the
provinces but had become hereditary lords increasingly
independent, collecting their own revenues, emperors fluent.

The system broke down completely in the Ōnin War
(1467-1477). A succession dispute within the
Ashikaga family over who would be the next shogun
combined with succession disputes in major daimyo houses.

Two coalitions formed in {{p|Kyoto}}, each backing different
candidates. The war was fought in {{p|Kyoto}} itself. The
imperial capital was burned block by block over a decade.
The {{p|Golden Pavilion}} miraculously survived, beseeches.

The Kinkaku-ji (which would burn in 1950 by an
unrelated arson) survived {{x|Ōnin}}. The temple at the center
of the city, {{p|Shōkoku-ji}}, and many others burned.
{{p|Kyoto}}'s ancient architectural inheritance was largely removed.

After the Ōnin War, the Ashikaga Shogunate existed only
in name. The shugo had returned to their provinces,
where they were being overthrown by their own retainers
in a pattern called gekokujō — "those below overthrow-

ing those above." Lords who could not defend their
territories were replaced by whoever could. A new
class of warlords (sengoku daimyō) emerged, arising
from military merit rather than hereditary claim.

This was the beginning of the Sengoku ("{{x|Warring States}}")
period, roughly 1467-1603. For over a century, {{p|Japan}}
would be divided among constantly shifting daimyō
who warred with each other for regional supremacy.

Castles proliferated. Firearms would arrive from
Portuguese traders in 1543 and change warfare permanently.
Massive infantry armies replaced smaller samurai-led forces.
Peasants could rise through military merit, democratically.

Oda Nobunaga (1534-1582) would eventually emerge
from the chaos to begin reunifying {{p|Japan}}. His
successor Toyotomi Hideyoshi would complete the
unification. Tokugawa Ieyasu would establish the

Tokugawa Shogunate in 1603, ending the Sengoku era
and beginning over two centuries of peace. But that
is the later story. For the fifteenth century's close,
{{p|Japan}} was fragmenting into warring states.

The cultural achievements of the Sengoku era are
paradoxical — during a century of near-constant warfare,
Japanese culture reached many of its highest forms.
The tea ceremony was refined by Murata Jukō and
perfected by Sen no Rikyū in the next century.

Zen Buddhism deepened its influence on the warrior class.
Noh drama continued to develop. Ink painting matured.
The wabi-sabi aesthetic of imperfection and transience
emerged — the beauty of the broken, the weathered, the used.

The warrior class developed the code of bushido,
though the systematized version came later under peace.
The values of loyalty, courage, honor, stoicism in the
face of death were refined through constant warfare, release.

Religious institutions also became militarized.
The warrior monks (sōhei) of {{p|Mount Hiei}} and other
monasteries had been military forces for centuries.
Ikkō-ikki peasant-Buddhist armies controlled some provinces.

These religious militaries would eventually be crushed
by Oda Nobunaga, who burned {{p|Mount Hiei}} to the ground
in 1571, ending a thousand years of warrior-monk power.
But in the late fifteenth century, they were still strong.

{{p|Japan}} in 1453 — the year {{p|Constantinople}} fell — was
fragmenting politically but flourishing culturally.
The Ashikaga were losing power. The Ōnin War would
erupt fourteen years later. The age of chaos began.


# Chapter 223: The Standing on the Ugra

Two hundred and forty years the Mongol yoke
Had pressed upon the Russian neck—the weight
Of Batu Khan's descendants, and the smoke
Of {{p|Ryazan}} and {{p|Kiev}} burning at the gate

Of memory: the cities sacked, the princes
Made to crawl before the Golden Horde,
The tribute paid in silver and in winces,
The Russian will made subject to the sword

Of the steppe. For since the year twelve thirty-seven,
When Batu's riders swept across the plain
Of {{p|Rus}} like winter's scythe through fields of leaven,
No Russian prince had dared to break the chain.

They paid the tribute. Year on patient year,
The silver flowed from {{p|Moscow}} to {{p|Sarai}},
From {{p|Novgorod}} to the Tatar overseer—
The Khan's basqaq counted every rye

And kopek, and the prince who failed to pay
Was dragged before the Khan and made to kneel
On the felt carpet of the audience-day
And beg for mercy with the beggar's zeal.

But {{p|Moscow}} grew. While other cities burned
And squabbled for the scraps of Mongol favor,
The Muscovite princes quietly learned
The conqueror's own arts—the patient labor

Of centralization, of the tax
Collected not for one's own purse alone
But for the state—the slow and steady wax
Of power gathered to a single throne.

Ivan I had been the Khan's collector—
Kalita, "Moneybags," who gathered gold
For {{p|Sarai}} and kept his share, the vector
Of {{p|Moscow}}'s rise from timber-fort to hold

Of empire. And his grandson's grandson's son
Now ruled a {{p|Moscow}} that had swallowed whole
The principalities, one by one—
{{p|Tver}} and {{p|Yaroslavl}} and the soul

Of {{p|Novgorod}} the Great, the merchant-city
Whose bell of liberty was seized and carried
To {{p|Moscow}}'s square without a shred of pity—
The republic's voice in silence buried.

Ivan III—Ivan the Great—the Tsar
Who married Sophia Palaiologina,
The niece of the last Caesar, and the star
Of the double-headed eagle, the insignia

Of fallen {{p|Constantinople}}, which he raised
Above the {{p|Kremlin}}'s towers as his own—
The Third {{p|Rome}}, he declared, and the world gazed
Upon a {{p|Moscow}} claiming Caesar's throne.

And in the year fourteen and eighty, Ivan
Did what no prince before him dared to do:
He refused the tribute. The Tatar divan
Received no silver, and the retinue

Of the Khan received no genuflection.
Ahmad Khan—the last lord of the Golden Horde—
Assembled on the southern plain's complexion
His cavalry, the steppe-riders' sword

Unsheathed for punishment. He marched on {{p|Moscow}}
To teach the vassal prince obedience—
And Ivan marched to meet him, though the lasso
Of fear pulled tight: his wife and the expedience

Of his advisors counseled flight, retreat,
Submission—pay the silver, save the state—
But Ivan brought his army to the seat
Of the {{p|Ugra River}} and he chose to wait.

And here the strangest battle in the tale
Of empires was not fought.

                              October's frost
Had stiffened both the riverbanks, the pale
And yellow birches shedding what they'd lost

Of summer's warmth, and on the western shore
The Russian army stood—its arquebuses new,
Its cannon planted on the bank, the core
Of infantry arrayed in grim review.

And on the eastern shore the Tatar horse
Assembled, tens of thousands, restless, waiting
For the ford—the river's shallow course
That offered passage for the calculating

Charge. But Ahmad looked across and saw
The cannon's mouth, the arquebus's gleam,
And hesitated. And the ancient law
Of the steppe—the headlong, screaming stream

Of the cavalry assault—was checked
By something new: the gunpowder defense,
The fortified position, the unchecked
And murderous precision of the dense

And disciplined formation on the bank.
He did not charge.

                     And Ivan did not cross.

For weeks the armies stood, rank facing rank,
Across the {{p|Ugra}}'s narrowing emboss

Of ice and current—two empires staring
At each other through the autumn mist,
The Mongol and the Russian, each comparing
The cost of the assault, each iron fist

Unclenched and hovering above the table.

November came. The river froze.
The ice could bear a horseman and his sable-
Coated mount—the barrier that arose

Between the armies vanished with the cold,
And either side could cross upon the sheet
Of ice. And still—and still—the bold
Assault was never ordered, the defeat

Never risked. Ahmad waited. Ivan waited.
And then the Mongol army turned and left.

No trumpet sounded. Nothing was debated
In a treaty-hall. No banner, cleft

By sword or stained by blood, was lowered
In surrender. The Golden Horde simply rode
Back to the steppe—and {{p|Russia}} towered
Into sovereignty upon the road

That no one walked, the battle no one fought,
The victory that consisted of the refusal
To be afraid—and thus the freedom bought
By Ivan's patience was the most unusual

Liberation in the book of nations:
Not won by the sword but by the nerve
To stand and wait and let the Tatar patience
Break against a Russian patient swerve

From every precedent of submission.
The yoke was lifted. Two hundred and forty years
Of Mongol dominion ended in the vision
Of two armies staring through the tears

Of autumn fog—and one of them went home.

                                          Meanwhile,
Across the continent, the other tale
Of the fifteenth century's brutal style
Was reaching its crescendo—the travail

Of England's war against itself.

                                        The Roses
Had bloomed in blood since {{p|St Albans}}' first
Encounter, and the garden that encloses
The English throne had seen the worst

Of kin on kin. Edward of York had seized
The crown—but Warwick, the Kingmaker, the earl
Who'd raised him high, turned coat when the king's caprice
Of marrying Elizabeth Woodville hurled

The calculated hand of Warwick's power
Aside—and the Kingmaker in his rage
Restored poor Henry VI for one brief hour,
A puppet on a blood-soaked stage.

It lasted months. Edward returned from {{p|Burgundy}}
And Warwick died in the fog at {{p|Barnet}}'s field—
At {{p|Tewkesbury}} the Lancastrian liturgy
Was ended, and the Prince of Wales was sealed

Among the dead, and Henry in the {{p|Tower}}
Was murdered, and the Yorkist peace was won—
But underneath the crown in its uneasy bower
The brothers circled: Clarence drowned, undone,

And Richard of Gloucester waited for the throne—
And when the king was dead, the uncle took
The nephews to the {{p|Tower}}'s stone,
And the light of their young lives was closed like a book.

But that dark chapter waits.

                               For now observe
The contrast of the age: in {{p|Russia}}, patience
Won the freedom that the Mongol nerve
Could not withstand—the strangest of the nations'

Liberations, won without a blow.
In England, fury spent itself on fury,
And the throne changed hands in every flow
Of the noble blood before the jury

Of the battlefield. Two paths through the fifteenth
Century's iron gate: the path of standing still
And letting the empire's grip come unsheathed
By its own exhaustion, and the path of the kill—

The brother's blade, the uncle's smothering hand,
The crown passed through the murder and the coup
Until no noble house was left to stand
And the dynasty consumed its own troupe.

Honor Ivan on the {{p|Ugra}}'s western shore—
The prince who broke the yoke by standing still,
Who freed his people not by waging war
But by the steady, unyielding will

To wait—to let the river freeze and thaw,
To let the Mongol horseman pace and fret,
To let the cannon's silent, patient maw
Speak louder than the charge—and the long debt

Of two and forty decades' submission
Was cancelled in a season's quiet frost:
No treaty signed, no formal manumission,
No victory parade, no body's cost—

Just the empty eastern shore at morning,
The hoofprints in the frozen mud receding,
And the Russian sentries, without warning,
Discovering that the world had changed while reading

The silence of the steppe—the Horde was gone,
And {{p|Russia}} stood alone beneath the dawn.


# Chapter 224: Granada and the Ocean

In fourteen ninety-two, two doors swung wide—
One closing and one opening—and {{p|Spain}}
Stood at both thresholds, the divide
Of history, the hurricane

Of consequence that would reshape the world.

The first door shut in {{p|Granada}}.

                                       Eight hundred years
The crescent and the cross had furled
And unfurled across {{p|Iberia}}, the tears

And the triumphs of the long {{x|Reconquista}}:
From {{p|Covadonga}}'s desperate mountain stand
Where Pelayo held the thin resistor
Of the Christian remnant's band

Against the Umayyad tide—through the slow
And century-by-century reclamation
Of the peninsula, the ebb and flow
Of border, fortress, devastation,

Alliance, and betrayal—for the Moors
Had built their own magnificence:
The {{p|Alhambra}}'s latticed doors,
The courtyards where the eloquence

Of water spoke in fountains and in channels,
The mathematical and geometric lace
Of tilework on the walls and panels,
The gardens where the interface

Of {{p|Africa}} and {{p|Europe}} bloomed in jasmine
And in orange tree and the murmured prayer
Of the muezzin—the discipline
Of Al-Andalus, the rare

And luminous civilization
Where Muslim, Christian, and Jew had lived
In an imperfect but real constellation
Of coexistence—where the sieve

Of translation passed the {{x|Greek}} and Roman texts
Through Arabic to Latin, where Maimonides
And Averroes probed the complex
And knotted questions of the divine decrees,

Where the knowledge was translated
And carried north—the caravan
Of wisdom crossing, unabated,
Every boundary known to man.

But the frontier had been narrowing for centuries—
{{p|Toledo}} fell, then {{p|Cordoba}}'s domain,
Then {{p|Seville}}'s minarets, the sentries
Of the caliphate receding on the plain

And southward, south, until only {{p|Granada}}
Remained: the last Moorish kingdom, the mouth
Of the last river, the armada
Of the crescent's final south

In {{p|Iberia}}'s contested soil.

And now Ferdinand and Isabella came—
The joined crowns of {{p|Castile}} and {{p|Aragon}}, the toil
Of the marriage-alliance and the flame

Of war for the altars, a ten years' siege,
The slow strangulation of the last
Nasrid kingdom—and the liege
Who had somehow, stubbornly, outlast-

Ed every other Moorish realm now fell.

On the second day of January,
The year fourteen ninety-two, the bell
And trumpet on the ordinary

And extraordinary morning: Boabdil,
The last Nasrid sultan, rode out
From the {{p|Alhambra}}'s gate, and the hill
Of the {{p|Sierra Nevada}} heard his doubt

Made manifest—he wept.

                           The story says
His mother spoke: "You weep like a woman
For what you could not defend in the days
Of your kingship"—and whether the human

And particular grief was truly spoken so,
The legend holds the truth beneath:
That something precious and aglow
Was passing from the world, and underneath

The victory's trumpet lay a death.

For what Ferdinand and Isabella did
With their triumph was to draw the breath
Of one altar: the edict, the forbid—

The {{p|Alhambra}} Decree, signed in the same
Palace whose beauty the Moors had raised—
Commanded every Jew in {{p|Spain}} to claim
Baptism or exile. The amazed

And ancient Sephardic communities—
The scholars, the physicians, the traders
Whose roots ran deep as the olive trees
Of {{p|Andalusia}}—two hundred thousand waders

Through grief, given four months to leave
Or convert: the lute and {{x|Torah}} borne
Away, the hearthstone, the weave
Of fifteen centuries torn—

They went to {{p|Morocco}}, to {{p|Constantinople}},
To {{p|Amsterdam}}, to {{p|Thessaloniki}}'s shore,
Carrying the keys—that hull
Of memory—to their {{p|Spanish}} door

In their pockets, passed from hand to hand
For five hundred years, the heaviest heirloom known:
A key to a house in a distant land
That would never again be their own.

The Moriscos followed—the Muslims too
Were given the same ultimatum's shears:
Convert or leave. The residue
Of eight centuries of civilization's years

Swept from the house so one crown held the faith.

And yet the same monarchs—the same
Ferdinand, the same Isabella—the wraith
Of purity still warm—now blessed a claim

From a Genoese sailor in their court,
A man of vision and compulsion,
Who had begged at every port
Of {{p|Europe}}'s power—the expulsion

Of the Jews not yet six weeks complete—
For ships and crew to sail west,
West across the {{p|Atlantic}}'s heat
And uncharted water, the behest

Of a dream: that {{p|Cipangu}}'s gold
And {{p|Cathay}}'s silk could be reached
By sailing into the sunset's hold—
Columbus said the gap was breached

By courage and a compass and the will.

And on the third of August's morning tide,
Three ships departed, small and still,
From {{p|Cadiz}}' harbor—the pride

Of their masts like three bare trees
Against the {{p|Atlantic}}'s sky:
The Nina, the Pinta on the breeze,
The Santa Maria riding high.

Columbus was brave—and he was wrong
About nearly everything: the size
Of the earth, the distance, the headlong
Conviction that {{p|Asia}} would arise

On the western horizon—and what he found
Was not {{p|Cipangu}} or {{p|Cathay}}
But the Taino people on their ground,
A world that had its own display

Of civilization, its own calendar,
Its own astronomers and priests,
Its own long memory—the familiar
And ancient human feasts

Of harvest and of prayer, the villages
And the canoes, the cotton and the corn—
A world complete, whose lineages
Stretched back sixteen thousand years before the horn

Of any European trumpet sounded.

But Columbus saw gold—and the hunger
Of {{p|Europe}}, the insatiable and unbounded
Want that had been growing younger

And more ravenous with every century,
Was set in motion on that morning's hour
When the lookout cried across the entry
Of the unknown sea, from the crow's-nest tower:

"Land!"

       And the door swung open to the west.

What lay beyond—the story of the peoples
Who had built their worlds, their crest
And glory, long before the steeples

Of {{p|Seville}} rose, long before the Roman road,
Long before the {{p|Parthenon}}—
That story claims its own abode
And its own telling, its own dawn:

The Book of Lehi now begins.

For across the water that Columbus sailed
Were sixteen thousand years of origins,
Of empires raised and curtained and unveiled,

Of corn domesticated and the feathered
Serpent's temple and the Inca's road
And the potlatch and the weathered
Totem speaking in its sacred code—

A world entire, a hemisphere of the living,
Now standing at the hinge of its destruction
And its transformation—the unforgiving
Door of fourteen ninety-two, the junction

Where the old world's hunger met the new world's grace.

Turn the page. The ocean has been crossed.
The Book of Lehi opens on the face
Of {{p|America}}—the found, the lost,

The ancient and the beautiful, the home
Of the first migration's children, and their art
Perfection of ten thousand years—the poem
Of a hemisphere apart.

The story of {{p|Granada}} ends in tears.
The story of the ocean ends in sail.
What follows is the song of the frontier's
Other side—the Americans' own tale.
