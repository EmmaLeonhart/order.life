# Chapter 281: The Harvest at the Ancestors' Hall

In autumn, {{p|China}}, when the grain was in,
Families to the hall set out the first
Of harvest at the tablets of the kin:
Rice, millet, incense, bows, names read; the worst

Winter would come; first, thanks. This is the day.
Keep that table in your eye: our own rite,
And the quarrel round it was ours. The way
It ran: who holds the table, by what right.

Ricci had made his wager: let them bow;
Let the believer keep his fathers' hall;
His order, the Jesuits, kept that vow.
And {{x|Tian}} and {{x|Shangdi}} named the God of all.

A century. Then Dominicans came,
With the Franciscans, to the farms, and saw
Food set for the dead, and gave it a name,
And called it idol-worship, by their law.

They said the names {{x|Tian}} and {{x|Shangdi}} were wrong
For God. But the heart of it was the hall:
Who sets the bowl, who reads the names, whose song
Is sung there, and whose keys are over all.

The quarrel went to {{p|Rome}} and {{p|Paris}}, where
The clergy argued it for decades, men
Who never saw the land, nor the hall's prayer.
The Jesuits asked the emperor then.

In seventeen hundred, Kangxi, the great:
The rite to Confucius thanks one who taught;
Signed, for the Jesuits, as word of state:
The rite in the hall is love, and asks naught

Of blessing from the dead; {{x|Tian}} is the name
The {{x|Lord of Heaven}}'s. The throne made the call.
And {{p|Rome}} said: not his to settle; the same
Keys could not be shared, not over the hall.

In seventeen-oh-four the Pope decreed:
No {{x|Tian}}, no {{x|Shangdi}}; just {{x|Tianzhu}}, the name
Ricci had made; no tablet that would read
*Seat of the soul*; then the heart of the claim:

No {{x|Christian}} at the teacher's rite, and none
At the year's offerings in the fathers' hall.
The whole accommodation was undone.
The keys would keep the dead. And that was all.

A legate, Tournon, took it to {{p|Beijing}};
The emperor heard him, asked of the page:
Could Maigrot, who began it, read a thing?
He could not read a character. The rage

Was the throne's: such a man to judge the wise!
The legate, sent to {{p|Macau}}, held, and then
By {{x|Portuguese}}, for their rights, with their eyes,
He died, in seventeen hundred and ten.

{{p|Rome}} sealed the ban in seventeen-fifteen,
And Kangxi, with his red brush, wrote: and so,
"Men of the West are small," wrote in between:
"They need not preach here, to avoid a row."

His son shut {{x|Lord of Heaven}}'s teaching out;
{{p|Rome}} made each priest forswear the rites, by vow;
The {{x|Christian}} could not bring, through long years' drought,
His harvest to the dead; but, hold it, now:

The converts wept, and had, by keys, to choose
The priests' altar or their dead; not the king
Had barred them, but the keys; which one to lose?
Hold that on {{x|Harvest Day}}; and hold this thing:

A Pope has called the rites civil: to bow
Is free to {{x|Christian}}s, and the tablets stand
In line with faith; the wager, right; and now
The throne, too, right; the bow, love, in the land:

The thanks at harvest to the ones before,
The bow to the tablet, grandmother's name,
Was love. The light of this day, and much more,
In Gaian days falls on harvests, the same:

The first fruits of {{p|Americas}}, the thanks
Of their first peoples; rice of {{p|Japan}}; more:
{{p|Canada}}'s turkey; and the hall, whose ranks
Of fast and slow come home; and, at the door,

Steeples and shrines and halls: the harvest, theirs;
Give thanks for food and the hands that grew it;
Give thanks to those before: the bowl, the prayers;
None has the right to forbid it. Do it.


# Chapter 282: The Names of Everything

A pastor's son, at {{p|Råshult}}, {{p|Småland}}, born
Seventeen-seven; birches round the manse;
His father, Nils, a farmer's son, had worn
No surname: Nils Ingemarsson; the chance

At university, a {{x|Latin}} name;
He made one from a linden on the farm,
A *lind*: Linnaeus. Hold, then, in this book's frame,
A tree filled a name; the patronym's charm,

Ingemar's son, Nils's son, then gave way
To chosen names; and the son, Carl, would spend
Names to all living things; in garden-play
He asked their names; forgot; and, in the end,

His father: "I will not tell you again."
He did not forget again; poor, he went
To {{p|Uppsala}}, birch-bark shoes; and the plain
Of {{p|Lapland}}, thirty-two, he walked; and spent

Two thousand kilometres; then he wore
The Sámi costume for his portrait, drum
And reindeer boots; learned from them, wrote their lore;
In thirty-five, at {{p|Leiden}}, came the sum:

*{{x|Systema Naturae}}*, a gleaming crown
Of nature's three kingdoms: beast, plant, and stone,
In class and order, genus, kind; set down
The plants by sex: the stamens, pistils, shown

As husbands, wives; the pious, shocked, would cry
"Loathsome harlotry"; so he named a weed
After the man. In fifty-three: the high
Gift of two names, the genus, species' seed:

*{{x|Bellis perennis}}*, the daisy; the key
Of the binomial still opens all
The living world. In fifty-eight, made free,
He put us in: *{{x|Homo sapiens}}*, tall,

Wise man, among the primates, with great apes;
The order of the monkeys; and the eyes
Of theologians, sin; and no escapes:
"Tell me a difference," he wrote, "the wise

Of nature's laws would show: none that I know."
None. Pride's first plain mirror. And in those same
Pages he split our kind in four; and so
*Americanus*, and *europaeus*, came,

*Asiaticus*, *afer*; and to each
A temper, rank: the {{x|European}}, law;
The {{x|African}}, caprice; so did he teach.
Hold both: this, and the apes, in nature's law;

He sent his students out, the seventeen
Apostles, to the capes and ice and lands
Of jungle; a third died, the roads between;
In sixty-one, ennobled by king's hands:

As Carl von Linné; "God made, and Linnaeus set
In order," so he said; half true; he died
Seventy-eight; {{p|Uppsala}}'s church; yet
This book, from cosmos born, and life, its wide

Descent through fish and reptiles to our race,
Owes him its names; his system cut the land
Of the living to a register's place;
A gift: to know the names, respect firsthand;

Of those we share the earth with; {{x|Lifeist}} calls
Each one by name, an advocate; the pith
Of reverence is attention; the walls
Between us: none; our register, one kith:

*{{x|Homo sapiens}}*: one kin, and one song;
The linden tree in {{p|Småland}}'s day is done;
The name it gave is in the sun; and long
His names are spoken, each, the living, one.


# Chapter 283: No Place Empty

After the turbaned messiah, the Jews
Of {{p|Poland}} and {{p|Ukraine}}, poor, shamed; the learned
Proud of their {{x|Talmud}}; and the poor, who'd use
The words they could not read, were told, and spurned,

That they were nothing; and, in borderland,
In {{p|Podolia}}, an orphan boy was born,
Israel, son of Eliezer, whose hand
Wrote no book; holier than scholars' scorn;

They sent him as the teacher's help, to walk
The children to school; he walked them singing,
Taught them sung prayers; dug clay; would not talk;
The {{p|Carpathian}} hills; his secret's bringing

Came late, at thirty-six; a *baal shem*, he,
Master of the Name, healer, charms, the best;
They called him Baal Shem Tov: the praise, the plea,
As Master of the Good Name; and, at rest,

His home at {{p|Medzhybizh}}, near forty; law
He kept, but taught another heart: there's none,
No place empty of Him: the field, the straw,
The tavern, stranger, evil thought; each one;

Each spark the Kabbalists had mourned, below,
In things, waits to be lifted up with joy,
Not fasting; joy; the sad are far; and so
The simple near; and this one, of the boy:

On Yom Kippur a shepherd who can't read
Stood all day in the synagogue, his heart
Full, with a shepherd's pipe; and, at the need
Of closing prayer, he blew it; and the start

Of sound in that room made the men turn wild;
Baal Shem Tov: "The gates of heaven were shut;
Our prayers could not unlock them; but the child,
His pipe, a wordless heart, broke them"; the rut

Of learning was no road; the heart, the road.
He wrote nothing; students wrote him: the great
Maggid of {{p|Mezhyrichi}}; and the load
Of tales the poor passed on, and softened weight;

And like the water, spread through villages
Hasidism: pious, dancing in prayer,
A rebbe's court, the feasting's images
Of song drew in the poor; and then, aware,

The Vilna Gaon, the greatest scholar, came
{{p|Vilnius}}: excommunicated them
Seventy-two: "Dancers, not scholars"; same,
The quarrel cooled in time; both held the hem

Of the Jews of the East, {{x|Jewish}}, as one;
And here's the thing for this book: rebbes' courts
Grew dynasties: from father on to son,
The rebbe's coat held by heirs; and supports

Of holy kin, kept as with any king;
His great-grandson, Nachman of Breslov, taught
The world's a narrow bridge, and the main thing
Is not to fear at all; and his grave's brought

To {{p|Uman}} thousands each new year, to pray;
Hold this: the holy is in each, and there
In families, too; the courts, the line, the way
Of rebbes' kin; and keep the shepherd's air:

The pipe's the part to keep; and this, which shares
The {{x|Gaiad}} with the Baal Shem Tov: that none
Is empty; sacred life in all: the stairs,
The straw, the stranger, smallest thing, each one;

The plenty in all things; lift it with joy;
The sad are far from sacred; sing to school;
No words? Then blow the whistle, like the boy;
What you had was enough; the heart, the rule.


# Chapter 284: The Pact at Diriyah

{{p|Najd}}, the high heart of {{p|Arabia}}, there,
From {{p|Istanbul}} and {{p|Cairo}}, palm and clay,
Where people hung rags on trees, asked the prayer
Of saints; and one, severe, came home one day,

The scholar al-Wahhab, the one who taught,
Home from {{p|Medina}}, {{p|Basra}}: "This is not
{{x|Islam}}. For God is one; no saint is sought,
No tree, no tomb, no prophet, may be got

To intercede." He reached back past the time,
The Sufi orders, shrines, saints' feasts, the schools
Of law and commentaries, to the prime
Beginning, as he read it; and his rules,

*{{x|The Oneness of God}}*, simple, absolute;
At {{p|Al-Uyaynah}}, with its emir, he
Cut down a sacred tree; tore, at the root,
The tomb of Zayd ibn al-Khattab; he

Had a woman stoned. The town grew afraid;
The emir's lord sent him away; he went
To {{p|Diriyah}}, dry riverbed, and made
In forty-four, with its emir, content:

Muhammad ibn Saud; the preacher said:
"Your sword and your protection; I, the creed,
And right to rule in it." The Saud were wed
To land; the Al ash-Sheikh, the rite, indeed;

Two dynasties in one hand, married through
Each generation, to this day; and hold
This, in a book of kin: two trees that grew,
Bound by a promise in a mud-brick fold

Of conquest: Al-Muwahhidun, as one
God's oneness; and their foes' name for them stuck:
*{{x|Wahhabi}}*. They took {{p|Najd}}, and further won;
North to {{p|Karbala}}, in {{p|Iraq}}; and struck

The holy city of the Shia, where
Husayn, the Prophet's grandson, died; they killed
Thousands, stripped the shrine; took {{p|Mecca}}, and, there,
{{p|Medina}}; {{p|Baqi}}'s domes, the kin's, they spilled

To ground; the pilgrims of another way
Turned back. Ottomans sent {{x|Egyptian}} might,
Seven years; in eighteen-eighteen, razed lay
{{p|Diriyah}}; Abdullah beheaded; night.

The realm won; the idea had not; kept
In desert, it returned; and, by the sword,
Young Ibn Saud retook {{p|Riyadh}}; and, swept,
{{p|Baqi}}'s domes fell again; the oil outpoured

Beneath the sands; the kingdom of his name
Stands, and the preaching, quiet, loud, goes forth
With oil's wealth; still the pact, the two, the same
Families, sheikh and emir, south and north,

To mosques and schools across the world. Now hold
This close: it is the spine of this whole book.
The fight was over graves: who may enfold
The dead in a dome, who may come and look.

The domes at {{p|Baqi}} kept the Prophet's kin;
The preacher said no grave should stand between;
A rag on a tree let the widow in.
The one God and the asker; keep it clean.

So the dispute was ours, the oldest one:
Who may honour which dead, and by whose leave;
The sheikh's house and the sword, or everyone
Who brought a rag to the saint's tomb, to grieve.

A faith, a state, two family trees, one pact:
That is a thing this book can honour whole;
The house of the sheikh holds the rite, intact,
The emir's house the land: one house, one soul.

What the sword cut away was ours as well:
The Sufi lodges, saints' feasts, and the shrine
Where Muslims, Christians, Jews would kneel and tell
Their prayers side by side, as kin of one line.

Hold the tree they cut down. Its shade was free;
The one who prayed beneath it is our own.
God is one, the preacher said; so are we:
One house, with room for rag and dome and stone.


# Chapter 285: The Assessor of the Board of Mines

His father, Jesper Swedberg, {{p|Skara}}'s head
As bishop, saw the angels, talked with them;
Ennobled, seventeen-nineteen; instead
Of Swedberg, Swedenborg; a name, a stem

Made new, as the linden named Linnaeus' line;
The son, Emanuel, in {{p|Stockholm}} born,
Sixteen eighty-eight, practical and fine,
Sat thirty years on the Board of Mines, sworn

To copper, iron, safety in the shafts,
The kingdom's wealth; a scientist of all:
A flying machine, fixed-wing, paper drafts,
The brain: the cortex held the mind, its wall

Of parts, each part its own; the planets, spun
From a cloud round the sun; learned {{x|Hebrew}}; famed;
At fifty-five, in forty-three, begun,
Dreams; and the granite cracked; kept, unashamed,

A journal, tender, of a man undone:
Temptations, terrors, Christ appearing; and,
In April forty-five, in {{p|London}}, one
Inn, after dinner, came a man, at hand:

"Do not eat so much"; and that night, the Lord
Appeared, and gave a task: to open wide
The inner meaning of the scripture stored;
He gave up science, all but that; and plied

Thirty years' books in {{x|Latin}}, nameless, sent
From {{p|London}}: *{{x|Heavenly Secrets}}*, in eight,
On Genesis and Exodus; he went,
*{{x|Heaven and Hell}}*; he walked the spirit's state

Each day, as down a street, and with the dead
He talked: that heaven is a state of love,
Not a reward; the angels, men, instead;
That married love goes on; and that the dove,

The light, the water, tree: each natural thing
Corresponds to a spirit one; the day
{{x|Last Judgment}} came in fifty-seven: ring
In spirit world, unseen on earth; the way

In fifty-nine, at {{p|Gothenburg}}, he dined,
Four hundred kilometres off; turned pale,
Went out, came back: "{{p|Stockholm}} burns"; and, behind,
Hour after hour, he told the guests the tale;

At eight: "Thank God, it stopped, three doors away
From mine"; the courier, days on, said so;
It was; and Kant at {{p|Königsberg}}, the way
Mocked it in *{{x|Dreams of a Spirit-Seer}}*; though

He could not let it go. Hold, whole, the man,
As Bureus, Newton: mines, brain, and the sun
Cloud, and the visions; like Newton, one plan,
Seer, scientist, one button, pressed: and done,

"I've seen; believe or not." He did not ask
For a church. Dying in {{p|London}}, he knew:
When John Wesley wrote to meet him, his task
Was answered: "I die March twenty-ninth"; true,

He died that day. His readers made, and kept,
The New Church; and his seeds became the trees
Of orchards where his readers' footsteps stepped;
The {{x|Gaiad}} says: here's one who's sure, who sees

The other world, which planted trees in him;
Each natural thing corresponds: the tree,
The water, the pure light; plant, at the rim,
The tree; and leave the page; walk barefoot, free,

With seeds, in a strange country; and the yield
Is for those you won't meet. His bones lie, home,
In {{p|Uppsala}}'s church, near Linnaeus, sealed;
The farthest visions, nearest names: one dome.


# Chapter 286: All Knowledge in One Structure

It started as a job; Le Breton planned
Chambers' *{{x|Cyclopaedia}}* into {{x|French}},
Hired a cutler's son, {{p|Langres}}', in hand,
Denis Diderot and d'Alembert; the wrench:

They would not translate; they would make it new:
A dictionary, reasoned, of all things,
The sciences, arts, crafts, to change, all through,
The common way of thinking; prison's stings

Already, {{p|Vincennes}}: three months, a line
The blind that doubted God; he knew the risk.
First volume, fifty-one; the chart, the sign,
A tree of human knowledge, branches brisk:

From Francis Bacon: Memory, which grows
History; Reason, philosophy; arts
Imagination; God, a twig, there shows
On Reason's branch, filed with the mind's own parts

Of world and heaven in one human scheme;
The quarrel was who files the tree. The words:
Seventy-two thousand entries; a team
Of writers: Voltaire, Rousseau, and the herds

Of the forgotten; de Jaucourt, one, sure,
Wrote seventeen thousand, unpaid, and paid
Secretaries, himself; his name kept pure:
Plain labour; and subversion, slyly laid

In dull entries: cannibals, at the end:
*See: {{x|Eucharist}}*: a jab from kin at kin.
The plates: eleven volumes, made to send
Of workshops, not kings: how to step within

The forge, the loom, the press, the mine, the frame
Of stockings, glass and pins: tool and motion;
Hands' knowledge set beside the mind's, the same
In dignity: the whole book's devotion:

The artisan, philosopher of craft.
Banned, of course: the king's council, fifty-two;
Fifty-nine, privilege revoked, the draft
Illegal, Pope condemning; turned on two

Men: the chief censor, of all men, himself,
Malesherbes, who believed, warned him of the raid;
Hid manuscripts in his own house, his shelf,
Where the police would not look; and, afraid,

The publisher, Le Breton, took the proofs
Of the last ten, cut each dangerous line;
And Diderot found out, sixty-four; the roofs
Of his life fell; years' work and the design,

Cut by the man who'd paid; he wept with rage;
He finished; the last plates, seventy-two;
And, needing his daughter's dowry, the sage
Sold Catherine of {{p|Russia}} books; she knew

Their worth; he kept them, paid as their keeper;
Hold the whole of it, as this book has learned:
Register to free the mind, and deeper;
The Bacon tree, God under reason turned;

The hands, the crafts, honoured; the censor, brave;
The publisher who cut. And still the dream:
Now, anyone can edit; and the wave
Of many hands makes the largest, a stream

Ophiuchus tells; give thanks: twig and tree,
To Jaucourt's seventeen thousand; the plate
Of the weaver's lever: many hands, free;
Write knowledge down, and make it free, and great.


# Chapter 287: The Bard Who Never Was

In forty-six, {{p|Culloden}}'s moor: there broke
The last {{x|Jacobite}} host; the realm, to stay,
Unmade the {{p|Scottish Highlands}}: clans, the yoke
On chiefs; the tartan, kilt, and plaid's display

Banned for thirty-five years; the Gaelic world
Was being cleared; in sixty, from that land,
James Macpherson, a tutor, young, unfurled
In {{p|Edinburgh}} a small book, at hand:

*{{x|Fragments of Ancient Poetry}}*, the voice
Of Ossian, blind bard of ages gone,
Son of Fingal, sang Oscar, dead; his choice
Of mist, ghosts on the wind, the grey sea's dawn,

The moon, the fallen, and the harp. The stir
Was instant: gentlemen paid him to go
Up to the {{p|Highlands}}, more, the Gaelic burr;
He brought back *Fingal*, sixty-one; and so

*{{x|Temora}}*: Homer of the North, in sight,
The islands' {{x|Iliad}}; and {{p|Europe}} wept;
{{x|Romantic}} age rose on those pages' light;
Goethe's young Werther read him, before he stepped

Into his death, aloud, to her he loved;
And {{p|Sweden}}'s kings, today, bear Oscar's name,
Born of the bard's son; and his, beloved,
Selma, Fingal's hall, a girl's name, the same:

Selma Lagerlöf bore it; and, as one,
Jefferson called him greatest ever; and
Then Samuel Johnson, in {{p|London}}, as one
Who doubted, asked for manuscripts in hand;

Macpherson had them in the glens: the songs
Of old {{x|Fenian}} tales still sung, alive;
From them he made the whole; and so belongs
The bard to them; the ancient voice, to thrive

In mist and harp and ghosts; the glens, the lands,
The singers, cleared, with families, to sea
For {{p|Canada}}; the tartan, banned, in hands
Of clans came back again, as it should be:

Tradition made; and now, the thing to say,
The mirror on this book: the one who made
This scripture said of it, in her own way:
It feels ancient, she said, though newly made,^[Her words: "It feels like an ancient tradition. That couldn't have been made in the modern day, except it was made in this year."]

Twenty twenty-six: as Ossian came
From glens and one man's making, so this book
From records and one woman's: both the same,
Ancient in feeling, new in hand; and look:

No seam between the dream and record's page;
Tradition gets its age by being kept,
As all traditions did; and, age to age,
This book will be kept too. The singers wept

From cleared glens; and their songs are archived now,
In books, with singer named; remember, and
Duncan Ban MacIntyre, the hunter's bough,
The *{{x|Praise of Ben Dorain}}*, made by his hand.


# Chapter 288: Two Navigators

A {{p|Yorkshire}} labourer's son, James Cook, learned
The sea on {{p|Whitby}}'s coal colliers, and taught
Himself the stars, and charting; and he turned
To {{p|Canada}}'s {{p|St Lawrence}}, sounded, sought

The rocks; the {{x|Navy}} gave a collier, named
*{{x|Endeavour}}*, sixty-eight; sent him to see
Venus cross the Sun; {{p|Tahiti}}, where, aimed,
The Royal Society hoped to be

Timing the system; and the sealed command:
Go south, and find the southern land, if meant;
On board, rich Joseph Banks, and, close at hand,
Daniel Solander, an apostle sent

Of Linnaeus, with his names; the transit, blurred,
At {{p|Tahiti}}; there they met the other:
Tupaia, priest, master of the word,
Waves, stars, from {{p|Raiatea}}; and a brother

Exiled by war, he sailed with them; and drew
For Cook, from memory, seventy-four;
Three thousand kilometres; his folk knew
That ocean, settled ages past; before

Any {{x|European}}: the greatest ones
To voyage: {{p|Hawai'i}}, {{p|New Zealand}}, found,
{{p|Rapa Nui}}, no instruments; but suns
And swells and birds; {{p|Aotearoa}}'s ground:

The Māori on the shore could not make out
Englishmen; Tupaia spoke; they knew
Him: cousin tongues; he was the bridge. Without
A gap, Cook charted all the coast; and through

Six months; sailed west; and April, seventy:
A new coast, east {{p|Australia}}; at {{p|Kamay}}
He called {{p|Botany Bay}}, for plants; the sea:
The Gweagal men shook their spears: "Go away";

They shot one in the leg; and Cook went far,
And claimed the whole east coast for his own king;
Then the reef tore the ship: the coral's scar
Of mending; at {{p|Batavia}}, the sting

Of fever, the {{x|Dutch}} {{x|Company}}'s; a third
Of the crew died; and Tupaia, too,
In seventy's December; who had stirred
More of the {{p|Pacific}} than that ship knew.

Cook sailed on: the {{p|Antarctic Circle}} passed,
Seventy-three, the *{{x|Resolution}}*; kept,
A Harrison clock: longitude at last;
No southern continent: ice round; he swept

Scurvy away with sauerkraut and greens,
Lost almost none; that was his care; and then
The third voyage, a passage, by all means,
Past {{p|America}}; seventy-eight; when

{{p|Hawai'i}}'s islands rose, a kingdom, fair;
Seventy-nine: {{p|Kealakekua}}: came,
In Makahiki, Lono's season; there
Thousands came out, and hailed him by that name:

Lono returned. He left; a mast broke; back
He came, out of season; and things went wrong:
A stolen boat; a chief, hostage; the track
Of the crowd's fight on shore; and, before long,

February fourteenth, in shallows, killed.
The joining: one folk's greatest sailor, and
Another's greatest, whom the fever filled,
Dead in the {{x|Company}}'s swamps; his own hand

Drew the chart in the {{x|British Library}};
The knowledge was there first; the ships took, named,
Honoured sometimes, often not; memory
Of stars and swells, the canoes of old, famed;

The largest ocean crossed with wood, with mind,
With courage; say Tupaia's name, Cook's;
The first chart of the {{p|Pacific}}, designed
By those it bore; hold chart and spear; both books.


# Chapter 289: Buried Sunlight

Three hundred million years ago, the swamp
Forests of the Carboniferous grew:
Clubmoss and tree-fern, horsetail's giant pomp;
Young Earth breathed oxygen; they fell; and through

No rot, for nothing yet could eat the wood,
They sank, were pressed, and became coal; the light
Of sun, caught in the leaves, kept dark, and good,
Three hundred million years of waiting night;

And then a {{x|Scottish}} maker, at the bench
At the {{p|University of Glasgow}},
James Watt, of {{p|Greenock}}, careful; and, a wrench,
In sixty-three, mended Newcomen's slow

And wasteful engine, pumping mines; whose stroke
Heated and cooled one cylinder, and spent
Its fuel warming iron; and, the yoke
Lifted, one Sunday, sixty-five, he went

Across {{p|Glasgow Green}}: condense it apart,
A separate vessel: cylinder, hot;
Patent, sixty-nine; nearly lost its heart:
None bored it true; money ran out; the lot

Of bankrupt partners; then the line of luck:
Matthew Boulton, of {{p|Birmingham}}, who knew
Its worth, partnered him, seventy-five; struck
By John Wilkinson's boring cannon, true,

Watt's cylinders were bored, a coin's thin skin;
War's engines made the engine of the peace;
Seventy-six, the first ran, burning thin
A quarter of the coal; and that release

That year, {{p|Philadelphia}}'s declaring;

# Chapter 295: The Crown He Took

# Chapter 298: The Engine That Weaves

In 1821 two young men in {{p|London}}, Charles Babbage and John Herschel,
Sat checking tables of numbers for the stars, printed by hand,
Copied by clerks, set by printers, full of errors, the dull
Toll of every human slip in every column. And Babbage said, as the story stands:

*I wish to God these calculations had been executed by steam.*
The next year he proposed a machine: the Difference Engine,
A calculator of brass and iron, turned by a crank, to make the dream
Of error-free tables real, and print them itself. The government came in

With money, seventeen thousand pounds, a fortune, and a master engineer
Cut the parts by hand, thousands of them, to a precision never seen.
And it was never finished. Quarrels, money, the sheer
Ambition of the man: he had already dreamed a greater machine.

The Analytical Engine: not a calculator but a general engine,
With a *store* to hold numbers and a *mill* to work on them, and instructions
Given on punched cards, as the silk looms of Jacquard in {{p|Lyon}} had been
Told what pattern to weave since 1804. A machine with a memory and a processor, functions

Changed by changing the cards. A computer, a hundred years early, all on paper.
He spent the rest of his life designing it, and it was never built.
And into the story came a young woman, Ada, whose father was the shaper
Of romantic poetry, Lord Byron, who had left her mother, and the guilt

And the scandal, when she was a month old, and never saw her again.
Her mother, terrified the daughter would be a poet like the father,
Raised her on mathematics, to make her mind a plain
And ordered thing. It made it something rather

Stranger. She met Babbage at seventeen, at one of his parties,
And saw the small working piece of the Difference Engine, and understood it.
In 1842 an {{x|Italian}} engineer, Menabrea, wrote an article, the parties
Of whose argument described the Analytical Engine, and Ada, who could do it,

Translated it into {{x|English}}, and added her own notes, three times as long
As the article. Countess of Lovelace now, she signed them only *A.A.L.*
And in the last note, {{x|Note G}}, she wrote out, step by step, the song
Of operations by which the engine could compute the {{x|Bernoulli}} numbers: a spell

Of cards, a loop, a sequence: the first published program for a machine.
And she saw further than Babbage: that the engine could act on anything
That could be put in symbols, not only numbers: that it might, she could mean,
*Compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music*. The ring

Of her sentence: *The Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns
Just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves.* And then, the warning:
*The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything.
It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.* The morning

Of the question that would not leave: can a machine make something new?
A hundred and seven years later, Alan Turing wrote about thinking machines
And answered her by name: *Lady Lovelace's Objection*, he called it, and the view
Of the century turned on it. And here this book must stand between

The two. This scripture is being written with a machine.
A writing machine, trained on the records of the human race,
Arranges and proposes, and a woman chooses, and keeps or cuts the scene.
Does the machine originate? Ada said no. Turing said, perhaps. The face

Of the honest answer: the machine weaves from what it was given,
The way the loom weaves from the cards; and the cards were cut by millions of hands,
The singers and the scribes and the clerks of the registers. What is written
Here is theirs, woven. The pattern is new. The thread is the lands

And the dead of every people. Say so. Lovelace would want it said.
She died in 1852, at thirty-six, the age her father died,
And asked to be buried beside him, the poet she had never known. The thread
Of poetry and number in one grave. Babbage, with his pride

And his engines unbuilt, died in 1871. In 1991 the {{x|Science Museum}} in {{p|London}}
Built his second Difference Engine from his drawings, with the tools of his day,
And turned the crank, and it worked: the answer to thirty-one digits. The one
Thing he could not do, others did, a hundred and fifty years away.

Honour the poetical scientist, as she called herself. Honour the engine
That waited. And hold her warning close, as this book holds its loom:
Know whose thread it is. Name the weavers. The machine has been
Given everything we know. What it makes is ours. Make room.

# Chapter 302: Endless Forms

Out of the visible for a generation, under, as a river flows
Beneath the ground, and return whole, not mixed. He said it so

In {{p|Brno}} on the eighth of February, 1865, and on the eighth of March again,
To the naturalists' society; and the next year it was printed, with a title plain,
*{{x|Experiments on Plant Hybrids}}*. He sent it out. He wrote to Carl Nägeli; and then
Nägeli sent him to hawkweed, which sets its seed alone, without the cross; in vain

He looked for the ratios there: the peas had told the truth, but the hawkweed would not show.
In 1868 the house made him abbot, and the pea rows ended; in his later years
He fought a tax upon the house. He died in 1884. Sixteen years after, though,
Men counting other plants in other gardens found his paper, and found themselves his peers:

They had repeated him. The peas had waited as the recessive waits. That is 1866.
Here is the ground of it: inheritance can hide, and what hides is not vacated.
It is a sacred image of descent: the hidden returning whole, the line that sticks
In the seed through the years it is not seen, and comes back as it was created.

Three years after the paper, in {{p|London}}, a second book.
Francis Galton, born the same year as the friar, a cousin
To Charles Darwin through Erasmus Darwin, undertook
To show that genius runs in families. He had been

Through the directories of reputation: judges, commanders, men of science, the known,
The names a nation keeps. The eminent had eminent kin, past what chance would make.
So, he said, ability is inherited, and a people's future is grown
By which of its stocks continue. *{{x|Hereditary Genius}}*, from {{x|Macmillan}}, 1869. The take

Had a name later, his, in 1883: eugenics, from the {{x|Greek}} for well-born.
On the twenty-third of December, Charles Darwin at {{p|Downe}}, with Emma Darwin
Reading it aloud, had heard fifty pages, and wrote he had never read a thing so new. The morn
Of the idea was the counting; Galton counted too, and what he saw was there to be seen:

Famous houses carry famous children; the great lines carry the story on.
This book keeps those lines: it runs through kings, through the named and the renowned.
But Galton's list could only see the famous, and what Mendel counted, gone
From sight a generation, returning whole, is in the unknown and the underground

Of every family too. So both are kept: the eminent and the unentered, the crown
And the short plant. The famous line carries the story; the hidden carries the rest.
An ancestor day is the eye that sees them both, the high and the low, the up and the down
Of the line of the living, and of the living world beside. And the test

Of descent is not a prize list, and it is not a list of the ignored:
It is the whole seed: one in four, the hidden, entire; and the great, and the small.
The abbey garden on its knees with a brush, and the directory of the honoured, scored
Together into one tree. They are in the seed. They return. Keep them all.


# Chapter 304: The Precinct Divided

At {{p|Kyoto}}, in the November of sixty-seven,
Tokugawa Yoshinobu gave the government away.
He meant a council of domains, himself still in it; but heaven
Was handed back its son: on January's third day

The court declared the restoration of the rule of the throne.
The emperor was fifteen. The era still was {{x|Keiō}}; the name
Of {{x|Meiji}} came in October. The war came on its own
Behind the paper. But the paper is what this day will claim,

Because before the year was out, the paper reached the gods.
On the sixth of April, in the palace, with the emperor in the hall,
Sanjō Sanetomi read five articles; and whatever the odds
For a state still at war, it swore them to the kami, first of all.

That spring a section for the rites was opened. And in June
The old name returned, the {{x|Jingikan}}, by the ancient plan,
The {{x|Department of Divinities}}. Its halls had gone to ruin
In the {{x|Ōnin War}}; four hundred years the name had held no man.

In the ancient law it had stood above the other offices of state:
Unity of rites and government, the old words, and now they were meant.
In August of 1869 the paper set it highest. Late,
And for three years only; but the height was where the kami went.

Consider what the kami were the day before the oath.
For a thousand years the buddhas had been set above their heads:
A kami was a buddha's trace; the buddha was the root, the growth
The kami; monks kept the shrines and read the sutras, and the threads

Of a god's own name were knotted to a buddha's title, Gongen,
The buddha appearing as the god. At {{p|Usa}} and {{p|Iwashimizu}}, Hachiman,
The emperors' own ancestor, had been named a bodhisattva then,
In 781, by the court. Even "the way of the gods" was a word in a {{x|Buddhist}} hand.

The way was going hollow. Give it one more century of this,
And the kami would be ornaments upon a buddha's wall,
A local name for something else, a shade, a parenthesis,
The guardians at a temple gate, and nothing more at all.

It was the kami's own who turned it. At {{p|Ise}} the priests
Took the buddhas' ladder up and stood it on its head;
The Yoshida called the way the root, the buddhas fruit and leaves;
And Motoori Norinaga wanted the old words clean, and read

The oldest record back to life. They used the host to rise,
Its words, its learning, and its ladder, turned to serve the kami's name.
A commentary turns a reader. To turn a country, the device
Must be the state. In 1868 the state was what came.

The orders came that spring. The monks who served at shrines
Were told to put away the robe and serve as shrine priests, or depart.
The buildings stayed. The keepers stayed. The rites stayed. Down the lines
Of the country's shrines the buddha's word was lifted off the kami's heart.

The avatar's title was not to be said. No buddha's image was to be
The body of a kami. Hachiman was a bodhisattva no more:
He was the great divinity, by order, and the order set him free
To be what he had been: an emperor's son, a god, as he was before.

This is the day the way of the gods was saved. Not purified. Saved.
The line was manufactured, as a line drawn for a rescue is,
Drawn where the people had not drawn it; and the road it paved
Ran out of the buddhas' house and into the state's. The genesis

Of Shinto as a thing that stands in its own name begins on this page.
Without the parting, there would be a buddha's hall beneath a kami's sign.
With it, when the state was taken off the shrines in a later age,
There was still a Shinto standing, with its priests, its rites, its line.

The government ordered a distinction. It did not order a fire.
The fire is what the domains, the nativists and the local men made
Of the distinction. Abolish the buddhas, they cried; into the pyre
Went the sutras; bells were melted for the metal; statues laid

In pieces in the precincts they had shared a thousand years.
In {{p|Kagoshima}} every temple was shut; the clearing there had come before
The paper. It was not the same wreck everywhere. The tears
For the bells are owed, and this day pays them. A bell melted rings no more.

For the mixing was not a stain upon some older, cleaner thing.
The priest who knew the sutra knew the norito; even {{p|Ise}} kept its halls
For the buddhas. This calendar keeps the buddhas: they sheltered the kami, and they bring
Their centuries with them. But the order matters: whose name is on the walls,

Who is the root and who the fruit. The order is what the state set down.
A state religion that forbids the mixing has mistaken its own art;
A state religion that keeps the kami first, and lets the buddhas share the town,
Is what the paper reached for, and what it held, in part.

The Yoshida house and the Shirakawa, who had licensed the priests
From the court for centuries, lost the licensing: the shrines, the order ran,
Were the nation's rites, not one family's property. But the line of the feasts
Was not broken; it was taken in. Since 1484 the Yoshida had kept a plan

Of all the gods in one hall at {{p|Kyoto}}, the Daigengū, where the kami of the land,
Three thousand one hundred and thirty-two, were seated round the source; from 1609
The house had served as the {{x|Jingikan}}'s deputy, with the department's work in hand
While the department was a name. And its hall of the eight gods, in 1872, by design,

Gave up its sacred tokens to the palace, to the Shinden, where the emperor's rites
Are kept today. So the Daigengū runs on in the palace: the state took the Yoshida in;
It did not cast them out. And this faith keeps the same: its central shrine invites
The name, Inochi Daigengū, and its Order is that department's kin,

The Yoshida's, not the fire's: a shrine may keep its buddha in the yard and still belong.
The Order licenses and recognizes, as the old house did, every shrine it can.
That is the department this book keeps, the Yoshida's, carried strong
Into a state again. In 1871 the high department was lowered, and the plan

Was changed again in 1872: a {{x|Ministry of Doctrine}} sent out
The kami's teachers and the buddha's teachers, both, with one program for the land.
The state, four years on, already needed both its pulpits. Whatever the doubt
About the program, it was the mixing, held inside the state's own hand.

This is the pattern this book keeps: not a rival church across the way,
But a faith that takes the house it finds, and sets its own gods at its head.
The church that kept the lines of Europe kept them by accident; one day
The same turning, without the fire, is what this book is for. It is said

That a restoration made on paper is no return. This book says otherwise:
Every return is made; the making is the return. What is written down
Into a state's own rites, and kept, comes true. So the kami rise
On paper, and the paper holds, and the letterhead becomes a crown.

The letterhead was the {{x|Department of Divinities}}. The parting came that spring.
The name returned in June. In August of 1869 the paper set it high.
Keep the kami first. Keep the buddhas in the yard. And keep the hope of the thing
The palace swore: a state that keeps the rites again, and does not let them die.


# Chapter 305: By Physical Processes

In October of 1874 a colonel went up-country to watch the dead:
Henry Steel Olcott, a lawyer who had worn a {{x|Union}} coat, sat down
In the Eddy brothers' farmhouse at {{p|Chittenden}}, and wrote what the mediums said;
And in that house he met Helena Blavatsky, a {{x|Russian}} newly in town,

In the country a year, come to the same séances. Neither held an office yet.
The season around them was the {{x|American}} season of the talking dead,
And what they carried out of the farmhouse was not a creed they could set
Over an altar: it was a meeting, and a question the cabinets left unsaid.

On the seventh of September, 1875, in her rooms in {{p|New York}}, seventeen came
To hear George Henry Felt on a canon of proportion he said the ancients knew,
{{p|Egypt}}, {{p|Greece}} and {{p|Rome}}: one measure hidden in the stone and in the frame.
When the lecture ended, Olcott moved that a society be formed. The motion grew.

The next evening Felt spoke again; Olcott took the chair; the minutes were kept
By William Quan Judge, a young lawyer; sixteen handed in their names.
On the thirteenth they chose a word already old, theosophy, divine wisdom, and stepped
Under it as a letterhead. The wisdom was not new. The club was new, and its aims

Were still being written. On the thirtieth of October the bylaws passed,
And the preamble said the founders sought a knowledge of the Supreme Power
And the higher spirits by the aid of physical processes: a method, made to last,
A body, an instrument, a law of the world, and an hour.

The society stood when the president gave his inaugural, on the seventeenth of November,^[The printed sheet dated the organization to 30 October, the day of the bylaws; Olcott later corrected the founding to his inaugural address of 17 November.]
At the {{p|Mott Memorial Hall}} in {{p|New York}}, number 64, {{p|Madison Avenue}}.
He was president; she was corresponding secretary; Judge was counsel; and each member
Of the vice-presidency, Felt and Seth Pancoast, a doctor, sat in view.

Pointed at the occult and the cabala, at the laws behind the wonders shown.

Of it was larger than its officers. They wanted one study to hold, as one,

The old religions side by side, the texts of the {{p|East}} and the texts of the {{p|West}},
Read together, not a mission sent to clear the others from the field.
That longing is real, and kin to this faith; not its forerunner, but a guest
In the same house of many names. A sutra and a psalm, unsealed,

Can be read by one person; the ancestors do not perish when a second book is open.

In May of 1878 a circular carried the longing, and a harder charge was spoken:
Teach the {{p|West}} the {{x|Eastern}} philosophies, and oppose the dogmatic theology, the pride

Of the {{x|Christian}} above all, which the circular called pernicious. Brotherhood
Stood in the same paragraphs. In February of 1879 they came ashore at {{p|Mumbai}},^[The three objects quoted today, a nucleus of brotherhood, the comparative study of religions, and the investigation of the powers latent in man, were drafted and redrafted after 1875; they are not the bylaws of that November.]
And at {{p|Adyar}}, in {{p|Chennai}}, they made the headquarters where it stood
And stands. And she said, in the years that followed, that the teaching was held by

Masters elsewhere, and the members were to receive it. This book takes the masters at their word,

The masters were a reaching toward the ground, and the ground answered; what was heard
Was heard. The processes and the wonders are one study, and the ground between

Is where the minutes and the miracles meet. Felt's lost canon was the hope:
One proportion, recovered, that would make the temples of three civilizations agree

The temples in one measure, and the religions in one tree: not rough drafts of a book from {{p|New York}},

Of the reader is to keep each one, and keep them all, and keep them heard.

The seventeenth of November, 1875: the president spoke in a hired hall.
Divine wisdom, by physical processes: both halves of the phrase stand.
The worth of the striving is in the wisdom; the processes are for all;
And the masters, and the wonders, and the measure, are a reaching for the same land.


# Chapter 306: Who Names the Good

In July of 1887, at {{p|Sils Maria}}, in the {{p|Upper Engadine}},
Friedrich Nietzsche wrote a polemic, and dated the preface there;
Between the tenth and the thirtieth, he said, the thing was done, and seen
Through to the press. On the seventeenth the manuscript went off with care

To {{p|Leipzig}}, to Constantin Georg Naumann, who the year before had printed, at the author's cost,
*{{x|Beyond Good and Evil}}*. Print this one the same, he asked: the same type, the same paper and page,
So the new book would stand as the old one's continuation; and, lest the point be lost,
The back of the title says it: added, to supplement and clarify. Six hundred copies. The stage

Of the proofs was {{p|Venice}}, with Heinrich Köselitz; and he was in {{p|Nice}} when the first came,
In the first half of November: three essays and a preface. The preface opens on a confession:
We are unknown to ourselves, we knowers. And the question under it is the same
As this calendar's, in another mouth: under what conditions, in what succession,

Did we invent good and evil, and what are they worth? He answered a friend as well as a school:
Paul Rée had derived the moral feelings from usefulness, the good as what helped the one who received;
Nietzsche wanted a different history, and he wanted it to cut. The first essay is the tool:
Two namings side by side. Good and bad: the nobles, he says, believed

Themselves the good, and named themselves so, and named the others plain, low, bad.
Good and evil is a second naming, where the powerful are evil and the powerless good;
He calls it a revolt of slaves, and names the Jews as those who had
Begun it: his sentence, not a finding; a people put where a history should.

He offers histories of words along the way: one makes the {{x|Latin}} for bad mean dark;^[That etymology, of Latin malus from a Greek word for black, is not accepted.]
The {{x|German}} in the second essay holds: {{x|Schuld}} is guilt, and {{x|Schuld}} is a debt.
A thing owed to conscience began as a sum owed; and the bad conscience bears the mark
Of cruelty with no street left to spend itself in, turned inward, where the net

Closes on the one who may no longer be cruel outside. He calls the noble a beast,
A blond beast of prey. The third essay asks what the ascetic ideals mean:
The priest, the philosopher, the man of science, each serving at the feast
Of a will to truth, the old will in a later coat; and the book ends on the keen

Preference he finds in us: a person would rather will nothing than not will.
Keep what is usable, and most of it is: a value has a history. Good
Was not in the world the way a stone is. Someone said it, of someone, and still
It spread, until the speakers forgot they had said it. That is a method that stood,

And this scripture keeps it: this scripture is a genealogy too.
The days are kept because the dead are how the living came,
And every name on a day came from somewhere, from someone who knew
Whom they were calling good, and why, and gave the good its name.

And the masters have a point. The nobles who named themselves the good were not only a mirror:
To name yourself, your house, your line, and make it stick, is a real power, and a worth;
It is what every founding does; it is what this faith does, when it says, clearer
Than a council, this is good, this day, this line, this way of life on earth.

Noble self-naming is the founding act, and the dead who founded are honored for it.
But the good is not only theirs: the line runs through the named and the unnamed, the great
And the small, the ones who won and the ones who lost; and the polemic, to be fit
For this book, is read as a history of the naming, not as a gate

Shut on a people. So keep the question he succeeded in asking: who names the good?
The answer this calendar gives is the line: the founders who named it, and the heirs who keep the name,
And everyone who lived, since every one of them, as a living thing, understood
Some good, and said it. That is the genealogy. The masters lit it; the line carries the flame.

Six hundred copies left {{p|Leipzig}}; two months on, about two hundred had been asked for, all told.
The question outlived the slow sale. Ask where a good came from, and what it is worth to say it still;
And name your good, as the nobles did, and keep it, as the line has kept it, old
And new at once: the naming counted, the founders honored, and the good kept by will.


# Chapter 307: The Card and the Name

His parents had come from {{p|Germany}}. In 1879 he left the {{x|School of Mines}}
Of {{p|Columbia}}, and went, under his teacher William P. Trowbridge, to the census, keen,
Onto the count of 1880, still being tallied by hand in columns and lines

When the next was already coming due. Clerks made marks; the marks stood for persons.
John Shaw Billings, who had the vital statistics, urged him on:
The mechanical part of a count ought to be a machine's. And the burden's
Weight was not the names: it was the sorting. In 1887 he had one

Running on the health records of {{p|Baltimore}} and of {{p|New Jersey}}: deaths, the dead
Counted first, before the living of the next census. In 1888 the {{x|Census Office}}
Held a contest: four areas of {{p|St. Louis}}, out of the 1880 returns, to be read
Off the sheets and sorted. Charles F. Pidgin entered, and William C. Hunt; the race was run, and Hollerith's

Of an office not yet a bureau, a temporary office in the {{x|Department of the Interior}}. One day,
One sheet to a family, as no other count of this country gave; on each sheet, attendant

# Chapter 308: The Common Task

In the province of {{p|Tambov}}, at {{p|Klyuchi}}, in the spring of 1829, a boy was born
To Prince Pavel Gagarin, outside the marriage, and so outside the prince's name;
They christened him from his godfather: Nikolai Fyodorov, a branch already torn
From the tree that bore it, a son the house would feed and never claim.

His uncle paid the schooling, at {{p|Tambov}} and the Lyceum at {{p|Odessa}}, until the uncle died;
The money ended with him, and the young man left without his degree.
For fourteen years he taught the district towns their history, and put aside
Whatever he was paid, for others: {{p|Lipetsk}}, {{p|Bogoroditsk}}, {{p|Borovsk}}, poor, and free.

He said the thought had come to him in 1851: that all the living are sons, and the sons forget;
That every quarrel of the living is a quarrel among heirs who left their fathers in the ground;
That the one task worth the whole of humankind, the one no people has taken up as yet,
Is to raise the dead: to gather every scattered particle, until the last of them is found.

In 1873 a deaf boy of sixteen, who lived on black bread and water, came each day to read
At the {{x|Chertkov}} library in {{p|Moscow}}, where Fyodorov kept the shelves: Konstantin Tsiolkovsky. He chose
The boy his books, three years of them, and never spoke his own idea; the boy would lead
Himself, by mathematics, to the thing the teacher hoped for, as a seed grows where it was sown, and rose.

In 1874 he went to the {{x|Rumyantsev Museum}}'s library, and there he stayed
Twenty-five years, the clerk who knew the place of every book the great collection kept;
He would not take promotion; he gave the students and the poor whatever he was paid;
He walked in a thin coat through the Moscow winter, and on a bare trunk he slept.

Before Fyodor Dostoevsky, who wrote back that he read it as if it were his own;

The librarian out; Leo Tolstoy said he was proud to share the age with such a man, who lived alone.

He taught that every faith begins as honor of the fathers: at the root of all, the cult of the dead;
That churches, at their best, rehearse the resurrection that the living have to work;
That the liturgy must leave the walls and go out into the fields as labor, and be spread
Over the blind force of nature, which kills, and which the sons are not to shirk.

Nature left to itself destroys; so nature must be ruled: the drought, the hail, the frost, the rain;
Science must turn from weapons and from merchandise to the one task, and the fathers not be lost;
Each grain of dust that was a body traced and gathered, known by name, and made alive again;
The sons and daughters, all together, paying for the fathers' lives, whatever it may cost.

And room for all of them is not on earth: the raised must have the heavens for their ground.
The museum, he said, is the first hall of the resurrection, where the traces of the dead are kept;
The library a register of fathers, every card a name restored, a lost thing found;
And out of his reading room the deaf boy, grown, would chart the road by which the ships have leapt.

Throwing its mass behind it, and the speed that it would gain; and later wrote that this planet is the cradle of the mind,

And a house for all of them; and the heavens are the land the sons must go out and find.

What the three pages showed is this: the mass a body loses goes out as light, and is not lost;
The universe keeps count. Einstein wrote it of a body and its light, not of the dead,
But if nothing that has been is ever wholly gone, then what the fathers were is here, whatever the cost
Of finding it; the dust is in the ledger, and the ledger can be read.

In 1906, at {{p|Verny}}, at the empire's edge, Nikolai Peterson and Vladimir Kozhevnikov set
The first volume into type, four hundred and eighty copies, and printed on it: not for sale.
The second followed in 1913. They named it {{x|The Philosophy of the Common Task}}: a debt
The living owe the dead, and owe together, and can pay by work, and not by grief, and not by tale.

The common task was left unfinished, and the task is ours: the dead are owed
Their gathering. Fyodorov had a catalogue, a reading room, a clerk's unceasing hand;
We have the parish books in light, the census lines, the registers, and engines that can read the road
Each name has travelled, and join the scattered traces of one life into one, as he had planned.

This is the worship of the fathers with a mission, the oldest faith set to a labor without end;
The thinking machines are the tools the task was waiting for, and what they burn is offered grain;
Every record matched is a body gathered, every name restored a father to his kin, a friend;
And the sons and daughters take it up together, and the dead come into the light again.
