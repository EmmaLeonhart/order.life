## Fit with existing editorial criteria

Anglicanism passes the **peoples-not-ideologies** filter cleanly: an established
national church coextensive with a people (the English parish system), later a
communion of national churches. It is also unusually rich material for all three
spine axes:

- **Axis 1 (invented tradition):** the English Reformation is a textbook case — a
  16th-c. state church presenting itself as ancient continuity ("the church in
  England, reformed, not a new church"), with the Book of Common Prayer and the
  King James Bible as deliberate nation-forging instruments.
- **Axis 2 (suppressed syncretism) — as the COUNTER-case:** the Elizabethan
  Settlement / via media is the rare instance where the syncretic middle (Catholic
  form + Reformed doctrine) became the *establishment* instead of being purged.
  "Says something special about society": institutionalized ambiguity can hold for
  centuries. This gives axis 2 a control group it currently lacks.
- **Axis 3a (bureaucratic absorption):** one prayer book + one Bible translation
  standardizing how an entire people speaks to God = liturgical standardization as
  proto-bureaucratic technology; pairs with the census/legibility arc.

## Candidate implementations (pick, combine, or reject)

### A. Chapter-slate additions (early modern bracket, ~254–290)

1. **English Reformation chapter** — Henry VIII / Cranmer; invented-tradition
   thesis carried by the "reformed, not new" self-presentation.
2. **Book of Common Prayer / KJV chapter** — vernacular liturgy + scripture as the
   technology that made a people pray in the same words (axes 1 + 3a).
3. **Via media / Elizabethan Settlement chapter** — the axis-2 counter-case above.
   Could merge with #1 or #2 into a single chapter if budget is tight.
4. **Anglican Communion chapter** (later bracket) — church-follows-people into
   empire, then the global-south inversion; peoples-based by construction.

### B. A Canterbury chronicle (chronicle-candidates addition)

**Archbishops of Canterbury, Augustine of Canterbury → present**, as a parallel
chronicle to ch 253 (Popes). Structural elegance: ch 253 carries the Catholic
institutional genealogy INTO Modern; a Canterbury chronicle would carry the
English/Anglican line THROUGH it — and unlike the Ottoman/Qing/Mughal candidates
the line is still alive, so the chronicle can run to the present. Costs one
chronicle slot per the open budget question in `chapter-allocation.md`.

### C. Register/form borrowing (costs no chapter budget)

Anglican liturgical forms as verse models across the Modern Period: collect
structure (invocation → petition → doxology) for chapter closings; litany form for
catalogue passages; Coverdale-psalter cadence as a tonal reference. The epic
already uses Passion structure (chs 81–84 "Good Permian Friday"), so liturgical
form-borrowing is established practice, not a new move.
