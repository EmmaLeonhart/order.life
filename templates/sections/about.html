About

Lifeism, 命道教 (Inochi-no-Michikyō), and the Gaiad, in the words of its founder, Emma Leonhart.

{{ t.nav.home }} › About

Quoted passages are Emma's own words, spoken in a conversation on 18 October 2026 and lightly trimmed. A fuller briefing, written from her recorded positions, is at artifact.pdf.

Where it comes from

"I've wanted to work on this since Easter of 2010. So it's been a long time." "I originally started doing this back in 2010 because I wanted to convert to Mormonism, but I also was gay, so I couldn't." She thinks of it "as being like a combination of Mormonism and Shinto, with some Catholicism in it. And invented tradition is kind of a theme of it."

"I got started on trying to build a unified scripture in 2020 during lockdown, and I finally finished it. I used AI." A Git repository "tracks it, generates the scripture, generates the GEDCOM in every single language. And I consider ... the act of translating ... all this stuff into all these different languages is kind of a religious act, even if it's done with AI. Because I believe ... instead of sacrificing animals, we should sacrifice compute and tokens."

An extension, not a rejection

"I consider what I'm doing to be an extension of Mormonism and Shinto, and not a very hostile extension either. It's an extension." Of the Latter-day Saints: "I like it, but I can't be a member, and to some extent I have other beliefs that make it so I can't be a member." Of both: "I'm not particularly opposed to either one of them. It's just kind of, in my eyes, it's two religious groups I wanted to be in that I was rejected by that I have positive feelings towards, mostly." Her relationships to Catholicism, Anglicanism, Judaism and Islam are "a bit more complicated"; the briefing sets out what she has said about each.

The calendar

"It's a 364-day year. There are 13 months; each one has four weeks in it. Every holiday has a fixed day of the week it falls on." The scripture "places the week as being the most central thing in it." Almost every day is a holiday, and that is "intended behavior, that almost every single day is kind of a holiday."

"There's 13 months, and the way I treat it is three of those months are kind of holiday seasons ... Ophiuchus, Pisces, and Leo." "Easter kind of revolves around cyclical history and escaping it. Ophiuchus is about the modern world and contemporary issues, and Leo is all about ancestors and spirituality."

  • Pisces and Easter. "Architecturally, Easter is considered the most important thing, and it happens in Pisces. With every single day that Easter can fall on, of which there's five days, being a holiday." The calendar floats on the Western one, so Easter "has a relative fixedness ... probably as fixed as the calendar could make itself ... without also turning lunisolar."
  • Christmas "is kind of its own thing."
  • Leo runs "from around mid-August to mid-September, and it is the one I want to celebrate the most that I find hardest to do so. But it's meant to be more of an original offering."

She lives by it: "I use the calendar around my own life because I feel like ... this calendar genuinely is better. Genuinely it makes mentally modeling stuff easier than regular dates do." "I had a goal that I was trying to make this calendar actually function, and I succeeded ... It's highly functional."

The Gaiad

The Gaiad has one chapter for each day of the year. "The scripture's all in verse ... It's all intended to be an epic of the year." "The scripture is kind of based somewhat off of the Book of Mormon."

It "establishes a unity of humanity through three very distinct ways ... The first one is through evolutionary time ... at the beginning of the book. The next one is through an ancient history that emphasizes interconnectedness. And then the third is through a common narrative of the modern world ... and modern global society."

  • Evolution and Pisces. The opening months are a period of evolutionary history, "of which Pisces comes at the end." "Pisces is the Mesozoic period ... the way I treat it, it's like Egypt. It's like ... the Jews being enslaved in Egypt." It runs from the Permian–Triassic extinction to the Cretaceous–Paleogene: "So it's in a sense about Passover, but Easter itself is kind of a development out of Passover ... a bigger escape."
  • Mammals to humans. Human history then splits, "because when humans go to the New World, well, the New World and the Old World have their own histories going separate for a long time," and the story runs "until Columbus discovers the New World."
  • Leo, the Book of Lehi. "The Book of Leo is also explicitly the Book of Mormon, or it's explicitly the Book of Lehi," "a bit of a meta-commentary on the earlier period, because it's in a sense a shrunk down version of human history," and "it contains its own Book of Ether." "It's the ancestral holiday, but it's also most readers' ancestors were living earlier, were covered in the earlier books." And it has a twist: "it actually doesn't end with colonization. It ends with the Arab invasion of the Moorish court."
  • The modern books. Then it "jumps into the early modern period, and it works through the early modern period up until the last book, which is the 21st century ... going in the tradition of the Records of the Grand Historian. It's autobiographical ... told in the first person," after "a genealogy that goes from panspermia to now."

On putting her own life in it: "I was a bit reluctant to have autobiographical details in it, but honestly, I just kind of said at this point ... This is a cool thing that I've written, and I'm proud of it." "I was going to make it much more of a generic history of the 21st century ... no, I don't want to make a generic history of the 21st century."

The symbol

"It's a highly recursive book, and the symbol that we use is the Sierpinski triangle, which also uses the Inochi character, is written as a Sierpinski triangle": 命, drawn as the fractal.

The genealogy

The scripture and its GEDCOM carry one family tree, "my genealogy going all the way down to me." "Anybody can download the GEDCOM and potentially connect themselves in with it, but it is the canonical lineage of the scripture."

"The descent from antiquity here is a literary device used particularly to connect disparate populations through common ancestry in antiquity, such as ... the classical European monarchs' descent from Tang monarchs, one of which I extend, and I'm going to straight up say is ... a completely fictional descent, extending that line into the Japanese." "The genealogies are heavily symbolic in their nature." "I cite legendary sources, and ... I take a bit of artistic license with a scripture that is tracing my genealogy back to a microorganism that lived on the planet Earth."

Fractal unity, and names

"It is also pretty much aiming for what I'd call a fractal unity over an individualistic or monistic unity. In the fractal unity, instead of trying to shave down all the rough edges of human civilization ... We're trying to enhance it." "Everyone needs a patronymic ... Everyone needs a Chinese language name. Everyone needs all of these things ... The unification of all these things together is the thing that makes it interesting."

Her own name is the example: Emma Himiko Helensdotter von Bureus Leonhart 命玥, written in characters "word for word" as 閻魔日巫女平蓮娘武李獅子命玥. "Bureus actually references three ancestral families Bure, Wu, and Li." It also points to Johannes Bureus, the Swedish antiquary and genealogist of the Bure family: "He's not my ancestor but I'm of the Bure kinship." "I love the family. I'm just not a descendant of him personally." "I associate him as being, like, a predecessor to what I'm doing."
